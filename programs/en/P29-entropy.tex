\program[Entropy and surprise]{Entropy and the measure of surprise}
\label{prog:P29}
\index{entropy}
\index{surprise}

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{1--4}{write down how surprising an outcome is, and say why the
  measure has to be a logarithm}
\outcome{5--10}{compute an entropy, and say which distribution over a given
  number of outcomes has the largest}
\outcome{11--18}{say what a bit is, in terms of a code rather than a unit}
\outcome{19--25}{read an entropy as an effective number of equally likely
  choices, and a perplexity as the same thing}
\outcome{26--30}{use the entropy of a distribution a model reports as a
  runtime signal, and say what it does not tell you}
\outcome{31--34}{say what a bits-per-token figure is missing before it can
  support a claim about compression}
\end{outcomes}

\begin{quiz}
\item A model assigns probability $\frac{1}{\val{p29.rare.den}}$ to the token
  it then sees. How surprised should you be, in nats? \teachesat{2--3}
  \answerto{$\val{p29.rare.nats}$ nats, which is
  $\ln \val{p29.rare.den}$. Surprise is the negative logarithm of the
  probability, so a rarer outcome surprises you more and a certainty
  surprises you not at all.}
\item Why is surprise a logarithm rather than, say, $1 - p$?
  \teachesat{1--2}
  \answerto{Because independent surprises must add while their probabilities
  multiply, and the logarithm is the function that turns one into the other.}
\item Of all distributions over $\val{p29.seven.n}$ outcomes, which has the
  largest entropy? \teachesat{8--9}
  \answerto{The uniform one, and its entropy is
  $\ln \val{p29.seven.n} = \val{p29.seven.nats}$ nats.}
\item A model's perplexity is $\val{p29.f03.ppl}$. Say what that claims about
  the model, in a sentence with no logarithm in it. \teachesat{21--22}
  \answerto{At each position it is as uncertain as somebody choosing
  uniformly among $\val{p29.f03.ppl}$ tokens \dash{} out of a vocabulary of
  $\val{p29.vocab}$.}
\item Two models report next-token distributions. One has entropy
  $\val{p29.wrong.ent}$ nats, the other $\val{p29.spread.ent}$. Which is more
  likely to be right? \teachesat{28--29}
  \answerto{The question has no answer. Entropy measures how confident the
  distribution is, not whether it is correct; a confidently wrong model has a
  low one.}
\end{quiz}

\section{How surprised should you be}
\label{sec:P29-surprise}
\index{surprise!as negative log probability}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:P28} ended by saying the next question is not how sure you
are, but how much a message is worth. Start with a smaller one, because
everything in this program is built out of it.

A model puts probability $p$ on the token it then sees. You want one number
saying how surprising that was: large when $p$ is small, zero when the model
was certain, and \dash{} this is the requirement that does the work \dash{}
\textbf{additive over independent events}, because learning two unrelated
things ought to surprise you by the sum of what each does alone.

Independent probabilities \emph{multiply}. Program~\ref{prog:F03} owns the one
function that turns a product into a sum. So what is the measure?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{The negative logarithm of the probability}

\[ \text{surprise}(p) \;=\; -\ln p \]

and the negative sign is there only because a probability is at most one, so
its logarithm is at most zero. Nothing was chosen: the additivity requirement
plus Program~\ref{prog:F03}'s product law forces the logarithm, and the base
decides the unit rather than the function.

Check the two ends. A certainty, $p = 1$: the logarithm of one is zero, so a
certainty surprises you by \textbf{exactly nothing}. And as $p$ falls towards
zero the surprise grows without bound, which is what you want of an event the
model said could not happen.

A model puts $\frac{1}{\val{p29.rare.den}}$ on the token that arrives. How
surprised are you?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p29.rare.nats}$ nats, or $\val{p29.rare.bits}$ bits}

$\ln \val{p29.rare.den}$ and $\logb{2} \val{p29.rare.den}$, the same
measurement in Program~\ref{prog:F03}'s two units.

\begin{note}
$\val{p29.rare.bits}$, and not ten. A thousand is not a power of two \dash{}
$2^{10}$ is $1024$ \dash{} so the bits figure is a little under ten, and
Program~\ref{prog:F01}'s own $2^{10} \approx 10^{3}$ is exactly the
approximation that makes it nearly so. It is worth writing the digits rather
than the round number, because the whole of §3 turns on a bit being a
countable thing.
\end{note}

The additivity was the requirement, so it is checked rather than assumed: over
$\val{p29.add.pairs}$ pairs of independent events the surprise of the pair and
the sum of the two surprises agree to better than
$\val{p29.add.bound}$, which is rounding.

\mermaidfig{p29-surprise-adds}{Why the measure has to be a
  logarithm}{surprises add}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{So surprise is just another word for improbability.}}

It is a \emph{function} of the improbability and not a synonym for it, and the
difference is the whole reason the logarithm is there.

Take two independent events at $p = \frac{1}{2}$ each. Their probabilities
multiply to $\frac{1}{4}$; their surprises add to two bits. Now take a single
event at $p = \frac{1}{4}$: same probability, same surprise. That is the
property, and it is what makes surprise \textbf{accumulate over a sequence}
the way a probability cannot \dash{} which is why
Program~\ref{prog:F03} computes a sequence's likelihood in logarithms and
never as a product, and why Program~\ref{prog:P01} showed that the product
underflows to zero while the sum does not.

\enquote{Improbability} has no such arithmetic. Nothing sensible adds two
improbabilities.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\section{Entropy is average surprise}
\label{sec:P29-entropy}
\index{entropy!as average surprise}

\begin{fr}
One outcome has a surprise. A \emph{distribution} has a whole set of them, one
per outcome, and each arrives with its own probability.

Program~\ref{prog:P24} gives you the operation that turns a set of values and
their probabilities into one number, so there is nothing new to define here.
Write down the average surprise of a distribution $p$.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$H(p) = \Ex[-\ln p(X)] = -\sum_i p_i \ln p_i$}

That is the \textbf{entropy}, and it is Program~\ref{prog:P24}'s expectation
applied to §1's function and to nothing else. Every property it has comes from
one of those two places.

Two readings to fix it, and both are worth saying aloud:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{how surprised you should expect to be}, on average, by a draw
  from this distribution;
\item \textbf{how much uncertainty the distribution carries} \dash{} which is
  the same sentence read from the other end, because a distribution you are
  rarely surprised by is one you already knew the answer to.
\end{itemize}

Take the fairest coin there is. What is its entropy in bits?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Exactly $\val{p29.coin.bits}$}

Both outcomes at a half, each surprising you by one bit, so the average is one
bit. \textbf{That is the definition of the unit}: a bit is the entropy of one
fair coin, and §3 says what makes that a useful thing to measure in rather
than an arbitrary choice of scale.

Notice that the unit is a choice of logarithm base and nothing more. The same
coin is $\val{p29.coin.bits}$ bit or $\ln 2$ nats, and
Program~\ref{prog:F03} converts between them; neither is the more
fundamental. The bit wins the rest of this program for one reason only
\dash{} §3 sends binary digits down a wire, and a digit is a two-way choice.

Now bias the coin to $\val{p29.bias.p}$. Before computing: is the entropy
larger or smaller?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Smaller \dash{} $\val{p29.bias.bits}$ bits}

Under a half, and by a lot. Most draws now surprise you very little, and the
rare ones surprise you a great deal but arrive rarely enough not to make it
up. So a loaded coin carries less uncertainty than a fair one, which is what
the word ought to mean.

Note the shape, because the book has already met it:
$-p\ln p - (1-p)\ln(1-p)$ is largest at a half and falls away in both
directions, exactly as Program~\ref{prog:P25}'s $p(1-p)$ does. Two different
functions with the same qualitative story \dash{} a half is where a two-way
choice is hardest.

That suggests a general claim. Over a fixed number of outcomes, which
distribution has the \emph{largest} entropy?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{The uniform one}

The answer almost everybody gives, and it is right \dash{} but notice that
being right is not the same as having checked. So the script checks it, over
$\val{p29.max.tested}$ distributions on three outcomes laid on a grid of
$\val{p29.max.grid}$, and none beats the uniform one's
$\val{p29.max.nats}$ nats.

\begin{note}
The grid \textbf{contains} the uniform point, because $\val{p29.max.grid}$ is
divisible by three, so the maximum is attained rather than approached and the
comparison is an equality. A grid that missed it would report a maximum that
is not one \dash{} which is Program~\ref{prog:P10}'s finding about the flat
direction arriving from the other side: you cannot find a special point by
sampling unless you have arranged to land on it.
\end{note}

For $\val{p29.seven.n}$ outcomes that gives $\ln \val{p29.seven.n} =
\val{p29.seven.nats}$ nats, or $\val{p29.seven.bits}$ bits \dash{} which is a
pair Program~\ref{prog:F03} already committed, and §4 is about to need.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{Entropy measures how much information is in the message.}}

It measures how much you did not already know, which is not the same thing and
is a property of the \emph{distribution} rather than of any message.

A distribution that always emits the same token has entropy zero. The token
still arrives, still occupies a slot, still costs a forward pass \dash{} and
carries nothing, because you could have written it down beforehand. Nothing
about the message changed; what changed is what you knew.

That is why the same text has different entropies under different models, and
why a claim of the form \enquote{this text contains $N$ bits} is incomplete
until it names the distribution it was measured against. §6 is what happens
when somebody forgets.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\section{What a bit is}
\label{sec:P29-code}
\index{entropy!as a code length}
\index{bit}

\begin{fr}
Section 2 said a bit is the entropy of a fair coin, which fixes the scale and
explains nothing. Program~\ref{prog:F03} converts between bits and nats and
never says what either one \emph{is}.

Here is the question that settles it. You have to send one symbol from a small
alphabet down a wire, as binary digits, and you get to choose the codewords.
You want the \textbf{average} number of digits to be as small as possible,
over many symbols drawn from a known distribution.

One rule: the code must be \textbf{prefix-free}, so no codeword is the
beginning of another. Otherwise the receiver cannot tell where one symbol ends
and the next begins.

Given that rule, what stops you giving every symbol a one-digit codeword?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{There are only two one-digit codewords, and a short codeword uses up a
whole branch of the possibilities}

Spend `0' on one symbol and every codeword beginning with `0' is gone. That is
the constraint, and it has an exact form: a prefix-free code with lengths
$l_1, \ldots, l_n$ exists if and only if

\[ \sum_i 2^{-l_i} \;\le\; 1 \]

Each codeword of length $l$ consumes a fraction $2^{-l}$ of the available
space, and you cannot spend more than all of it. Nothing here is about
probability yet: it is a statement about how many strings there are.

So the design problem is now completely concrete. Choose lengths meeting that
budget, and minimise $\sum_i p_i l_i$.

\mermaidfig{p29-code-budget}{What a short codeword spends}{the code budget}

Try the budget on a case. Three symbols, lengths $1$, $1$ and $2$ \dash{}
admissible?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{No \dash{} $\frac12 + \frac12 + \frac14$ is more than one}

The two one-digit codewords are `0' and `1', and between them they use the
whole space; there is no room left for a third codeword of any length. The
budget is not a guideline, it is a count of the strings that exist.

Take four symbols at $\frac{1}{2}$, $\frac{1}{4}$, $\frac{1}{8}$,
$\frac{1}{8}$. Its entropy is $\val{p29.code.h}$ bits, which the script
computes over fractions with no rounding anywhere, because every probability
is a power of two and every surprise is therefore a whole number of bits.

Now design the code. Which four lengths would you choose?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$1$, $2$, $3$, $3$}

$\frac12 + \frac14 + \frac18 + \frac18 = 1$, so the budget is spent exactly,
and the expected length is

\[ \tfrac12(1) + \tfrac14(2) + \tfrac18(3) + \tfrac18(3)
   \;=\; \val{p29.code.best}\ \text{bits} \]

which is \textbf{the entropy}, to the digit. Each symbol got a codeword whose
length is its own surprise in bits, and the average of the lengths is
therefore the average of the surprises.

That is suggestive and it is one example. Is it the best code, or merely a
good one?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{The best, and this is checked rather than argued}

The script enumerates \textbf{every} assignment of lengths up to
$\val{p29.code.cap}$ that meets the budget \dash{}
$\val{p29.code.assignments}$ of them \dash{} and none does better than
$\val{p29.code.best}$.

\begin{center}
\emph{That is a proof for this alphabet rather than evidence about it,\\
because the search left nothing out.}
\end{center}

Program~\ref{prog:P14} owns that distinction and Program~\ref{prog:P13} used
it for topological orders; this is the same move, and it is available for the
same reason \dash{} the thing being quantified over is finite, and it was all
quantified over.

The general statement is Shannon's and this book does not prove it.

\begin{rigourbox}
\textbf{No prefix-free code has an expected length below the entropy}, for any
distribution, and one exists within one bit of it. The proof is Jensen's
inequality applied to the Kraft budget \dash{} which is
Program~\ref{prog:P19}'s inequality doing a third job \dash{} and it lives in
any information theory course. What is checked here is the two ends of it: the
lower bound by exhaustion on two alphabets, and the upper bound by
construction on one.
\end{rigourbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The example was chosen to make the bound tight, and it is worth seeing what
happens when it is not.

Four symbols at $\frac{2}{5}$, $\frac{1}{5}$, $\frac{1}{5}$, $\frac{1}{5}$
have entropy $\val{p29.nondy.h}$ bits. Those are not powers of two, so no
symbol's surprise is a whole number of bits \dash{} and a codeword length is a
whole number whatever you do.

What is the best achievable expected length, and where does the difference go?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p29.nondy.best}$ bits, and the difference is the rounding}

Exactly two, from four codewords of two digits each; the gap over the entropy
is $\val{p29.nondy.gap}$ of a bit. There is nowhere else for it to go: the
entropy says what the average \emph{could} be if a length could be
$\logb{2} \frac{5}{2} = \num{1.32}$ digits, and it cannot.

So the honest statement of §3, and it is what makes a bit a quantity rather
than a scale:

\begin{center}
\emph{The entropy of a distribution is the shortest average number of binary
digits\\
you can encode its outcomes in, and it is achievable to within one digit.}
\end{center}

A bit is not a unit somebody picked. It is a \textbf{digit you would actually
have to send}, and the entropy counts them.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{Then entropy is about compression, and my model's loss tells
me how well it would compress.}}

The first half is right and the second is a claim with a quantity missing.

Everything above is per \emph{symbol} of a known alphabet, and a model's loss
is per \emph{token}. A compression claim is about a file, which is measured in
characters or bytes \dash{} so the two are the same quantity only after
multiplying by how many tokens a character costs, which is a property of the
tokeniser and appears nowhere in the loss. §6 does the multiplication and
shows what changing the tokeniser does to the answer.

And the codes above assume the distribution is \textbf{known to the receiver}.
A model's distribution changes at every position and depends on everything
before it, so a real scheme has to send it or agree it in advance. That does
not break the bound \dash{} it is why the bound is a bound.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\section{The effective number of choices}
\label{sec:P29-effective}
\index{perplexity!as an effective number of choices}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:P19} works out why you cannot average perplexities, with the
measurement, and hands one thing here by name: \textbf{what the number means}.
This is that.

An entropy in nats is a real number and nobody has an intuition for one. But
§2 gave a distribution whose entropy is easy to read: the uniform one over $n$
outcomes has entropy $\ln n$ exactly.

So run that backwards. Given an entropy $H$ in nats, what does $e^{H}$ tell
you?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{How many equally likely choices would carry the same uncertainty}

\[ \text{effective choices} \;=\; e^{H} \]

For a uniform distribution over $n$ this returns $n$, exactly \dash{} checked
at every $n$ from $2$ to $\val{p29.eff.checked}+1$, because a claim of the
form \enquote{always} should not rest on one case. For everything else it
returns a number between one and $n$, and that number is the honest answer to
\enquote{how many options is this distribution really weighing?}

\mermaidfig{p29-nats-to-count}{An entropy read as a count}{entropy as a count}

A distribution on four tokens with one at $\num{0.97}$ has an entropy of
$\val{p29.wrong.ent}$ nats. How many options is it effectively weighing?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{A little over one \dash{} it has four options and is using one of them}

$e^{\val{p29.wrong.ent}}$, and §5 comes back to that distribution because it
is the shape of a model that is certain and may be wrong.

Now the number the reader actually meets. Program~\ref{prog:F02} has a model
at a mean loss of $\val{p29.f02.loss}$ nats per token, and
Program~\ref{prog:F03} exponentiated that to a \textbf{perplexity} of
$\val{p29.f03.ppl}$ without saying what the answer was a count of.

Put the two together. What does a perplexity of $\val{p29.f03.ppl}$ claim
about that model?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{At each position it is as uncertain as somebody choosing uniformly among
$\val{p29.f03.ppl}$ tokens}

\begin{center}
\emph{A perplexity is the effective number of equally likely choices\\
the model is weighing at each step, and nothing else.}
\end{center}

That is a statement about a model rather than a position on a leaderboard, and
it can be checked against a vocabulary. At $\val{p29.vocab}$ tokens this model
has narrowed the field to $\val{p29.ppl.frac}$ per cent of it \dash{} which is
either impressive or terrible depending on the text, and is a question you can
now ask.

\begin{note}
Program~\ref{prog:P26} shows that the mean loss \emph{is} a cross-entropy, so
a perplexity is the exponential of a cross-entropy rather than of an entropy.
That distinction is Program~\ref{prog:P30}'s and it matters: this program
computes the entropy of \textbf{one} distribution and never a quantity between
two. What the exponential does to the number is the same either way, which is
why the reading transfers.
\end{note}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The reading is not about language models, and the clearest evidence is a
measurement this book has already printed for another reason.

Program~\ref{prog:P25} ran experiment E9 on an attention row over
$\val{p29.att.keys}$ keys and reported its entropy in nats: with the
$\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_k}}$ division, $\val{p29.att.scaled.ent}$ at the largest
head size; without it, $\val{p29.att.raw.ent}$. Its conclusion was that
scaling holds the entropy still, which is true and hard to picture.

An attention row is a distribution \dash{} the softmax is what makes it sum
to one \dash{} so §2's definition applies to it with no change of any kind.
That is worth pausing on, because nothing in this program was built for
language models: entropy is defined for any distribution over a finite set,
and an attention row and a next-token distribution are both exactly that.

Read both as counts. How many keys is that head looking at, in each case?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p29.att.scaled.eff}$ of $\val{p29.att.keys}$ with the division, and
$\val{p29.att.raw.eff}$ without it}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
 & entropy, nats & effective keys & of \\
\midrule
without $\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_k}}$ & $\val{p29.att.raw.ent}$
  & $\val{p29.att.raw.eff}$ & $\val{p29.att.keys}$ \\
with it & $\val{p29.att.scaled.ent}$
  & $\val{p29.att.scaled.eff}$ & $\val{p29.att.keys}$ \\
\midrule
uniform, the ceiling & $\val{p29.att.maxent}$
  & $\val{p29.att.keys}$ & $\val{p29.att.keys}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Unscaled, the head has collapsed onto one key and a tenth of another.}
Program~\ref{prog:P25} could say the entropy was small; a count says the
architecture has thrown away seven eighths of what the head was built to do,
before any training has happened.

And the ceiling row is the identity from two frames ago rather than a fourth
measurement: $\val{p29.att.maxent}$ nats is $\ln \val{p29.att.keys}$, so its
exponential is $\val{p29.att.keys}$ on the nose.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{A perplexity of seven means the model was considering seven
tokens.}}

At one position, on one distribution, that reading is exactly right and it is
what §4 has just built: Program~\ref{prog:F03}'s seven-way uniform case has
entropy $\val{p29.seven.nats}$ nats and effective count $\val{p29.seven.n}$.

The trouble is that a reported perplexity is almost never one position. It is
the exponential of a \textbf{mean} loss over a corpus, and
Program~\ref{prog:P19} measured what that hides: at a spread of two nats the
per-token effective counts range over orders of magnitude while the reported
figure sits in the middle, and \emph{no position at all} need be weighing
seven tokens.

So the sentence to use is the guarded one. A perplexity of seven says the
model is on average as uncertain as a uniform seven-way choice; it does not
say any particular step looked like one. Program~\ref{prog:P19} owns why the
average is the exponential of the mean and never the mean of the
exponentials, and this is what that finding costs you when you read the
number aloud.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\section{Entropy at run time}
\label{sec:P29-runtime}
\index{entropy!as a runtime signal}

\begin{fr}
Everything so far has been about a number computed after the fact. The
distribution a model reports at inference is right there in front of you, one
per position, and its entropy costs a sum over the vocabulary.

Program~\ref{prog:F05} committed a four-token distribution at three
temperatures and Program~\ref{prog:P24} used it to price the sampling knobs.
Read its three rows as counts.

Before looking: which way does raising the temperature move the effective
number of choices?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Up \dash{} $\val{p29.tok.t05.eff}$, $\val{p29.tok.t10.eff}$,
$\val{p29.tok.t20.eff}$ of $\val{p29.tok.n}$}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
$T$ & $\num{0.5}$ & $\num{1.0}$ & $\num{2.0}$ \\
\midrule
entropy, nats & $\val{p29.tok.t05.ent}$ & $\val{p29.tok.t10.ent}$
  & $\val{p29.tok.t20.ent}$ \\
effective choices & $\val{p29.tok.t05.eff}$ & $\val{p29.tok.t10.eff}$
  & $\val{p29.tok.t20.eff}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Program~\ref{prog:F05} proved that temperature cannot move the $\argmax$, and
Program~\ref{prog:P24} measured what each sampling knob destroys. This is the
third reading of the same twelve numbers and it is the one you can put on a
dashboard: at $T = \num{0.5}$ the model is weighing about two of its four
options and at $T = 2$ about three and a half.

The ordering is what is asserted, not the three figures \dash{} a change of
logits moves them and cannot move the direction.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
That is a usable signal and it is unusually cheap. The entropy of a
distribution the model has already computed needs no second model, no
reference answer and no label; it is one pass over the logits you were about
to sample from anyway.

What it buys is a way of asking, at run time, \textbf{is this model choosing
or guessing} \dash{} an answer near $1$ says the next token is nearly forced,
and one near the vocabulary size says the model has no idea. Route the second
kind to a larger model, or to a human, or to a refusal.

Now the question that decides whether any of that is sound. Two models report
distributions over the same four tokens: one with entropy
$\val{p29.wrong.ent}$ nats, the other with $\val{p29.spread.ent}$. Which is
more likely to be \emph{right}?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{The question has no answer, and that is the point}

\transcript{p29-effective-choices}

The listing prints the two effective counts: four, exactly, for the spread
distribution, because a uniform distribution's effective count is its size;
and a little over one for the confident one.

\textbf{Entropy measures how confident a distribution is, not whether it is
correct.} A model that is certain and wrong has a low entropy, and it looks
from the outside exactly like a model that is certain and right. Nothing in
this program can tell them apart, because nothing in this program has seen the
answer.

That is Program~\ref{prog:P28} §6's judge from the other side. There, a model
stated $\val{p28.judge.says.pct}$ per cent and was right
$\val{p28.judge.means.pct}$ per cent of the time; a low entropy is exactly the
shape of that defect, and the repair was the same one \dash{} count what it
said against what happened.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{Low entropy means the model is confident, so I can trust the
answer.}}

The first clause is a definition and the second does not follow from it. What
you can conclude from a low entropy is exactly one thing: \emph{this model
would give nearly the same answer if you asked again}. That is repeatability,
and it is not accuracy.

The habit is worth naming because it is how a monitoring dashboard becomes
worse than nothing. An entropy threshold catches the case where the model
knows it is guessing, which is real and useful and cheap. It is silent on the
case that costs the most \dash{} the confident hallucination \dash{} and a
dashboard that is green through exactly the incident you care about will be
trusted right up until it is not.

\textbf{Use it as a filter and never as a warrant.} High entropy is evidence
of trouble; low entropy is evidence of nothing.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\section{What a bits-per-token figure is missing}
\label{sec:P29-tokeniser}
\index{entropy!bits per character}

\begin{fr}
Section 3 made a bit a digit somebody would have to send, which invites a
claim people make often: a language model is a compressor, and its loss says
how good a one.

Program~\ref{prog:F03} converted this model's $\val{p29.f02.loss}$ nats to
$\val{f03.nats.to.bits}$ bits. That is bits per \textbf{token}.

A compression figure is quoted in bits per \textbf{character}. What else do
you need?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{How many tokens a character costs}

\[ \frac{\text{bits}}{\text{character}}
   \;=\; \frac{\text{bits}}{\text{token}}
   \times \frac{\text{tokens}}{\text{character}} \]

and the second factor is a property of the \textbf{tokeniser}, which appears
nowhere in the loss. Two tokenisers on the same text at
$\val{p29.tpc.a}$ and $\val{p29.tpc.b}$ tokens per character give

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
tokens per character & $\val{p29.tpc.a}$ & $\val{p29.tpc.b}$ \\
bits per character & $\val{p29.bpc.a}$ & $\val{p29.bpc.b}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The same model and the same text. By what factor do the two answers differ,
and what is that factor a property of?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p29.bpc.ratio}$, and it is a property of the tokenisers}

$\val{p29.tpc.b} / \val{p29.tpc.a}$, exactly \dash{} the bits-per-token
factor cancels, so the ratio says nothing whatever about the model. The script
asserts that, because it is the section's whole claim.

So a bits-per-token figure is a ratio quoted without one of its two
quantities, which is this book's recurring complaint arriving in the place it
does the most damage: two models are routinely compared on loss, and a
comparison of losses is only a comparison of models when the tokeniser is
held fixed.

\begin{center}
\emph{A loss is per token. A compression claim is per character.\\
Between them sits a number nobody puts on the model card.}
\end{center}

Three consequences worth carrying, and each is checkable rather than a
caution.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A model can lower its loss by changing its tokeniser}, with the
  weights untouched. Coarser tokens mean fewer, harder predictions; the loss
  per token rises and the loss per character need not.
\item \textbf{Two losses are comparable only under one tokeniser.} That is
  the condition, it is usually unstated, and stating it costs a sentence.
\item \textbf{A compression claim is checkable and a loss is not.} Bits per
  character is a file size somebody can produce, so the claim has an
  experiment attached to it \dash{} which is why it is worth converting to.
\end{itemize}

\mermaidfig{p29-two-tokenisers}{The factor between a loss and a compression
  figure}{two tokenisers}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Look back at what this program did and did not do.

It defined one function, $-\ln p$, and took its average. Everything else was a
reading: entropy is a code length, the exponential of an entropy is a count,
and a perplexity is that count for a model. No new object was built, and the
only genuinely new fact is §3's \dash{} that the average code length cannot go
below the entropy, which is what makes a bit a thing you send rather than a
scale somebody picked.

What it deliberately did not do is compare two distributions. Every entropy
here is of \textbf{one} distribution against itself. The moment you ask what
it costs to use one distribution's code on another's outcomes, the answer
splits into the target's own entropy plus an excess \dash{} and that excess is
the object Program~\ref{prog:P26} named without defining and
Program~\ref{prog:P22} declared one fact about.

Program~\ref{prog:P30} defines it, and shows that it is not symmetric.
\end{fr}

\begin{summarybox}
\sumitem{1--2}{\textbf{\result{Surprise is $-\ln p$}}, and the logarithm is forced
  rather than chosen: independent surprises must add while their
  probabilities multiply. A certainty surprises you by exactly nothing.}
\sumitem{3}{A probability of $\frac{1}{\val{p29.rare.den}}$ is
  $\val{p29.rare.nats}$ nats or $\val{p29.rare.bits}$ bits of surprise
  \dash{} not ten, because a thousand is not a power of two.}
\sumitem{5--7}{\result{\textbf{Entropy is average surprise}, which is
  Program~\ref{prog:P24}'s expectation applied to that function:
  $H(p) = -\sum_i p_i \ln p_i$}. A fair coin is exactly
  $\val{p29.coin.bits}$ bit and a coin at $\val{p29.bias.p}$ is
  $\val{p29.bias.bits}$.}
\sumitem{8--9}{\result{The uniform distribution has the largest entropy
  over a fixed number of outcomes} \dash{} checked over
  $\val{p29.max.tested}$ distributions on a grid that contains the uniform
  point, so the maximum is attained and not approached.}
\sumitem{11--13}{\result{A prefix-free code exists with lengths $l_i$
  exactly when $\sum_i 2^{-l_i} \le 1$}. A short codeword is expensive because it spends a
  whole branch.}
\sumitem{14--15}{\textbf{\result{Entropy is the shortest average code length}}, and
  that is what a bit is. On a dyadic alphabet the best of
  $\val{p29.code.assignments}$ admissible codes is
  $\val{p29.code.best}$ bits and the entropy is $\val{p29.code.h}$ \dash{}
  exhaustive, so a proof for that alphabet rather than evidence about it.}
\sumitem{16--17}{\result{Where the probabilities are not powers of two the
  bound cannot be attained}: $\val{p29.nondy.best}$ against
  $\val{p29.nondy.h}$, a gap of $\val{p29.nondy.gap}$, and the difference is
  the rounding of a length to a whole number.}
\sumitem{19--21}{\result{\textbf{$e^{H}$ is the effective number of equally likely
  choices}, returning $n$ exactly for a uniform distribution over $n$}.}
\sumitem{22}{\result{\textbf{A perplexity is that count for a model.}}
  Program~\ref{prog:F02}'s $\val{p29.f02.loss}$ nats give
  Program~\ref{prog:F03}'s $\val{p29.f03.ppl}$, which is
  $\val{p29.ppl.frac}$ per cent of a $\val{p29.vocab}$-token vocabulary.}
\sumitem{23--24}{Program~\ref{prog:P25}'s attention entropies read as counts:
  $\val{p29.att.raw.eff}$ effective keys of $\val{p29.att.keys}$ without the
  $\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_k}}$ division and $\val{p29.att.scaled.eff}$ with it.}
\sumitem{25}{A reported perplexity is the exponential of a \emph{mean}, so no
  particular position need look like it \dash{} which is what
  Program~\ref{prog:P19}'s finding costs you when you read the number aloud.}
\sumitem{26--27}{Temperature read as a count on
  Program~\ref{prog:F05}'s own distribution: $\val{p29.tok.t05.eff}$,
  $\val{p29.tok.t10.eff}$, $\val{p29.tok.t20.eff}$ of
  $\val{p29.tok.n}$ as $T$ rises.}
\sumitem{28--30}{\textbf{\result{Entropy measures confidence and not correctness.}} A
  confidently wrong model has a low one. Use it as a filter on the model's own
  guessing and never as a warrant for an answer.}
\sumitem{31--33}{\result{\textbf{Bits per token is not bits per character.} Multiply
  by tokens per character, which belongs to the tokeniser}:
  $\val{p29.tpc.a}$ and $\val{p29.tpc.b}$ give
  $\val{p29.bpc.a}$ and $\val{p29.bpc.b}$ on the same model and the same
  text, a factor of $\val{p29.bpc.ratio}$ that is the tokenisers' ratio and
  nothing about the model.}
\sumitem{34}{\result{Two losses are comparable only under one tokeniser},
  and a compression claim is checkable where a loss is not.}
\end{summarybox}

\begin{testexercises}
\item A model puts probability $\num{0.02}$ on the token that arrives. Give
  the surprise in nats and in bits.
  \answerto{$-\ln \num{0.02} = \num{3.912}$ nats;
  $\num{3.912} \times \val{f03.inv.ln.two} = \num{5.644}$ bits. Note that a
  bit figure is always the larger of the two, because a bit is the smaller
  unit \dash{} Program~\ref{prog:F03}'s warning.}
\item Which has the larger entropy: a fair six-sided die, or a coin biased to
  $\num{0.99}$? Answer before computing, then compute.
  \answerto{The die, by a wide margin: $\ln 6 = \num{1.792}$ nats against
  $\num{0.056}$. Almost everybody gets the direction right; the size is the
  surprise, and it is because a coin at $\num{0.99}$ is very nearly a
  constant.}
\item You are told a distribution over $16$ outcomes has entropy $2$ bits.
  How many outcomes is it effectively weighing, and what is the largest it
  could have been?
  \answerto{$2^{2} = 4$ effectively, against a ceiling of
  $\logb{2} 16 = 4$ bits and $16$ outcomes. So it is using a quarter of the
  choices available to it.}
\item Your evaluation reports a perplexity of $\num{30.0}$ and a colleague
  says the model has \enquote{narrowed it down to thirty tokens}. Say what is
  right and what is wrong with that.
  \answerto{Right that the model is on average as uncertain as a uniform
  thirty-way choice. Wrong that any position looked like that: the figure is
  the exponential of a mean loss, and Program~\ref{prog:P19} measured how far
  the per-position counts spread around it.}
\item Two teams report losses of $\num{2.1}$ and $\num{2.3}$ nats per token
  on the same corpus and the second team's model is declared worse. What must
  you check first?
  \answerto{That both used the same tokeniser. Bits per token times tokens per
  character is bits per character, and only the second is a property of the
  model and the text rather than of the tokeniser \dash{} so a coarser
  tokeniser raises the loss per token while the model is unchanged.}
\end{testexercises}

\begin{furtherproblems}
\item Show that a distribution with one outcome at probability $1$ has entropy
  exactly zero, and say what happens to the terms for the outcomes at
  probability zero.
  \answerto{The certain outcome contributes $1 \times \ln 1 = 0$. Each other
  contributes $p\ln p$ at $p = 0$, which is not defined as written; the
  convention is that it is $0$, and it is the right one because
  $p \ln p \to 0$ as $p \to 0$. An impossible outcome carries no weight
  however surprising it would be.}
\item A prefix-free code assigns lengths $2, 2, 2, 3, 3$. Is it admissible,
  and if there is slack, what does the slack mean?
  \answerto{$3 \times \frac14 + 2 \times \frac18 = 1$, so it is admissible
  with nothing to spare. Had it come to less than one there would be an
  unused branch, and some codeword could be shortened \dash{} which is why an
  optimal code always spends the budget exactly when the probabilities allow
  it.}
\item Two independent distributions have entropies $H_1$ and $H_2$. What is
  the entropy of the pair, and what is the effective number of choices?
  \answerto{$H_1 + H_2$, because the surprises add and the expectation is
  linear; so the effective count is $e^{H_1 + H_2} = e^{H_1}e^{H_2}$, the
  product of the two counts. That is the reason the exponential is the right
  way to read an entropy: counts of independent choices multiply, as
  Program~\ref{prog:F10}'s product rule says they must.}
\item Program~\ref{prog:P25} reports an attention entropy of
  $\val{p29.att.raw8.ent}$ nats at the smallest head size without the
  division. Read it as a count and say why it is larger than
  $\val{p29.att.raw.ent}$ at the largest.
  \answerto{$\val{p29.att.raw8.eff}$ keys of $\val{p29.att.keys}$. It is
  larger because the unscaled scores grow with $d_k$, so at a small head size
  they have not yet spread far enough to saturate the softmax \dash{} which is
  the whole of what the $\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_k}}$ division is for, said as a
  count instead of as a variance.}
\item A colleague proposes monitoring the entropy of the next-token
  distribution and paging when it exceeds a threshold. Give one failure this
  catches and one it cannot, and say which is more expensive.
  \answerto{It catches a model that has no idea and knows it \dash{} a query
  outside the training distribution, a corrupted prompt, a truncated context.
  It cannot catch a confident hallucination, whose entropy is low by
  definition. The second is more expensive, because the first produces a
  visibly poor answer and the second produces a fluent wrong one.}
\end{furtherproblems}
