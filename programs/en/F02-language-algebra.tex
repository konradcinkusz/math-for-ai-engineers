\program{The language of algebra}
\label{prog:F02}
\index{algebra!symbols and terms}
\index{notation!reading a printed formula}

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{1--6}{say what each symbol in an expression stands for, and evaluate
  the expression by substitution, negative values included}
\outcome{7--12}{collect like terms, and say why two terms that look similar
  cannot be collected}
\outcome{13--19}{apply the order of operations, and restore the brackets a
  printed equation leaves out when you flatten it to one line of code}
\outcome{20--27}{expand a product of brackets, including $(a \pm b)^{2}$ and
  $(a+b)(a-b)$, and say exactly how many products a pair of brackets makes}
\outcome{28--31}{factorise a common factor and a difference of two squares, and
  read a factored form as the same value computed with fewer operations}
\outcome{32--37}{combine and simplify algebraic fractions, and cancel a factor
  without cancelling a term}
\outcome{38--44}{change the subject of a formula, state the condition the
  rearrangement needs, and check it by substitution}
\outcome{45--50}{read a published formula exactly as printed \dash{} floors,
  roots and all \dash{} and turn it into an expression you can defend}
\end{outcomes}

\begin{quiz}
\item Evaluate $wx + b$ for $w = -2$, $x = 5$ and $b = 3$. \teachesat{1--6}
  \answerto{$-7$. The multiplication is done before the addition.}
\item In $W^{(2)}x$, is the superscript a power? \teachesat{4--5}
  \answerto{No \dash{} it is a layer label. Nothing in the notation
  distinguishes the two, the bracket is the convention, and the convention is
  not universal.}
\item Evaluate $2 + 3 \times 4^{2}$. \teachesat{13--14}
  \answerto{$50$. The index first, then the multiplication, then the addition.}
\item Evaluate $-3^{2}$ and $(-3)^{2}$. \teachesat{14--15}
  \answerto{$-9$ and $9$. The index binds more tightly than the sign.}
\item Write the printed $\frac{x - \mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^{2} + \eps}}$ as one line
  of code. \teachesat{16--18}
  \answerto{\code{(x - mu) / sqrt(sigma2 + eps)}. The fraction bar and the root
  sign are both brackets that were never printed.}
\item Simplify $4p^{2} - 2p + 3 - p^{2} + 5p$. \teachesat{7--11}
  \answerto{$3p^{2} + 3p + 3$.}
\item Expand $(a+b)^{2}$. \teachesat{22--23}
  \answerto{$a^{2} + 2ab + b^{2}$. Check it at $a = 3$ and $b = 4$: the answer
  is $49$, not $25$.}
\item Expand $(2x+3)(2x-3)$. \teachesat{24--25}
  \answerto{$4x^{2} - 9$. The two cross terms cancel, and this is the one
  common case in which they do.}
\item Factorise $4x^{2} - 6x$. \teachesat{28--29}
  \answerto{$2x(2x - 3)$.}
\item Combine $\frac{1}{x} + \frac{1}{y}$ into one fraction.
  \teachesat{33--34}
  \answerto{$\frac{y + x}{xy}$.}
\item Simplify $\frac{a + b}{a}$. \teachesat{34--35}
  \answerto{$1 + \frac{b}{a}$, and not $b$. A factor may be cancelled; a term
  may not.}
\item $L = (y - \hat{y})^{2}$. Make $\hat{y}$ the subject. \teachesat{41--42}
  \answerto{$\hat{y} = y \pm \sqrt{L}$ \dash{} two answers. A loss says how far
  away you are and never on which side.}
\item With $W = \val{f02.conv.w}$, $k = \val{f02.conv.k}$,
  $p = \val{f02.conv.p}$ and $s = \val{f02.conv.s}$, what is the output size of
  a convolution? \teachesat{45--48}
  \answerto{$3$. The floor is part of the formula: $\num{3.5}$ is not a shape
  and $4$ is the wrong repair. One input column is discarded, silently.}
\end{quiz}

\section{Symbols, and what each one stands for}
\label{sec:F02-symbols}
\index{algebra!substitution}
\index{notation!superscript as a label}

\begin{fr}
Arithmetic is what you do to numbers you have. Algebra is what you do to
numbers you have not fixed yet, and that difference is the whole of this
program.

A \emph{symbol} stands for a quantity. Until you say which quantity, it stands
for any of them:
\[ y = wx + b \]
Four symbols and two operations \dash{} and also the smallest model in this
book: an input $x$, a weight $w$, a bias $b$, an output $y$. Notice what the
line does not say. It does not say what any of them is, and it is still a
complete instruction.

Take $w = 3$, $x = 2$ and $b = 1$. What is $y$?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$y = 7$}
None of that was algebra. You substituted three numbers and did arithmetic,
and that is what every equation in every paper you will ever read comes down
to, once you know what the letters are.

The same letter plays four different roles, and which one it is playing depends
entirely on what you are doing at the time:

\begin{itemize}
\item a \emph{variable} \dash{} a quantity that changes: $x$ and $y$;
\item a \emph{constant} \dash{} a quantity fixed once and for all, like $\pi$;
\item a \emph{parameter} \dash{} fixed for now, chosen earlier: $w$ and $b$;
\item a \emph{coefficient} \dash{} the number multiplying a term, which is $w$
  again, seen from the term's side.
\end{itemize}

At inference time $x$ varies while $w$ and $b$ are held still. In a training
loop it is the other way round: the data is given and the parameters are what
you are hunting for.

So, in a training loop: which symbols are the unknowns?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$w$ and $b$.}
Three different things get written with symbols, they are not the same thing,
and knowing which one you are looking at tells you what you are allowed to do
with it.

An \emph{expression} computes: $2(x+3)$ is a recipe, and it has a value the
moment $x$ has one.

An \emph{equation} claims: $2x = 6$ is a claim, and it is true for exactly one
value of $x$. Solving it means finding that value.

An \emph{identity} is an equation true for \emph{every} value:
$2(x+3) = 2x+6$ holds whatever $x$ is. Every rule in the rest of this program
is an identity, and that is precisely why you are allowed to swap one side for
the other whenever it suits you.

Classify each of $3x - 1$, $\;3x - 1 = 8$ and $\;3x - 1 = x + (2x - 1)$.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$3x - 1$ is an expression. \quad $3x - 1 = 8$ is an equation, true only at
$x = 3$. \quad $3x - 1 = x + (2x - 1)$ is an identity: clear the bracket on the
right and it is the left-hand side, whatever $x$ is.
\end{ansblock}

\begin{notationbox}
The letters are not arbitrary. Machine-learning writing has settled on a small
alphabet, and papers get considerably faster to read once you stop decoding it:

\begin{itemize}
\item $x$ an input, $y$ a target, $\hat{y}$ a prediction;
\item $w$ or $W$ a weight, $b$ a bias, $\theta$ the parameters taken together;
\item $\eta$ a learning rate, $L$ a loss, $\eps$ a small guard against
  division by zero;
\item $n$ a count, $d$ a dimension, $i$ and $j$ indices.
\end{itemize}

A subscript is nearly always an index: $x_{i}$ is the $i$th input. A superscript
is nearly always a power \dash{} except when it is bracketed, and then it is a
label. $W^{(2)}$ is the second layer's weights; $W^{2}$ is a weight matrix
squared. The bracket is doing all of the work and no paper stops to say so.
\end{notationbox}

So in $W^{(2)}x$: is the $2$ a power?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{No \dash{} it is a label.}
Nothing in the printed line tells you. The bracketed superscript is a
convention: most of the field follows it, enough does not, so you check. A
paper writing $W^{(l)}$ and $x^{2}$ a line later has used the same mark for
two unrelated jobs.

Substitution has its own hazard, brackets again. To evaluate
$3x^{2}$ at $x = -2$, write the substituted value \emph{in brackets}:
\[ 3(-2)^{2} = 3 \times 4 = 12 \]
Drop them, write $3-2^{2}$, and you get $-1$: the answer to a different
question.

\yourturn
Evaluate $wx + b$ at $w = -2$, $x = 5$ and $b = 3$; then evaluate $3x^{2}$ at
$x = -2$.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$wx + b = (-2)(5) + 3 = -10 + 3 = -7$.

$3x^{2} = 3(-2)^{2} = 3 \times 4 = 12$. The square of a negative number is
positive, and the brackets are what carry that fact into the arithmetic.
\end{ansblock}

\begin{aibox}
Every equation in this program is a line you will eventually type. A paper sets
$\frac{x - \mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^{2} + \eps}}$ and no editor will take it; what an
editor takes is one line of ASCII carrying brackets the printer never needed,
because the printer had a fraction bar and a root sign to group with and you
have neither.

That translation is what this program is for. The algebra is school algebra you
have met before. The discipline of getting it onto one line without changing
what it computes is not, and it is where the mistakes live.
\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\section{Terms, coefficients and like terms}
\label{sec:F02-terms}
\index{algebra!like terms}
\index{algebra!coefficient}

\begin{fr}
An expression is built out of \emph{terms}, and the terms are separated by the
$+$ and $-$ signs that lie between them. The sign belongs to the term that
follows it \dash{} read it any other way and you have a sign error waiting.

Inside a term everything is multiplied. The number in front is the
\emph{coefficient}, and the things multiplied together are the \emph{factors}.
So $4a^{3}$ is one term, its coefficient is $4$, and its factors are $4$, $a$,
$a$ and $a$. A term with no coefficient written down has one anyway: $a$ means
$1a$ and $-a$ means $-1a$.

Now read this one:
\[ 3x^{2} - 5x + 7 \]
How many terms has it, and what is the coefficient of $x$?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Three terms; the coefficient of $x$ is $-5$.}
The minus belongs to the term. It is $-5$ and not $5$, and getting that wrong
is the most common slip in the whole of algebra.

Two terms are \emph{like} when they carry exactly the same letters raised to
exactly the same powers. Like terms collect:
\[ 3x + 4x = 7x \]
and this is not a rule to be memorised. It is the distributive law read
backwards, $3x + 4x = x(3 + 4) = 7x$. You will meet the same law forwards in
\S\ref{sec:F02-expanding}; here it is doing its work in reverse.

Simplify $5a + 2b - 3a + b$.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$2a + 3b$}
Take the terms in order and keep each sign with its own term: $5a - 3a = 2a$,
and $2b + b = 3b$. There is nothing further to do, because $a$ and $b$ are
unlike and no rule combines them.

That is worth stating flatly. \textbf{Unlike terms do not collapse.} $x^{2}$
and $x$ are different terms and no amount of staring will merge them \dash{}
exactly as $2^{3}$ and $3^{2}$ refused to merge in
Program~\ref{prog:F01}. A law of indices needs a common base; collecting terms
needs the same letters at the same powers. It is one rule in two sets of
clothes.

Which of $2x^{2} + 3x^{2}$ and $2x^{2} + 3x$ collapses to a single term?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$2x^{2} + 3x^{2} = 5x^{2}$. The second does not collapse. $2x^{2} + 3x$ is
already as short as it goes, and writing $5x^{2}$ or $5x^{3}$ for it is wrong.
\end{ansblock}

\begin{warning}
Your framework will not stop you. Subtracting an array of shape $(n,1)$ from
one of shape $(n)$ is this same error \dash{} two unlike things combined
\dash{} and rather than refusing, the library broadcasts them against each
other and hands back an entirely ordinary $(n,n)$ result. No exception, no
warning, and a number at the far end that is wrong in a way none of your tests
is looking for. Program~\ref{prog:P07} measures what that one costs: a loss
averaged over every \emph{pair} instead of every example.

Mathematics refuses unlike terms. Array libraries do not, which is why
Program~\ref{prog:P07} spends its whole length on shapes.
\end{warning}

\yourturn
Simplify $4p^{2} - 2p + 3 - p^{2} + 5p$.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Collect the $p^{2}$ terms, then the $p$ terms, then the plain numbers:
\[ (4p^{2} - p^{2}) + (-2p + 5p) + 3 = 3p^{2} + 3p + 3 \]
Note that $-p^{2}$ has coefficient $-1$, not $0$. A missing digit is not a
missing term.
\end{ansblock}

Multiplying terms is a different operation from adding them, and mixing the two
up is the second most common slip here. When terms are multiplied, the
coefficients multiply and the indices add:
\[ 3x \times 4x = (3 \times 4)(x \times x) = 12x^{2} \]
The second half of that is Program~\ref{prog:F01}'s first law of indices,
turning up where it is actually used.

Simplify $2a \times 5a^{3}$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$10a^{4}$}
The coefficients give $2 \times 5 = 10$ and the indices give $1 + 3 = 4$. The
lone $a$ carries an index of $1$, which is invisible and easy to drop.

\begin{aibox}
Collecting like terms is what you are doing by hand whenever you count a
model's parameters. A layer with $m$ inputs and $n$ outputs contributes $mn$
weights and $n$ biases, and the feed-forward block of the original transformer
is two such layers back to back: $d$ in and $4d$ out, then $4d$ in and $d$ out.
Its weights therefore collect to $4d^{2} + 4d^{2} = 8d^{2}$, before a single
bias is counted. That is one collection of like terms carried out on a real
model's own numbers, and Program~\ref{prog:P32} does it for the whole block.
\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\section{Brackets, and what a printed equation leaves out}
\label{sec:F02-precedence}
\index{notation!invisible brackets}
\index{algebra!order of operations}

\begin{fr}
Everything so far has been read left to right without a fight. That stops here,
because an expression is not read in the order it is written.

The order is fixed, and it is the order your language uses as well:

\begin{enumerate}
\item brackets;
\item indices;
\item multiplication and division, left to right;
\item addition and subtraction, left to right.
\end{enumerate}

Two things about that list matter more than the list itself. It is a
\emph{binding} order rather than an instruction to work in stages \dash{} it
says which operation owns which operand. And where a reader could genuinely go
either way, brackets are not politeness; they are the only thing carrying what
you meant.

Evaluate $2 + 3 \times 4^{2}$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$50$}
The index binds first, giving $4^{2} = 16$; then the multiplication,
$3 \times 16 = 48$; then the addition, $2 + 48 = 50$. An answer of $80$ added
before multiplying, an answer of $146$ multiplied before taking the index, and
an answer of $400$ the whole line read strictly left to right.

Now a smaller one, which catches more people than the first did. Evaluate
$-3^{2}$, and then evaluate $(-3)^{2}$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$-3^{2} = -9$ \quad and \quad $(-3)^{2} = 9$}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{The index binds more tightly than the sign in front of it.} $-3^{2}$
means $-(3^{2})$, which is $-9$. Only the bracket in $(-3)^{2}$ makes the minus
part of the thing being squared.

Where the belief comes from is worth naming, because it is not carelessness.
You read $-3$ as a single number \dash{} which in almost every other context it
is \dash{} and then you square it. The notation disagrees with you: here the
minus is an operator applied to $3^{2}$, not a sign attached to the $3$.

Your language agrees with the mathematics rather than with you. In Python
\code{-3**2} is $-9$. JavaScript will not even guess: \code{-3 ** 2} is a
syntax error, and the message says a bracket is needed to disambiguate the
precedence. That refusal is this trap's own point \dash{} the line is ambiguous
to a reader in precisely the way it is unambiguous to a parser.

So when your reading and your program disagree, the bug is in your formula, and
a squared error that comes out negative is often exactly this. A
\emph{variance} that comes out negative is a different fault with a different
cause \dash{} catastrophic cancellation in the one-pass formula \dash{} and it
belongs to Program~\ref{prog:P02}.
\end{trapbox}
Something else gets left off the printed page, and multiplication is the usual
victim: $2x$ means $2 \times x$ with the sign not printed. Harmless, until it
meets a division. Read $1/2x$ \dash{} what are its two readings?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
It reads either as $\frac{1}{2}x$ or as $\frac{1}{2x}$, and those two differ
by a factor of $x^{2}$. Strict precedence gives the first, because $/$ and
$\times$ have equal standing and run left to right. A good deal of the world
writes it meaning the second.
\end{ansblock}

Which brings us to the rule this section exists for.

\textbf{A printed fraction bar and a printed root sign are brackets that were
never printed.} They group what sits under them, and they do it by position
instead of by a mark \dash{} so a display that is unambiguous on the page
becomes ambiguous the moment you flatten it onto one line.

Worked. The display
\[ \frac{a + b}{c + d} \]
carries two invisible bracket pairs, one round the numerator and one round the
denominator. Flattened without them, \code{a + b / c + d} computes
$a + \frac{b}{c} + d$, which is a different number and raises nothing at all.
The honest flattening is \code{(a + b) / (c + d)}.

\yourturn
Here is a real one \dash{} a layer normalisation, which centres a row of
numbers and divides by its own spread:
\[ \frac{x - \mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^{2} + \eps}} \]
Flatten it to one line of code, supplying every bracket the printer did not
need.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
\begin{center}
\code{(x - mu) / sqrt(sigma2 + eps)}
\end{center}
Three flattenings that look right and are not:

\begin{itemize}
\item \code{x - mu / sqrt(sigma2 + eps)} subtracts a quotient from $x$: the
  numerator's invisible bracket has gone.
\item \code{(x - mu) / sqrt(sigma2) + eps} adds $\eps$ after the division: the
  root's invisible bracket has gone, and with it the whole reason for the
  $\eps$.
\item \code{(x - mu) / (sqrt(sigma2) + eps)} keeps $\eps$ out of the root but
  inside the divisor. Closer, and still not what was printed.
\end{itemize}
\end{ansblock}

Take those last two seriously for a moment. Is the printed line
\code{sqrt(sigma2) + eps} or \code{sqrt(sigma2 + eps)}, and does the difference
reach the numbers?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{The second \dash{} and yes, it reaches the numbers.}
The $\eps$ sits inside the root because that is where it does its work: it
stops the square root of a nearly zero variance from being nearly zero, and so
stops the division from exploding.

Take a row whose variance is $\val{f02.lnorm.var}$ \dash{} a nearly constant
row, which is the case the $\eps$ exists for \dash{} with
$\eps = \val{f02.lnorm.eps}$, $x = \val{f02.lnorm.x}$ and
$\mu = \val{f02.lnorm.mu}$. Read as printed, the line gives
$\val{f02.ln.eps.inside}$. Misread with the $\eps$ outside the root but still
inside the divisor, it gives
$\val{f02.ln.eps.outside}$, which is \val{f02.ln.eps.diff.pct}\% above the
printed value.

\begin{aibox}
That is a real line of a real system \dash{} layer normalisation, from Ba,
Kiros and Hinton, with the $\eps$ as the reference implementations write it
rather than as the paper does \dash{} and it sits inside every transformer you
will use. The
$\eps$ is a numerical decision rather than punctuation, and where it sits
changes what the layer does to a near-constant feature.

Note the honest limit of the figure above. The variance was chosen small on
purpose: at a healthy variance the two readings agree to several decimal places
and the demonstration would show nothing. The ratio is not typical. The failure
mode is. What the $\eps$ is protecting against, and how much of it a given
number format can absorb, is Program~\ref{prog:P02}.
\end{aibox}

One more, and this time you have seen the shape of it. An optimiser update is
printed as
\[ \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \, \frac{\hat{m}}{\sqrt{\hat{v}} + \eps} \]
Is \code{eta * mhat / sqrt(vhat) + eps} that line?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
No. That line divides by $\sqrt{\hat{v}}$ and then adds $\eps$ to the whole
step, which leaves the division with nothing guarding it at all. The printed
line puts $\eps$ inside the divisor:
\begin{center}
\code{eta * mhat / (sqrt(vhat) + eps)}
\end{center}
\end{ansblock}

That is the Adam update, from Kingma and Ba, and it is the second real line in
this section that one missing bracket pair changes without saying so.

\begin{note}
Both $\eps$ conventions appear in the literature for this update: some papers
and some implementations put the $\eps$ under the root, as
$\sqrt{\hat{v} + \eps}$, and the difference is small without being zero. This
is not a case where one of the two forms is a typographical error. Read the
line the paper actually printed \dash{} and if you are reimplementing, read the
reference implementation too, because the paper's own line is the authority for
the paper and neither is the authority for the library you happen to be using.
\end{note}
\end{fr}

\section{Multiplying out}
\label{sec:F02-expanding}
\index{algebra!expanding}
\index{algebra!difference of two squares}

\begin{fr}
Removing a bracket is the move you will make more often than any other, and one
rule is behind all of it:
\[ a(b + c) = ab + ac \]
It is not an axiom to be swallowed. Count a rectangle. One that is $a$ high and
$b + c$ wide has area $a(b+c)$; cut it along the join and it is two rectangles,
one $a$ by $b$ and one $a$ by $c$, with areas $ab$ and $ac$. The same region,
counted two ways.

What the sign does comes along for nothing: $a(b - c) = ab - ac$, because
taking $c$ off the width takes $ac$ off the area.

Expand: $3(2x + 5) = \blank$ and $x(x - 4) = \blank$
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$3(2x + 5) = 6x + 15$ \quad and \quad $x(x - 4) = x^{2} - 4x$.

In the second, $x$ multiplied by $x$ gives $x^{2}$: the indices add, exactly as
they did in \S\ref{sec:F02-terms}.
\end{ansblock}

Two brackets multiplied together are the same rule applied twice. Take the
first bracket as a single quantity, distribute it, then distribute again:
\[ (a + b)(c + d) = a(c + d) + b(c + d) = ac + ad + bc + bd \]
\textbf{Two brackets of two terms give four products, one from each cell of a
$2 \times 2$ grid.} Not three and not two \dash{} four, every time, and the
grid is how you know you have them all.

Worked. $(x + 2)(x + 3)$ gives $x \times x = x^{2}$, then $x \times 3 = 3x$,
then $2 \times x = 2x$, then $2 \times 3 = 6$. Collect the two like terms in
the middle and it is $x^{2} + 5x + 6$.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Cover the rest of the page.

\textbf{Write down the expansion of $(a + b)^{2}$. One line, no working, as
fast as you can do it.}
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
If you wrote $a^{2} + b^{2}$, you are in the majority, and this is the trap the
program was built around.

Check it. Take $a = 3$ and $b = 4$:
\[ (3 + 4)^{2} = 7^{2} = 49 \qquad \text{against} \qquad
   3^{2} + 4^{2} = 9 + 16 = 25 \]
The gap is $24$, it is not a rounding difference, and it is $2ab$.
\end{ansblock}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{$(a+b)^{2} = a^{2} + 2ab + b^{2}$.} The square of a sum carries a cross
term, and the cross term is usually the biggest part of it.

The reasoning that produces the wrong answer is a true rule generalised past
its hypothesis. An index \emph{does} distribute over a product and over a
quotient: $(ab)^{2} = a^{2}b^{2}$ is Program~\ref{prog:F01}'s own law and it is
correct. Over anything else it does not. A sum is not a product, and the grid
shows precisely what the wrong rule discards \dash{} it keeps the two corner
cells and deletes the two edge cells.

You have met this once already, as
$\sqrt{a + b} \ne \sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b}$ in Program~\ref{prog:F01}, which handed
it forward to here. Roots, squares and every other index behave the same way
about a sum.
\end{trapbox}
The sign version needs no separate act of memory:
\[ (a - b)^{2} = a^{2} - 2ab + b^{2} \]
Expand $(x - 5)^{2}$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{f02-expansion-grid}{%
  The four products of $(a+b)(c+d)$, sorted into two corner cells and two edge
  cells, and what becomes of the edges in the two cases worth knowing by sight.
  The wrong rule is the one that keeps the corners and throws the edges away.}{%
  The two-by-two product grid, its corner cells and its edge cells.}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$x^{2} - 10x + 25$}
Now the one case in which the cross terms really do vanish, and it is worth
knowing by sight:
\[ (a + b)(a - b) = a^{2} - ab + ab - b^{2} = a^{2} - b^{2} \]
The two middle products are equal and opposite, so they cancel. This is the
\emph{difference of two squares}, and it is why the wrong rule survives contact
with experience: every reader has met a case that looked like
$(a+b)^{2} = a^{2} + b^{2}$ and came out right. It was this one, and it is a
different expression.

Expand $(2x + 3)(2x - 3)$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$4x^{2} - 9$}
Here is why the afternoon was worth spending. The squared error \dash{} the
loss under a great many of the models ever fitted \dash{} is
\[ L = (y - \hat{y})^{2} = y^{2} - 2y\hat{y} + \hat{y}^{2} \]
Look at what the middle term does. $y^{2}$ knows only about the target and
$\hat{y}^{2}$ knows only about the prediction; \textbf{the cross term
$-2y\hat{y}$ is the only part of the loss that knows about both.} Delete it and
the loss stops depending on the relationship between what you predicted and
what was true, which is the whole of its job.

Take $y = \val{f02.err.y}$ and $\hat{y} = \val{f02.err.yhat}$. Compute the loss
properly, then compute the trap's version, $y^{2} + \hat{y}^{2}$.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Properly, $(y - \hat{y})^{2} = \val{f02.sq.correct}$.

The trap's version gives $\val{f02.sq.wrong}$, more than three times as much.
What separates them is the cross term on its own,
$2y\hat{y} = \val{f02.sq.cross}$, which is larger than the loss it was dropped
from.
\end{ansblock}

\begin{aibox}
This shape returns, with the letters changed, more often than any other in the
book.

The squared length of a sum of two vectors is
$\norm{a + b}^{2} = \norm{a}^{2} + 2\,a \cdot b + \norm{b}^{2}$, and the cross
term there is an inner product measuring how far the two point the same way
(Program~\ref{prog:P05}). The variance of a sum of two random quantities breaks
into the same three pieces, and the cross term there is the covariance
(Program~\ref{prog:P24}). Both are $(a+b)^{2}$ with a different multiplication
underneath.

So whenever an argument about embeddings or about noise quietly drops a cross
term, it is asserting that two things are unrelated. Sometimes they are. It is
never free.
\end{aibox}

\yourturn
Expand $(3a - 2b)^{2}$ and $\left(x + \frac{1}{x}\right)^{2}$.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$(3a - 2b)^{2} = 9a^{2} - 12ab + 4b^{2}$. The coefficients get squared too:
$(3a)^{2} = 9a^{2}$, and the cross term is $2 \times 3a \times 2b = 12ab$.

$\left(x + \frac{1}{x}\right)^{2} = x^{2} + 2 + \frac{1}{x^{2}}$. Here the
cross term is $2 \times x \times \frac{1}{x} = 2$, which is where the constant
in the middle comes from.
\end{ansblock}

Three products are worth knowing by sight rather than deriving, because you
will meet them constantly:
\[ (a+b)^{2} = a^{2} + 2ab + b^{2} \]
\[ (a-b)^{2} = a^{2} - 2ab + b^{2} \]
\[ (a+b)(a-b) = a^{2} - b^{2} \]
\end{fr}

\section{Factorising}
\label{sec:F02-factorising}
\index{algebra!factorising}
\index{algebra!common factor}

\begin{fr}
Factorising is the last section read backwards. Where expanding turns
$3(2x + 3)$ into $6x + 9$, factorising turns $6x + 9$ back into $3(2x + 3)$,
and the move is always the same: find what every term has in common and take
it outside.

In $6x + 9$ both terms divide by $3$ and nothing larger divides both, so the
$3$ comes out and what stays inside is what each term becomes after the
division.

A letter comes out on the same terms. In $x^{2} + 5x$ both terms carry an $x$,
so it is $x(x + 5)$.

Factorise $4x^{2} - 6x$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$2x(2x - 3)$}
The common factor is $2x$: the $2$ divides both coefficients and the $x$ is in
both terms. Taking out only the $2$, or only the $x$, is not wrong \dash{} it
is unfinished, and \enquote{fully factorised} means there is nothing left to
take.

The difference of two squares runs backwards as well, and it is the one pattern
worth recognising on sight: anything of the form $a^{2} - b^{2}$ is
$(a+b)(a-b)$, so $x^{2} - 9$ is $(x+3)(x-3)$.

There is a point here worth rather more than the technique. \textbf{A factored
form is the same value computed with fewer operations.} Evaluating
$x^{2} + 5x + 6$ takes two multiplications and two additions; evaluating
$(x+2)(x+3)$ takes one multiplication and two additions. Identical value, less
work.

Be exact about what the saving is measured against, though. Horner's
rearrangement of the same polynomial, $x(x + 5) + 6$, is also one
multiplication and two additions, and it factorises nothing. So what
factorising beats here is the \emph{naive} expanded form and not the best one,
and the transferable habit is the one underneath both: count the operations
rather than trust the shape.

Factorise $x^{2} - 49$, and then $9y^{2} - 1$.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$x^{2} - 49 = (x + 7)(x - 7)$, because $49 = 7^{2}$.

$9y^{2} - 1 = (3y + 1)(3y - 1)$, because $9y^{2} = (3y)^{2}$ and $1 = 1^{2}$.
A coefficient that is itself a square is what makes this one visible.
\end{ansblock}

\begin{aibox}
Pulling a common factor out of a sum is what lets a constant escape from a
mean,
\[ \frac{1}{n}(cx_{1} + \dots + cx_{n})
   = c \cdot \frac{1}{n}(x_{1} + \dots + x_{n}) \]
which is why scaling every example scales the loss and leaves the argument that
minimises it alone (Program~\ref{prog:F05}).

And the smallest example in the book pays the largest dividend. The derivative
of the logistic function factorises to $\sigma(x)\bigl(1 - \sigma(x)\bigr)$, so
a backward pass through it costs one multiplication of a value you already
have, rather than a fresh exponential. Programs~\ref{prog:F07}
and~\ref{prog:F12} derive that; the reason it is cheap is this section.
\end{aibox}

\yourturn
Factorise $2x^{2} + 8x$, and then $t^{2} - 16$.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$2x^{2} + 8x = 2x(x + 4)$.

$t^{2} - 16 = (t + 4)(t - 4)$.
\end{ansblock}

\begin{note}
This book drills factorisation no further than that. A common factor and a
difference of two squares between them cover what the later programs actually
need. The general quadratic is not solved anywhere in this book, and that is
a decision rather than an oversight: nothing later in it requires one, and a
formula you will look up when you need it is not worth an afternoon here.

If your schooling included hours of factorising trinomials by inspection, you
have lost nothing by not repeating them here.
\end{note}
\end{fr}

\section{Algebraic fractions, and what may be cancelled}
\label{sec:F02-fractions}
\index{algebra!fractions}
\index{troubleshooting!cancelling a term}

\begin{fr}
A fraction with letters in it obeys exactly the arithmetic you already have.
Nothing new is introduced in this section. What is new is how easy it becomes
to break a rule you would never break on numbers.

Multiplication multiplies tops and bottoms:
\[ \frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d} = \frac{ac}{bd} \]
Division multiplies by the reciprocal:
\[ \frac{a}{b} \div \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a}{b} \times \frac{d}{c}
   = \frac{ad}{bc} \]
Simplify $\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{b}{c}$, and then
$\frac{x}{2} \div \frac{x}{6}$.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{b}{c} = \frac{ab}{bc} = \frac{a}{c}$. The $b$
goes because it is a factor of the top and a factor of the bottom.

$\frac{x}{2} \div \frac{x}{6} = \frac{x}{2} \times \frac{6}{x}
= \frac{6x}{2x} = 3$.
\end{ansblock}

Addition is the operation that asks for something first. Two fractions can be
added only once they stand over the same denominator:
\[ \frac{a}{c} + \frac{b}{c} = \frac{a + b}{c} \]
and where the denominators differ you make them agree, by multiplying each
fraction top and bottom by whatever the other one has and it has not.

That demand is not new either. Program~\ref{prog:F01} said you cannot add two
numbers in standard form until they share an exponent. It is the same shape:
addition needs its two operands expressed in the same units, and a denominator
is a unit.

Combine $\frac{1}{x} + \frac{1}{y}$ into one fraction.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\dfrac{y + x}{xy}$}
Multiply the first by $\frac{y}{y}$ and the second by $\frac{x}{x}$. Both of
those are $1$, so nothing has changed except how it is written.

Now cover the rest of the page.

\textbf{Simplify $\frac{a + b}{a}$. Write your answer down before you read
on.}
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
If you wrote $b$, you cancelled the $a$ against the $a$, and this is the second
of the two errors this program exists to remove.

Check it. Take $a = 2$ and $b = 6$:
\[ \frac{2 + 6}{2} = \frac{8}{2} = 4 \qquad \text{against} \qquad b = 6 \]
\end{ansblock}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Cancelling is dividing the top and the bottom by a common
\emph{factor}.} A factor is something the whole of the top is multiplied by. In
$\frac{ab}{a}$ the top is $a$ times $b$, so $a$ is a factor and it goes. In
$\frac{a + b}{a}$ the top is $a$ \emph{plus} $b$: the $a$ is a term, it is not
a factor of the whole, and it stays.

What produced the error is that your eye matched a letter rather than the role
the letter was playing. Both expressions carry an $a$ on the top and an $a$ on
the bottom; only one of them carries it as a factor.

The honest simplification splits the fraction instead:
\[ \frac{a + b}{a} = \frac{a}{a} + \frac{b}{a} = 1 + \frac{b}{a} \]
\end{trapbox}
Does $\frac{x^{2} + x}{x}$ equal $x + 1$?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Yes \dash{} here $x$ really is a factor of both terms.}
$x^{2} + x = x(x + 1)$, so the top is $x$ times something and the $x$ goes
honestly. This is the same near miss as the difference of two squares in
\S\ref{sec:F02-expanding}: a wrong rule survives because the reader has met a
case where it appeared to work, and in that case the letter happened to be a
factor after all.

\begin{aibox}
A softmax divides each exponential by the sum of all of them:
\[ p_{i} = \frac{e^{z_{i}}}{e^{z_{1}} + e^{z_{2}} + \dots + e^{z_{n}}} \]
The denominator is a \emph{sum}, so no single exponential cancels out of it.
That is this section's rule, and it is why a softmax cannot be computed one
entry at a time and why the whole row must be summed before any one probability
is known. It is also why the numbers in that row are not independent of one
another, which is what Programs~\ref{prog:F07} and~\ref{prog:P18} are about.
\end{aibox}

\yourturn
Simplify $\frac{3x^{2} - 6x}{3x}$, and then say whether
$\frac{x + 3}{x + 5}$ simplifies.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$\frac{3x^{2} - 6x}{3x} = \frac{3x(x - 2)}{3x} = x - 2$. Factorise the top
first and the common factor becomes visible; cancelling before factorising is
how the previous trap gets made.

$\frac{x + 3}{x + 5}$ does not simplify. Neither $x$ nor $3$ nor $5$ is a
factor of both top and bottom, and it is already as short as it goes.
\end{ansblock}

One line to carry out of this section: \textbf{cancel a factor, never a term.}
\end{fr}

\section{Changing the subject}
\label{sec:F02-rearranging}
\index{algebra!rearranging}
\index{troubleshooting!sign lost under a root}

\begin{fr}
An equation is a balance. Whatever you do to one side you do to the other, and
the equation stays true. That is the only rule in this section; everything else
follows from it.

Solving means undoing, in reverse order. To read $3x + 7 = 22$ forwards you
multiply by $3$ and then add $7$, so to undo it you take the $7$ away first and
divide by the $3$ second \dash{} the last thing done is the first thing undone.
\[ 3x + 7 = 22 \quad\longrightarrow\quad 3x = 15
   \quad\longrightarrow\quad x = 5 \]
Then check by substituting: $3 \times 5 + 7 = 22$. The check costs five seconds
and it is the reason the method is safe.

Solve $5x - 4 = 26$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$x = 6$}
From $5x - 4 = 26$ comes $5x = 30$ and then $x = 6$, and
$5 \times 6 - 4 = 26$ confirms it.

Now the same moves with letters instead of numbers. \emph{Changing the subject}
means rearranging a formula until the quantity you want stands alone on the
left. Nothing new is needed: the balance rule does not care whether the thing
being moved is a digit or a letter.

Start from the line this program opened with, $y = wx + b$, and make $x$ the
subject.

\begin{itemize}
\item Subtract $b$ from both sides: $\quad y - b = wx$.
\item Divide both sides by $w$: $\quad \frac{y - b}{w} = x$.
\item Read the equation the other way round:
  $\quad x = \frac{y - b}{w}$.
\end{itemize}

Two moves and a reading. Each move is one operation applied to the whole of
both sides, and that is what makes the chain auditable: you can point at the
step where any particular thing happened.
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{f02-rearrange-moves}{%
  One rearrangement as a chain of legal moves, with the inverse operation on
  each edge. The condition is acquired at the division and at no other step,
  which is the point: a rearranged formula picks up its conditions along the
  way rather than carrying them from the start.}{%
  Making $x$ the subject of $y = wx + b$, one node per legal move, with the
  condition attached to the step that introduces it.}

\begin{fr}
Look at what that rearrangement picked up on the way.

$y = wx + b$ is perfectly well defined when $w = 0$. It says $y = b$, which is
a dull model and a legal one. But $x = \frac{y - b}{w}$ is not defined there
at all, and nothing in the printed line records where the ban came from.

\textbf{A rearranged formula carries conditions the original did not need.}
They are acquired at the step that introduces them \dash{} here, at the
division \dash{} and if you do not write them down at that step, nobody
downstream can recover them. The formula on its own and the formula together
with $w \ne 0$ are two different statements, and only the second one is true.

Make $x$ the subject of $y = \frac{x - m}{s}$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$x = sy + m$}
Multiply both sides by $s$, then add $m$. As for conditions: the original
already needed $s \ne 0$ in order to be written down at all, so this
rearrangement acquired nothing new. That happens too, and it is worth noticing
when it does.

Now cover the rest of the page. The squared error again:
\[ L = (y - \hat{y})^{2} \]
\textbf{Make $\hat{y}$ the subject. Write it down before you read on.}
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
If you wrote $\hat{y} = y - \sqrt{L}$, the algebra is right and the answer is
incomplete. A square root has two roots:
\[ \hat{y} = y \pm \sqrt{L} \]
A loss of $\val{f02.sq.correct}$ is consistent with a prediction $\num{2.5}$
above the target and with one $\num{2.5}$ below it, and the loss cannot tell
you which of the two you have.
\end{ansblock}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Squaring threw a sign away, and no rearrangement will get it back.}
$(-3)^{2}$ and $3^{2}$ are both $9$, so squaring is not reversible: two inputs
went to one output, and running it backwards has to hand you both.

This is not a curiosity about roots. It is why \textbf{a loss value tells you
how far wrong you are and never in which direction}, and therefore why no
training procedure has ever been built out of loss values alone. What carries
the direction is the \emph{gradient} \dash{} the derivative of the loss, which
for this loss with respect to the prediction is $-2(y - \hat{y})$ and keeps
exactly the sign the square
destroyed. Programs~\ref{prog:F11} and~\ref{prog:P15} take that apart.
\end{trapbox}
Here is a rearrangement with a number at the end of it.
Program~\ref{prog:F01} computed memory from a parameter count with
\[ \text{bytes} = \text{parameters} \times \text{bytes per parameter} \]
Rearrange it for the parameter count, then say how many parameters fit in
$\val{f02.budget.gb}$~GB at two bytes each.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Divide both sides by the bytes per parameter:
\[ \text{parameters} = \frac{\text{bytes}}{\text{bytes per parameter}} \]
$\val{f02.budget.gb}$~GB is $\num{2.4e10}$ bytes, and at two bytes each that is
$\val{f02.params.in.budget}$ parameters \dash{} weights alone, with nothing
else loaded.
\end{ansblock}

That is the promise this program made at the start: \emph{rearrange a formula
so that the thing you want is on the left}. Here is the version of it that
earns the most.

The loss on one example under a language model is $L = -\ln p$, where $p$ is
the probability the model gave to the token that actually occurred. Two moves
make $p$ the subject: negate both sides, then undo the logarithm.
\[ L = -\ln p \quad\longrightarrow\quad -L = \ln p
   \quad\longrightarrow\quad p = e^{-L} \]
The algebra there is this program's. The last step \dash{} that raising $e$ to
a logarithm undoes it \dash{} is Program~\ref{prog:F03}'s, and it is taken on
trust here rather than borrowed quietly. Granting it, a loss of
$\val{f02.loss.nats}$ nats says the model gave probability
$\val{f02.prob.from.loss}$ to what actually happened, which is a far more
readable number than the loss is.

\yourturn
Make $s$ the subject of $z = \frac{x - m}{s}$, and then make $d$ the subject
of $s = \frac{q}{\sqrt{d}}$.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$s = \frac{x - m}{z}$, needing $z \ne 0$ \dash{} a condition the original did
not have.

$s = \frac{q}{\sqrt{d}}$ gives $\sqrt{d} = \frac{q}{s}$ and then
$d = \left(\frac{q}{s}\right)^{2}$, needing $s \ne 0$.
\end{ansblock}

That second one is the attention scale in its scalar form. The
$\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}$ that divides a score in every transformer is this
rearrangement read the other way round; the dot product it is built from is
Programs~\ref{prog:F09} and~\ref{prog:P05}, and the reason the divisor is
$\sqrt{d}$ rather than $d$ or anything else is derived in
Program~\ref{prog:P25} and checked through a whole block in
Program~\ref{prog:P32}.

\begin{note}
The habit that makes all of this safe costs ten seconds. \textbf{Check a
rearrangement by substituting numbers whose answer you already know.} Take
$y = 7$, $w = 3$ and $b = 1$ \dash{} the numbers this program opened with,
where $x$ was $2$. Then $\frac{y - b}{w} = \frac{7 - 1}{3} = 2$, which is the
$x$ you started from. A rearrangement that survives one such check is very
rarely wrong; one that fails it is wrong for certain.
\end{note}
\end{fr}

\section{Reading an equation exactly as printed}
\label{sec:F02-reading}
\index{notation!floor}
\index{troubleshooting!shape one smaller than expected}

\begin{fr}
Everything in this program has been building one skill, and this section names
it.

\textbf{Reading a published formula is a procedure, not an act of
comprehension.} Four steps, in order:

\begin{enumerate}
\item name every symbol, and say which of them are fixed and which vary;
\item find the invisible brackets \dash{} every fraction bar, every root sign,
  every rounding mark;
\item read what is \emph{inside} each of them, and be exact about where each
  one ends;
\item substitute a case whose answer you already know, and check.
\end{enumerate}

None of that is clever. What it is, is written down, and that is the whole
difference between a skill and a knack.

Apply the first three steps to this, the output width of a one-dimensional
convolution:
\[ \left\lfloor \frac{W + 2p - k}{s} \right\rfloor + 1 \]
$W$ is the input width, $k$ the kernel width, $p$ the padding added to each
side, $s$ the stride. The marks $\lfloor \cdot \rfloor$ are a \emph{floor}:
they round down to the integer at or below. The fraction bar groups
$W + 2p - k$ over $s$; the floor groups the whole quotient; the $+ 1$ sits
outside the floor.
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{f02-equation-to-code}{%
  The four steps, applied to the layer-norm line of
  \S\ref{sec:F02-precedence}, with the three wrong flattenings shown as
  dead ends and each labelled with what it actually computes. The method is
  the spine; the branches are what happens without it.}{%
  A printed formula, the four reading steps, and three wrong flattenings.}

\begin{fr}
Step four. Take $W = 7$, $k = 3$, $p = 0$ and $s = 2$: a seven-wide input, a
three-wide kernel, no padding, and the kernel moving two positions at a time.

\textbf{Worked, with gaps.} Take the three stages in the order the brackets
impose them.

The inside of the floor is $(7 + 0 - 3)/2 = \blank$

The floor of that is $\blank$

So the output width, with the $+ 1$ added, is $\blank$
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$2$, \quad $2$, \quad $3$}
$\frac{7 - 3}{2} + 1 = \frac{4}{2} + 1 = 2 + 1 = 3$. The stride divided the
numerator exactly, so the floor had an integer to work on and did nothing at
all, and the formula behaved like a fraction plus one.

Now change one digit and nothing else. Take $W = \val{f02.conv.w}$, with
$k = \val{f02.conv.k}$, $p = \val{f02.conv.p}$ and $s = \val{f02.conv.s}$
exactly as before.

What is the output width now?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
If you answered $\num{3.5}$, the floor was left out, and a shape cannot be
$\num{3.5}$. If you answered $4$, you noticed that and rounded up, which is the
natural repair and the wrong one.
\[ \left\lfloor \frac{8 + 0 - 3}{2} \right\rfloor + 1
   = \left\lfloor \num{2.5} \right\rfloor + 1 = 2 + 1 = 3 \]
The answer is $3$ \dash{} the same as for the narrower input.
\end{ansblock}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{The floor is part of the formula, and it rounds \emph{down}.} The
previous frame is what made you drop it: there the stride divided exactly, the
floor was decoration, and a rule that does nothing on the first case you try is
a rule you stop typing.

The divides-exactly case is the one everybody checks first, and it is the one
case in which the floor cannot be seen at all.

Rounding up is the wrong direction because the floor is not a rounding
convention, it is a count. Every output position needs a whole kernel's worth
of input underneath it, and when the stride does not divide the numerator the
last window has nowhere to land. The trailing input positions are dropped, no
error is raised, and dilation adds a further term inside the numerator that
this program does not teach.
\end{trapbox}
Two questions, then. How many input positions were discarded at that setting?
And what becomes of a $\val{f02.conv.stack.w}$-wide input after three such
layers?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
The count of discarded input positions is $\val{f02.conv.dropped}$. The three
windows start at positions $1$, $3$ and $5$ and reach as far as position $7$;
the eighth is never looked at by anything.

Three layers is where it stops being a curiosity. Start from
$\val{f02.conv.stack.w}$, ignore the floor, and the widths run
$30 \to \num{14.5} \to \num{6.75} \to \num{2.875}$. Round that up at the end,
because a shape has to be an integer, and you expect
$\val{f02.conv.expected.3}$. The floors give $\val{f02.conv.actual.3}$. You are
$\val{f02.conv.gap.3}$ short.
\end{ansblock}

\begin{warning}
Nothing raises. The mismatch surfaces at whatever operation first needs two
shapes to agree, which may be several layers downstream and inside a module
somebody else wrote, and there it reads as a bug in that module. This is the
standard place people lose an element, and it is why Dumoulin and Visin wrote a
whole guide to convolution arithmetic.

The habit is cheap: compute the shape at every layer before you run anything,
and check it against what the framework reports. Program~\ref{prog:P03} sizes a
network this way, and Program~\ref{prog:P07} turns shape checking into a
discipline.
\end{warning}

Last one, and you get no help with it. Read the optimiser update as printed:
\[ \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \, \frac{\hat{m}}{\sqrt{\hat{v}} + \eps} \]
Name the symbols; supply the brackets; and say what changes if the $\eps$ is
read as sitting outside the divisor, at $\eta = \val{f02.adam.eta}$,
$\hat{m} = \val{f02.adam.mhat}$, $\hat{v} = \val{f02.adam.vhat}$ and

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$\theta$ is the parameter, $\eta$ the learning rate, $\hat{m}$ and $\hat{v}$
the running estimates of the gradient and of its square, $\eps$ the guard. The
brackets: the fraction bar groups $\hat{m}$ over the whole of
$\sqrt{\hat{v}} + \eps$, and the root covers $\hat{v}$ and nothing else.
\begin{center}
\code{eta * mhat / (sqrt(vhat) + eps)}
\end{center}
At those values $\sqrt{\hat{v}}$ lands exactly on $\eps$, so the printed line
halves the step and gives $\val{f02.adam.correct}$. Read with the $\eps$ added
after the division instead, it gives $\val{f02.adam.wrong.prec}$, a factor of
$\val{f02.adam.ratio}$ larger \dash{} and, worse than the factor, a step with
no upper bound at all as $\hat{v}$ goes to zero. What the misreading removes is
exactly the guard.
\end{ansblock}

That is the program in one sentence. \textbf{A printed equation is an
expression with its brackets left out and its conditions left unsaid, and you
restore both before you type it.}

\begin{summarybox}
\sumitem{1--3}{\result{A symbol stands for a quantity you have not fixed. An
  \emph{expression} computes, an \emph{equation} claims something about one
  value, an \emph{identity} is true for every value \dash{} and only an
  identity may be substituted freely}.}
\sumitem{4--5}{\result{A bracketed superscript is a \emph{label}, not a power:
  $W^{(2)}$ is a layer and $W^{2}$ is a square. Substitute in brackets, or
  $3x^{2}$ at $x = -2$ becomes $3-2^{2}$}.}
\sumitem{7--10}{\result{Terms collect only when they carry the same letters at the same
  powers, and the sign belongs to the term after it. Unlike terms do not
  collapse \dash{} which an array library will not tell you}.}
\sumitem{11}{\result{Adding terms and multiplying them are different operations:
  $3x + 4x = 7x$, but $3x \times 4x = 12x^{2}$}.}
\sumitem{13--15}{\result{Brackets, indices, multiply and divide, add and subtract. The
  index binds tighter than the sign in front of it, so $-3^{2} = -9$ while
  $(-3)^{2} = 9$, and your language agrees}.}
\sumitem{16--17}{\result{A printed fraction bar and a printed root sign are
  \emph{brackets that were never printed}. Flattening to one line means
  supplying them}.}
\sumitem{18--19}{\result{Two real lines turn on that: layer normalisation, where the
  $\eps$ is inside the root, and the Adam update, where the $\eps$ is inside
  the divisor}.}
\sumitem{20--21}{\result{$a(b+c) = ab + ac$, and two brackets of two terms give
  \emph{four} products \dash{} one per cell of a $2 \times 2$ grid}.}
\sumitem{22--23}{\result{$(a+b)^{2} = a^{2} + 2ab + b^{2}$. An index distributes over a
  product or a quotient, and over nothing else. Check at $a = 3$, $b = 4$:
  $49$, not $25$}.}
\sumitem{24--26}{\result{$(a+b)(a-b) = a^{2} - b^{2}$ is the one case where the cross
  terms cancel. In $(y - \hat{y})^{2}$ the cross term is the only part of the
  loss that knows about both quantities}.}
\sumitem{28--29}{\result{Factorising is expanding backwards, and a factored form is the
  same value computed with fewer operations}.}
\sumitem{33--35}{\result{Adding fractions needs a common denominator. Cancelling
  removes a common \emph{factor}: $\frac{a+b}{a} = 1 + \frac{b}{a}$, never
  $b$. Cancel a factor, never a term}.}
\sumitem{38--40}{\result{Rearranging is the balance rule applied one operation at a
  time, undoing in reverse order \dash{} and a rearranged formula acquires
  conditions the original did not need, at the step that introduces them}.}
\sumitem{41--43}{\result{$L = (y - \hat{y})^{2}$ gives $\hat{y} = y \pm \sqrt{L}$: a
  loss says how far and never which side. $L = -\ln p$ gives $p = e^{-L}$}.}
\sumitem{45--48}{\result{Read a formula in four steps: name the symbols, find the
  invisible brackets, read what is inside them, substitute a case you can
  check. The convolution output width
  $\left\lfloor \frac{W + 2p - k}{s} \right\rfloor + 1$ has a floor in it, the
  floor rounds down, and where the stride does not divide, input positions are
  discarded in silence}.}
\end{summarybox}

\begin{testexercises}
\item Evaluate $2a - 3b$ at $a = 4$ and $b = -2$.
  \answerto{$8 - (-6) = 14$.}
\item Simplify $7x - 3y - 2x + 5y$.
  \answerto{$5x + 2y$.}
\item Simplify $3a^{2} \times 4a^{5}$.
  \answerto{$12a^{7}$. Coefficients multiply, indices add.}
\item Evaluate $10 - 2 \times 3^{2}$.
  \answerto{$10 - 18 = -8$. The index first, then the multiplication.}
\item Write the printed $\frac{p - q}{\sqrt{r + 1}}$ as one line of code.
  \answerto{\code{(p - q) / sqrt(r + 1)}.}
\item Expand $(x + 4)(x - 2)$.
  \answerto{$x^{2} + 2x - 8$.}
\item Expand $(2a + 5)^{2}$.
  \answerto{$4a^{2} + 20a + 25$. The cross term is
  $2 \times 2a \times 5 = 20a$.}
\item Expand $(3x - 1)(3x + 1)$.
  \answerto{$9x^{2} - 1$.}
\item Factorise $5x^{2} + 15x$.
  \answerto{$5x(x + 3)$.}
\item Simplify $\frac{x^{2} - 4x}{x}$.
  \answerto{$x - 4$. The top is $x(x - 4)$, so $x$ is a factor of both.}
\item Make $r$ the subject of $A = \pi r^{2}$, and state what the
  rearrangement needs.
  \answerto{$r = \sqrt{A/\pi}$. It needs $A \ge 0$ for the root to exist, and
  the square root's two values are cut to one by the radius being positive
  \dash{} which is a fact about the problem and not about the algebra.}
\item A convolution has $W = 10$, $k = 4$, $p = 1$ and $s = 3$. What is the
  output width?
  \answerto{$\left\lfloor \frac{10 + 2 - 4}{3} \right\rfloor + 1
  = \left\lfloor \frac{8}{3} \right\rfloor + 1 = 2 + 1 = 3$. The floor is
  doing work here.}
\end{testexercises}

\begin{furtherproblems}
\item Evaluate $x^{2} - 3x + 2$ at $x = -1$.
  \answerto{$1 + 3 + 2 = 6$. Both minus signs matter.}
\item Simplify $2(3x - 1) - 3(x - 4)$.
  \answerto{$6x - 2 - 3x + 12 = 3x + 10$. The second bracket is multiplied by
  $-3$, so both of its terms change sign.}
\item Expand $(a + 2b)(a - 3b)$.
  \answerto{$a^{2} - ab - 6b^{2}$.}
\item Expand $\left(x - \frac{1}{x}\right)^{2}$.
  \answerto{$x^{2} - 2 + \frac{1}{x^{2}}$.}
\item Show that $(a+b)^{2} - (a-b)^{2} = 4ab$.
  \answerto{$(a^{2} + 2ab + b^{2}) - (a^{2} - 2ab + b^{2}) = 4ab$. The squares
  cancel and the two cross terms add.}
\item Factorise $12x^{3} - 8x^{2}$.
  \answerto{$4x^{2}(3x - 2)$. Take the largest common factor, not the first one
  you see.}
\item Factorise $25 - 4t^{2}$.
  \answerto{$(5 + 2t)(5 - 2t)$.}
\item Simplify $\frac{x^{2} - 9}{x + 3}$.
  \answerto{$x - 3$. Factorise the top as $(x+3)(x-3)$ first; there is nothing
  to cancel before you do.}
\item Combine $\frac{2}{a} - \frac{3}{b}$ into one fraction.
  \answerto{$\frac{2b - 3a}{ab}$.}
\item Simplify $\frac{a}{b} \div \frac{c}{d} \times \frac{c}{a}$.
  \answerto{$\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{d}{c} \times \frac{c}{a}
  = \frac{d}{b}$.}
\item Make $t$ the subject of $v = u + at$, and state the condition.
  \answerto{$t = \frac{v - u}{a}$, needing $a \ne 0$. The original needed no
  such thing.}
\item Make $h$ the subject of $V = \pi r^{2} h$.
  \answerto{$h = \frac{V}{\pi r^{2}}$, needing $r \ne 0$.}
\item Make $b$ the subject of $c^{2} = a^{2} + b^{2}$.
  \answerto{$b = \pm\sqrt{c^{2} - a^{2}}$, needing $c^{2} \ge a^{2}$. The
  $\pm$ is the sign the squaring destroyed.}
\item Give two different one-line flattenings of
  $\frac{x - \mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^{2} + \eps}}$ and say what each computes.
  \answerto{As printed:
  \code{(x - mu) / sqrt(sigma2 + eps)}. Without the numerator's bracket:
  \code{x - mu / sqrt(sigma2 + eps)}, which subtracts a quotient from $x$.}
\item A convolution has $W = 32$, $k = 3$, $p = 1$ and $s = 1$. What is the
  output width, and what is the padding for?
  \answerto{$\left\lfloor \frac{32 + 2 - 3}{1} \right\rfloor + 1 = 32$. The
  padding is chosen to make the output the same width as the input.}
\item Now take $W = 32$, $k = 3$, $p = 0$ and $s = 2$. What is the output
  width, and how many input positions are discarded?
  \answerto{$\left\lfloor \frac{29}{2} \right\rfloor + 1 = 14 + 1 = 15$, and
  one input position is discarded: the last window ends at position $31$.}
\item For $y = 10$ and $\hat{y} = 8$, compute $(y - \hat{y})^{2}$ and
  $y^{2} + \hat{y}^{2}$, and say what the difference is made of.
  \answerto{$4$ against $164$. The difference is the cross term,
  $2y\hat{y} = 160$.}
\item If $L = -\ln p$ and the loss doubles, what happens to $p$?
  \answerto{It is squared: $p = e^{-2L} = \left(e^{-L}\right)^{2}$. A loss
  twice as large is a probability squared, not halved.}
\item By what factor do the two readings of $1/2x$ differ?
  \answerto{By $x^{2}$: $\frac{1}{2}x$ divided by $\frac{1}{2x}$ is
  $x^{2}$.}
\item Evaluate $-2^{4}$ and $(-2)^{4}$.
  \answerto{$-16$ and $16$. The index binds before the sign.}
\end{furtherproblems}
