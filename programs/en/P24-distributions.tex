\program{Random variables and distributions}
\label{prog:P24}
\index{random variable}
\index{expectation}

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{1--10}{say what a random variable is, and why the same sample space
  carries many of them}
\outcome{11--17}{use the linearity of expectation, and say what it requires}
\outcome{18--25}{compute a variance by either of its two routes, and read
  $D^{2}(X)$ when you meet it}
\outcome{26--36}{say what a covariance measures and what a correlation adds,
  and what neither of them sees}
\outcome{37--48}{name the distribution a quantity follows from its role rather
  than from its formula}
\outcome{49--64}{say what temperature, top-$k$ and top-$p$ each do to a
  distribution, and which of the three removes tokens}
\end{outcomes}

\begin{quiz}
\item A random variable is written $X$ and its values change from draw to
  draw. What kind of object is it? \teachesat{2--3}
  \answerto{A function. It takes an outcome of the sample space and returns a
  number, and there is nothing random or variable about the function itself:
  the randomness is in which outcome the space hands it.}
\item Is $\Ex[X + Y] = \Ex[X] + \Ex[Y]$ true when $X$ and $Y$ are dependent?
  \teachesat{11--12}
  \answerto{Yes, always, with no condition whatever. That is what makes
  expectation worth having: it passes through sums and through constant
  multiples and through nothing else.}
\item Write the variance of $X$ in two ways. \teachesat{18--20}
  \answerto{$\Var(X) = \Ex[(X - \Ex[X])^{2}]$ by definition, and
  $\Var(X) = \Ex[X^{2}] - (\Ex[X])^{2}$ by expanding the square. They are the
  same number rather than one approximating the other.}
\item Two quantities have correlation exactly zero. Are they independent?
  \teachesat{30--31}
  \answerto{No. A correlation measures the straight-line part of a
  relationship, so it can be zero while one quantity determines the other
  completely.}
\item Raising the sampling temperature makes a model more adventurous. Which
  of temperature, top-$k$ and top-$p$ can make a token impossible?
  \teachesat{50--57}
  \answerto{The two truncations. Temperature reweights every token and leaves
  all of them possible; top-$k$ and top-$p$ set some of the weights to exactly
  zero, and no later temperature brings them back.}
\end{quiz}

\section{A function on the sample space}
\label{sec:P24-rv}
\index{random variable!as a function}
\index{sample space}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:F13} ended on a sentence it refused to finish. It built the
value times the density, accumulated:
\[ \int x\,p(x)\,\mathrm{d}x \]
It said the thing has a name, and then declined to use it, on the grounds that
naming it would need three words the book had not defined.

Program~\ref{prog:P23} supplied two of them. What is the third?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{A random variable}

Program~\ref{prog:P23} gave you a \emph{sample space} and a \emph{probability}
on it. What was missing is the thing being averaged, and it is the subject of
this program.

A \textbf{random variable} is a function. It takes an outcome and returns a
number:
\[ X \colon \Omega \longrightarrow \mathbb{R} \]
and that is the whole definition. Nothing else is required of it.

Two of the words in the name are wrong. Which two, and what is wrong with
them?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
\emph{Random} and \emph{variable}. It is neither.
\end{ansblock}

Almost every later confusion in this part of the book starts here, so both are
worth a frame.

It is not \textbf{random}. The function is fixed, written down, and the same
every time; what varies is which outcome the space hands it.

It is not a \textbf{variable} either, in the sense Program~\ref{prog:F06} uses
that word. It is not an unknown waiting to be solved for. $X$ names a rule,
the way $\sin$ names a rule.

\begin{notationbox}
\textbf{Capital letters for the function, small letters for a value it took.}
$X$ is the rule; $x$ is the number that came out on one particular draw. So
$\Prob(X = x)$ reads \enquote{the probability that the rule returns this
number}, and $\Prob(X = X)$ is not a sentence. The convention is universal and
this book follows it without further comment.
\end{notationbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
A space to work on, and it is one the book already has.
Program~\ref{prog:P21} used an explicit population of
$\val{p24.pop.n}$ numbers:
\[ 3,\; -1,\; 4,\; 1,\; -5,\; 9,\; 2,\; -6,\; 5,\; 8 \]
Draw one of them, every one equally likely. That is a sample space with
$\val{p24.pop.n}$ outcomes and a probability of $\frac{1}{10}$ on each, which
is all of Program~\ref{prog:P23}'s machinery and no more.

Let $X$ be the number drawn. Now write down \emph{two other} random variables
on the same space.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Anything that turns \enquote{which one was drawn} into a number. For instance
$X^{2}$; or $1$ when the number drawn is positive and $0$ otherwise; or the
number of characters it takes to write it down.
\end{ansblock}

\textbf{One space carries as many random variables as you care to define}, and
they all ride on the same draw. That is why the space and the variable have to
be kept apart in your head: the space says what can happen and with what
weight, and a random variable is one question you have decided to ask of it.

The two above are worth naming, because the rest of this program uses them.
Write $Y = X^{2}$, and write $\mathbb{1}$ for the second one \dash{} a function
that is $1$ on the outcomes where something is true and $0$ elsewhere, which is
called an \textbf{indicator}.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Now the summary Program~\ref{prog:F13} built and did not name.

The \textbf{expectation} of $X$ is every value it can take, weighted by how
likely that value is, added up:
\[ \Ex[X] \;=\; \sum_{\omega} \Prob(\omega)\, X(\omega) \]
which on a continuous space is F13's integral with the sum replaced by an
integral and nothing else changed.

The sum runs over the sample space, not over the distinct values. An outcome
carrying a value that another outcome also carries contributes twice, and that
is exactly right: the weights that have to add to one are
Program~\ref{prog:P23}'s, and they are attached to outcomes.

Compute $\Ex[X]$ for the ten numbers above.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p24.ex.x}$}

The ten add to $20$ and each carries a weight of $\frac{1}{10}$.

That is Program~\ref{prog:P21}'s own figure.
\code{code/p24\_distributions.py} reads P21's committed value and fails the
build if the two ever disagree \dash{} the two programs are describing one
population, so they may not come apart about it.

Now something the arithmetic does not settle. Must $\Ex[X]$ be one of the
values $X$ can take?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{No, and usually it is not}

\begin{notationbox}
\textbf{An expectation is a number, not an outcome.} $\Ex[X] = \val{p24.ex.x}$
even though $2$ is one of the ten and would have been the answer whether or
not it was. The expectation of a fair die is $\num{3.5}$, which the die cannot
show. \enquote{The value to expect} is not what the word means.
\end{notationbox}

Here it happens to be one of the ten, which is a coincidence of this
population and not a property of expectations.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The indicator earns its own frame, because it is the whole bridge back to
Program~\ref{prog:P23}.

Let $\mathbb{1}$ be $1$ when the number drawn is positive and $0$ otherwise.
What is $\Ex[\mathbb{1}]$?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\frac{\val{p24.pos.num}}{\val{p24.pop.n}}$}

Seven of the ten are positive. The zero terms contribute nothing and the seven
one-terms each contribute $\frac{1}{10}$, so the expectation is the
probability.

\textbf{The expectation of an indicator is the probability of the event}, and
that single line is why the two programs are one subject rather than two.
Every probability in Program~\ref{prog:P23} is an expectation, and every
expectation is a weighted sum of probabilities. Nothing is being generalised;
the same object is being read the other way round.

\mermaidfig{p24-space-to-number}{The three objects, and which of them the
randomness is in.}{space, function, weight}
\end{fr}

\section{Expectation passes through a sum}
\label{sec:P24-linearity}
\index{expectation!linearity of}

\begin{fr}
One property of the expectation carries almost every derivation in the rest of
this book, and it is the reason the object is worth defining at all:
\[ \Ex[aX + bY] \;=\; a\,\Ex[X] \;+\; b\,\Ex[Y] \]
for any two random variables on the same space and any two constants.

Before using it: does that need $X$ and $Y$ to be independent?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
No. It needs nothing at all.
\end{ansblock}

Most readers answer yes, and the reason the wrong answer is so available is
that the \emph{other} rule people remember does need independence. Keeping the
two apart is worth the next four frames.

The proof is one line and you can do it in your head. Both sides are sums over
the same outcomes with the same weights, so
\[ \sum_{\omega} \Prob(\omega)\,\bigl(aX(\omega) + bY(\omega)\bigr)
   \;=\; a\sum_{\omega} \Prob(\omega)X(\omega)
       + b\sum_{\omega} \Prob(\omega)Y(\omega) \]
which is nothing but rearranging a finite sum. No property of $X$ and $Y$ was
used, because none was available to use.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The demonstration is deliberately run on the most dependent pair available.
Take $X$ and $Y = X^{2}$ from section 1: one \emph{is} a function of the other,
so if any dependence could break linearity this one would.

$\Ex[X]$ is $\val{p24.ex.x}$ and $\Ex[Y]$ is $\val{p24.ex.y}$. What is
$\Ex[X + Y]$?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p24.ex.sum}$}

Which is $\val{p24.ex.x} + \val{p24.ex.y}$, and the script checks it over
fractions at four different pairs of constants rather than at this one.

There is no approximation anywhere in that, and no condition attached to it.
When you see a derivation later in the book split an expectation across a sum
of a hundred terms without a word of justification, this is the frame it is
leaning on.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Now the rule that does need something. Is
\[ \Ex[XY] \;=\; \Ex[X]\,\Ex[Y] \]
true for the same $X$ and $Y = X^{2}$? The two numbers are
$\val{p24.ex.xy}$ and $\val{p24.ex.xtimesy}$.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
No: $\val{p24.ex.xy}$ against $\val{p24.ex.xtimesy}$.
\end{ansblock}

\textbf{Expectation passes through a sum and through a constant multiple, and
through nothing else.} It does not pass through a product, and
Program~\ref{prog:P19} spent a whole program on the fact that it does not pass
through a convex function either. That program had to say it in terms of
\emph{means}, because the expectation had not been defined yet \dash{} the
mean of the squares is at least the square of the mean. With this section's
notation it is $\Ex[f(X)] \ge f(\Ex[X])$ for convex $f$, and it is the same
statement about the same object.

The gap between those two products is not noise and it is not an error. It is
a quantity with a name and a use, and section 4 is about it.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{aibox}
\textbf{Three results already in this book rest on the linearity above, and
none of them mentions it.}

Program~\ref{prog:P07}'s broadcasting identity splits a mean over $n^{2}$
pairs into a mean over $n$ of them plus a cross term. Program~\ref{prog:P21}
shows that the average of every one of its $\val{p21.pop.subsets}$ batches
equals the population mean exactly. Section 5 below turns a probability into a
count of alarms per million requests.

All three are the same step, and all three would need a condition attached if
linearity had one. The reason a training loop can average a loss over a batch
of correlated examples and still be estimating the right quantity is this
frame and no other.
\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\section{Variance, and what it is measured in}
\label{sec:P24-variance}
\index{variance!definition}
\index{variance!two routes to}

\begin{fr}
The expectation says where a distribution sits. It says nothing at all about
how far from there the draws land, and two distributions with the same
expectation can behave entirely differently.

The second summary fixes that. The \textbf{variance} is the expected squared
distance from the expectation:
\[ \Var(X) \;=\; \Ex\bigl[(X - \Ex[X])^{2}\bigr] \]

Compute it for the ten numbers. Their mean is $\val{p24.ex.x}$, so the
distances are
\[ 1,\; 3,\; 2,\; 1,\; 7,\; 7,\; 0,\; 8,\; 3,\; 6 \]
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p24.var.x}$}

The squares are $1, 9, 4, 1, 49, 49, 0, 64, 9, 36$, which add to $222$, and
each carries $\frac{1}{10}$.

That route needs the mean before it can start, so it reads the data twice, and
a machine would rather read it once. Expand the square in the definition and
use the linearity of section 2: what does $\Var(X)$ become?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
\[ \Var(X) \;=\; \Ex[X^{2}] - (\Ex[X])^{2}
   \;=\; \val{p24.ex.y} - \val{p24.ex.x}^{2}
   \;=\; \val{p24.var.x} \]
\end{ansblock}

\textbf{They are the same number and not an approximation of one another},
which the script asserts over fractions. Program~\ref{prog:P02} owns what
happens when a machine computes the second one in floating point on data far
from zero, and the answer there was a variance that came out negative.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{notationbox}
\textbf{$\Var(X)$ and $D^{2}(X)$ are the same thing, and so are $\Ex[X]$ and
$M(X)$.}

This book writes $\Var(X)$ and $\Ex[X]$ throughout, because that is what the
papers and the libraries the reader will actually open write. Polish teaching
materials write $D^{2}(X)$ for the variance and $M(X)$ for the expectation,
and they are current usage rather than a historical curiosity: a reader who
learned them will meet both notations in the same week.

The square in $D^{2}$ is doing the same job as the square in
\enquote{standard deviation squared}: it records that the quantity is in the
units of $X$ \emph{squared}. Appendix~B carries the correspondence, and points
at this frame.
\end{notationbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The units are the next thing, and they are the reason the square root has a
name of its own.

Suppose the ten numbers were times in minutes and somebody re-expresses them
in seconds, so every one is multiplied by $\val{p24.var.scale}$. What happens
to the variance?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{It is multiplied by $\val{p24.var.scale.sq}$}

Because $\Var(aX) = a^{2}\Var(X)$ \dash{} the distances all scale by $a$ and
the definition squares them. The script checks that at four scale factors,
exactly.

So a variance is in units \emph{squared}, which is not a thing anybody can
picture. Its square root is:
\[ \operatorname{sd}(X) = \sqrt{\Var(X)} = \val{p24.sd.x} \]
in the same units as $X$, and that is the number to quote. It is called the
\textbf{standard deviation}, and Programs \ref{prog:P05} and \ref{prog:P21}
have both been using the word \emph{spread} for it, in advance of this
definition.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
That closes a loan. Program~\ref{prog:P21} used the word \emph{variance} two
parts before this one, in a rigour box that gave the reader a single sentence
\dash{} the average squared distance from the mean \dash{} and pointed here.

The definition is now the frames above, applied to P21's own population and
gated against its own committed mean. What P21 did with it \dash{} that the
variance of an average of $B$ draws falls like $1/B$, so the spread falls like
$\sqrt{B}$ \dash{} is a statement about \emph{sums} of random variables, and
that is Program~\ref{prog:P25}'s subject rather than this one's.

It is worth knowing why the book keeps both numbers rather than settling on the
readable one. The standard deviation is what you quote; the variance is what
\emph{adds}, and adding is the property Program~\ref{prog:P25} needs. A root
taken early is a root that has to be undone.

One question before leaving the variance, and most readers get it wrong. The
ten distances from the mean were
\[ 1,\; 3,\; 2,\; 1,\; 7,\; 7,\; 0,\; 8,\; 3,\; 6 \]
which average to $\num{3.8}$. The standard deviation came out
$\val{p24.sd.x}$. Are those two supposed to be the same number?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{No, and the second is always the larger}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{The standard deviation is not the average distance from the mean.}
For the ten numbers the average distance is $\num{3.8}$ and the standard
deviation is $\val{p24.sd.x}$, which is larger. Squaring before averaging
weights the far points more heavily, and the square root does not undo that.

The two are different summaries and the second one is the one every library
reports. If you have been reading it as \enquote{the typical distance from the
middle} you have been reading a number that is systematically bigger than the
one you meant, and the gap widens exactly when the distribution has a tail
\dash{} which is when you were relying on it.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\section{Covariance, correlation, and what neither sees}
\label{sec:P24-covariance}
\index{covariance}
\index{correlation!and covariance}

\begin{fr}
Section 2 left a gap on the page: $\Ex[XY]$ was $\val{p24.ex.xy}$ and
$\Ex[X]\Ex[Y]$ was $\val{p24.ex.xtimesy}$. That gap has a name.

The \textbf{covariance} of two random variables is
\[ \Cov(X, Y) \;=\; \Ex\bigl[(X - \Ex[X])(Y - \Ex[Y])\bigr]
   \;=\; \Ex[XY] - \Ex[X]\,\Ex[Y] \]
and the two forms are equal for the same reason the two forms of the variance
are: expand the product and use linearity.

Set $Y = X$ in it. What do you get?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\Var(X)$}

$\Cov(X, X) = \Ex[(X - \Ex[X])^{2}]$, which is the definition of the variance
verbatim. \textbf{A variance is a covariance of something with itself}, so the
second summary was a special case of the fourth all along, and anything true
of covariances is true of variances.

For the ten numbers and $Y = X^{2}$ the covariance is
$\val{p24.cov.xy}$, exactly, and it is positive: large $X$ goes with large
$X^{2}$ at this end of the range.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
A covariance is hard to read on its own, because its size depends on the size
of the two quantities. Dividing by both spreads fixes that:
\[ \Corr(X, Y) \;=\; \frac{\Cov(X, Y)}
   {\operatorname{sd}(X)\,\operatorname{sd}(Y)} \]
which always lands in $\intcc{-1}{1}$.

The division does more than tidy the number up. A covariance carries the units
of both quantities multiplied together, so one computed from a length and a
price is in units nobody has a name for and cannot be compared with anything
else; dividing by both spreads cancels the units, and correlations from
unrelated pairs of quantities then sit on one scale.

Now the question. $Y$ \emph{is} $X^{2}$: knowing $X$ tells you $Y$ with
certainty, which is as complete as a dependence gets. What is $\Corr(X, Y)$?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p24.corr.xy}$}

About a half, for two quantities where one determines the other exactly.

\textbf{A correlation measures the straight-line part of a relationship and
nothing else.} $X^{2}$ is not a straight-line function of $X$, so most of the
dependence is invisible to the number, and what survives is the part that
happens to look linear over this particular set of ten values.

That is already uncomfortable. The next frame makes it worse.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Take the smallest space that shows it. $Z$ is $-1$, $0$ or $1$, each with
probability $\frac{1}{\val{p24.tri.n}}$, and $W = Z^{2}$.

$W$ is a function of $Z$, so again the dependence is total. What is
$\Cov(Z, W)$?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Exactly $0$}

$\Ex[Z] = 0$ by symmetry, so the covariance is $\Ex[ZW] = \Ex[Z^{3}]$, and the
cubes are $-1$, $0$ and $1$, which also average to zero. Both are exact over
fractions; nothing is being rounded away.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{The correlation is zero, so they are independent} is the
inference this frame exists to break.}

$Z$ and $W$ have correlation exactly zero and $W$ is determined by $Z$. The
test Program~\ref{prog:P23} gave you says it plainly:
$\Prob(W = 0)$ is $\frac{1}{\val{p24.tri.n}}$, and
$\Prob(W = 0 \mid Z = 0)$ is $1$. Being told $Z$ moves the answer about $W$
from a third to a certainty, so they are as far from independent as two
quantities can be.

Independence implies zero correlation. The converse is false, and it is false
in the direction that matters: a correlation matrix full of zeros is not
evidence that a set of features carries no shared information. That is what
Program~\ref{prog:P23} meant when it said a table of pairwise correlations
cannot settle independence, and Program~\ref{prog:P31} builds the quantity
that does see it.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The other half of the reason people misread these two numbers is units.

Go back to the ten and suppose again that they were minutes and are now
seconds, so $X$ becomes $\val{p24.var.scale}X$ while $Y$ is untouched. What
happens to $\Cov(X, Y)$, and what happens to $\Corr(X, Y)$?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
The covariance is multiplied by $\val{p24.var.scale}$. The correlation does
not move.
\end{ansblock}

$\Cov(aX, bY) = ab\,\Cov(X, Y)$, and the two spreads underneath the
correlation pick up exactly the same factors and cancel them. The script
checks both at four pairs of scale factors, and it checks the correlation's
invariance by cross-multiplying rather than by comparing two rounded square
roots, so the claim stays exact.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Reading a covariance matrix as if it were a correlation matrix is
reading your units.}

A covariance carries the units of both quantities, so a matrix of them is
dominated by whichever feature happens to be measured in the largest numbers.
Rescale one column from seconds to milliseconds and its row and column of the
matrix grow by a thousand while nothing about the data has changed. A
correlation matrix has ones down the diagonal and entries in
$\intcc{-1}{1}$ by construction, and is the one to read if the question is
about strength rather than about size.

The trap is not that people do not know the definitions. It is that the two
matrices look identical on screen.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Two debts fall due here, and both are one sentence now that the definitions
exist.

Program~\ref{prog:P07} printed $2\Cov(p, t)$ as the exact excess a shape bug
adds to a reported loss, and declared neither $\Cov$ nor $\Var$. This is what
they were. Its finding reads better with them in hand: the excess is twice the
covariance of the predictions with the targets, and a covariance of exactly
that pair is what training exists to increase, which is why the reported
number gets worse as the model gets better.

Programs \ref{prog:P10} and \ref{prog:P11} use the \textbf{covariance matrix}
$C = \frac{1}{n}X\T X$, whose $(i, j)$ entry is the covariance of feature $i$
with feature $j$. They declared it as a forward reference to this program and
said they needed exactly two facts about it. Which two?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
That it is symmetric, and that it is positive semi-definite.
\end{ansblock}

Both are visible in the definition rather than in this section:
$C = \frac{1}{n}X\T X$ is symmetric by construction, and
$v\T C v = \frac{1}{n}\lVert Xv \rVert^{2}$ is a sum of squares. P10 proved
both where it stood, which is why the forward reference cost it nothing.

\textbf{What those programs were owed was the word and not the facts}, and
that is worth having as a rule: a forward reference whose facts can be proved
locally should be.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{rigourbox}
\textbf{Not proved here: that a correlation is always between $-1$ and $1$.}

It is the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality, which this book does not state
anywhere. What is worth having is the step to it rather than the proof: a
covariance \emph{is} a dot product \dash{} of the two lists of
distances-from-the-mean, with the probabilities as weights \dash{} and
Program~\ref{prog:F09} writes a dot product as
$\lVert a \rVert\, \lVert b \rVert \cos\theta$. A cosine is never outside
$\intcc{-1}{1}$, so a correlation is not either.

The bound is therefore not a fact about statistics. It is
Program~\ref{prog:F09}'s geometry, read on a different space.
\end{rigourbox}

\section{Six distributions, by what they are for}
\label{sec:P24-families}
\index{distribution!six families}

\begin{fr}
A handful of shapes account for almost everything a working engineer meets,
and the useful way to hold them is by the question each one answers rather
than by its formula. Six is enough.

The ordering is deliberate: each of the six answers a question about how the
data was \emph{generated}, so the way to pick one is to describe the process
and see which shape it is, never to fit a curve to a histogram and read off
the closest match. A count that can only be a whole number does not become
continuous because a bell fits it tolerably well.

Start with the smallest. A \textbf{Bernoulli} variable is $1$ with probability
$p$ and $0$ otherwise \dash{} one yes-or-no. It is an indicator, so section 1
already gives you its expectation. What is it, and what is its variance?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$\Ex = p$ and $\Var = p(1-p)$.
\end{ansblock}

The expectation is the indicator frame. For the variance, $X^{2} = X$ when $X$
is only ever $0$ or $1$, so $\Ex[X^{2}] = p$ and the second route gives
$p - p^{2}$ directly.

Worth reading the shape of that: $p(1-p)$ is largest at $p = \num{0.5}$ and
zero at both ends. \textbf{A coin you cannot predict is the noisiest one},
which is obvious once stated and is the reason a class-imbalanced label column
has a small variance and tells you almost nothing.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Count $n$ of them and you have a \textbf{binomial}: how many successes in $n$
independent tries.

Program~\ref{prog:P23}'s detector raises an alarm on
$\frac{\val{p23.alarm.num}}{\val{p23.alarm.den}}$ of requests. Over
$\val{p24.binom.n}$ independent requests the number of alarms is binomial, and
its expectation and variance are $nq$ and $nq(1-q)$ \dash{} which the script
checks against all eight enumerated outcomes rather than quoting.

The useful thing an expectation does here is change the units of the question.
Over $\val{p24.million}$ requests, how many alarms, and how many of those are
real?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$\val{p24.alarms.million}$ alarms, of which $\val{p24.real.million}$ are real.
\end{ansblock}

Those are two expectations, each a probability multiplied by a count, and they
are what a capacity plan is written in: one is a queue of tickets and the
other is the number of incidents that were worth finding.

Divide them and you get $\val{p23.ppv.pct}$ per cent, which is
Program~\ref{prog:P23}'s headline arriving from the other side. The script
gates the division against P23's committed figure. Nothing new was computed
\dash{} a probability was turned into a count, and a count is the form in which
somebody will argue about it.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Third is the one this program is aimed at, and you can name it before it is
defined. A softmax produces $k$ numbers that are never negative and add to
$1$. By Program~\ref{prog:P23}'s rules, what has it produced?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{A probability distribution over $k$ outcomes}

A \textbf{categorical} variable takes one of $k$ values with probabilities
$p_1, \ldots, p_k$ that add to $1$, and that is all a language model's output
layer is.

A Bernoulli is the case $k = 2$, and the binomial counts repeats of one.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Fourth and fifth are a pair, and they answer opposite questions about the same
stream of events: the \textbf{Poisson} and the \textbf{exponential}.

Requests arrive at a steady average rate, independently of one another. One of
the two answers \emph{how many arrived this second} and the other answers
\emph{how long until the next one}. Which is which?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Poisson counts; exponential waits.
\end{ansblock}

The Poisson is a count and takes whole-number values; the exponential is a
duration and takes any positive one. A queue is built out of both.

The one property worth carrying is that the exponential is
\textbf{memoryless}: having waited ten seconds already tells you nothing about
how much longer you will wait. That is why a retry timer drawn from it needs
no state, and why \enquote{it is due} is never true of a Poisson process.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Sixth is the \textbf{Gaussian}, and the brief for this program says to
introduce it by its role rather than its formula, because the formula explains
nothing on first meeting.

Its role is this: it is what you get when a great many small independent
contributions add up, whatever each one of them looked like. That is why it
turns up in measurement error, in initialisation, and in the distribution of a
sum of gradients over a batch \dash{} not because anybody chose it, but
because addition produces it.

Two numbers fix it entirely, and they are the two summaries this program has
already built. Within one standard deviation of the mean lies
$\val{p24.gauss.one}$ of the weight; within two, $\val{p24.gauss.two}$.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{rigourbox}
\textbf{Not proved here: that sums of independent things become Gaussian.}

That is the central limit theorem, and it is Program~\ref{prog:P25}'s, along
with the two facts that make it useful \dash{} that variances of independent
quantities add, and that the spread of an average therefore falls like
$1/\sqrt{n}$.

This program states the role and stops there deliberately. A Gaussian used
because a sum was involved is being used correctly; a Gaussian used because
the data \enquote{looked bell-shaped} is an assumption nobody checked, and the
difference between the two is the theorem.
\end{rigourbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Three quantities, and one question. Which of these is close to Gaussian:
the frequencies of the tokens in a corpus, the latencies of a service, or the
mean of a thousand of those latencies?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Only the third}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{The data are normally distributed} is a claim about a
population, and almost nothing in this field is.}

Token frequencies are not; latencies are not, and their tail is the whole
reason anybody looks at a p99; loss values across a corpus are not, which is
Program~\ref{prog:P19}'s perplexity finding in another form. What \emph{is}
usually near-Gaussian is an \emph{average} of many of them, and that is a
different object with a different variance.

The habit worth acquiring is to ask which of the two a stated normality is
about. \enquote{Latencies are normal} is nearly always false;
\enquote{the mean of a thousand latencies is near-normal} is nearly always
true and is what the tooling is relying on.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\section{Distribution surgery}
\label{sec:P24-surgery}
\index{sampling!temperature}
\index{sampling!top-k and top-p}

\begin{fr}
Everything in this program has been building one sentence, and it is worth
saying flatly: \textbf{sampling a token is a draw from a categorical
distribution}, and temperature, top-$k$ and top-$p$ are three ways of editing
that distribution before the draw.

The distribution is Program~\ref{prog:F05}'s, unchanged and gated against its
committed values: four tokens with scores $2$, $\num{1.5}$, $\num{0.5}$ and
$-1$. At $T = 2$ the softmax gives
\[ \val{f05.sm.t20.p1},\quad \val{f05.sm.t20.p2},\quad
   \val{f05.sm.t20.p3},\quad \val{f05.sm.t20.p4} \]
Those four numbers are the whole object being edited.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Take the three knobs in order, and for each one ask a single question: what
does it \emph{destroy}?

Program~\ref{prog:F05} settled temperature. Dividing every score by $T$ and
re-normalising cannot move the $\argmax$, at any $T$, because dividing by a
positive number keeps the order of a list and so does exponentiating.

So what does temperature change?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
The weights, and the tail most of all. Nothing is destroyed.
\end{ansblock}

Every token that had a positive probability still has one. The ranking is
untouched. What moves is how much of the total sits away from the top: across
Program~\ref{prog:F05}'s three temperatures the least likely token rises more
than $\val{f05.sm.tail.floor}$-fold while the most likely falls by a factor of
$\val{f05.sm.top.ratio}$.

\textbf{Temperature is a reweighting}, and reweighting is reversible: set $T$
back and you have the distribution you started with, exactly. Hold on to that,
because the other two are not like it at all.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\textbf{Top-$k$} keeps the $k$ most likely tokens, sets the rest to zero, and
re-normalises what is left.

At $T = 2$ with $k = \val{p24.topk.k}$, the two survivors are re-weighted to
$\num{0.5622}$ and $\num{0.4378}$. What has been destroyed?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Two tokens, and $\val{p24.topk.del.t20}$ per cent of the distribution's
weight.
\end{ansblock}

Not reduced \dash{} removed. Their probability is exactly zero afterwards, and
nothing downstream can distinguish \enquote{very unlikely} from
\enquote{impossible}, because after the truncation there is no difference.

\textbf{Temperature changes the weights; truncation changes what the
distribution is over.} That is a difference in kind rather than in degree, and
it is why the two knobs cannot be reasoned about as though they were both
dials for adventurousness.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Now the interaction, which is the thing worth taking away from this section.

The same $k = \val{p24.topk.k}$ deletes
$\val{p24.topk.del.t05}$ per cent of the weight at $T = \num{0.5}$ and
$\val{p24.topk.del.t20}$ per cent at $T = 2$. Before reading on: why should
the temperature change how much top-$k$ throws away, when $k$ has not moved?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Because raising the temperature is precisely the operation that moves weight
into the tail, and the tail is what top-$k$ deletes.
\end{ansblock}

A factor of $\val{p24.topk.del.ratio}$, on one distribution, from a knob that
was not touched.

\textbf{So the two settings work against each other.} Raising the temperature
to get variety and then truncating at a small $k$ spends most of what the
temperature bought, and the arithmetic says how much rather than leaving it to
taste. A pair of settings tuned at one temperature does not survive a change
to the other, and nothing in a sampling configuration records that they are
coupled.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\textbf{Top-$p$} is the third, and it is defined differently: keep the most
likely tokens until their weight reaches $p$, drop the rest, re-normalise.

That looks like the same kind of knob. It is not, and one measurement shows
why. With $p = \val{p24.topp.mass}$ fixed, how many of the four tokens survive
at $T = \num{0.5}$, and how many at $T = 2$?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$\val{p24.topp.kept.t05}$ and $\val{p24.topp.kept.t20}$.
\end{ansblock}

\textbf{The number of tokens top-$p$ keeps is an outcome, not a setting.} It is
whatever the distribution makes it, which is exactly the design: on a step
where the model is confident it keeps one or two, and on a step where the model
is unsure it keeps many. Top-$k$ keeps the same count either way.

Over $\val{p24.topp.sweep.n}$ temperatures from $\num{0.05}$ to $3$ the same
$p$ keeps between $\val{p24.topp.sweep.lo}$ and $\val{p24.topp.sweep.hi}$ of
the four, which on a real vocabulary is the difference between one candidate
and thousands.

\transcript{p24-surgery}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
One question remains, and it is the one that makes the categorical view worth
having rather than merely tidy.

Sampling from a distribution needs a source of randomness. There is a way to do
it that uses only an $\argmax$: add independent noise of a particular shape to
each $\ln p_i$ and take the largest.

Which of the two it is decides whether a sampler written that way is a sampler
or a heuristic, and a histogram that looks about right cannot settle it. Note
where the noise goes: onto the $\ln p_i$, never onto the $p_i$. That is the
half people misremember, and it is the half the answer turns on.

Is that an approximation to sampling, or is it sampling?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{It is sampling, exactly}

With $G_i$ drawn independently from the standard \textbf{Gumbel} distribution,
\[ \argmax_i \bigl(\ln p_i + G_i\bigr) \]
has exactly the distribution $p$. Not nearly; exactly. It is called the
Gumbel-max trick, and in a library it is the line
\code{argmax(logits + gumbel\_noise)}.

The two-token case is already in this book. The difference of two independent
Gumbels is logistic, so the first token wins with probability
$\sigma(\ln p_1 - \ln p_2)$ \dash{} and Program~\ref{prog:F07} proved that
$\softmax$ on two scores \emph{is} $\sigma$ of their difference. With
$p_1 = \frac{\val{p24.gumbel.two.num}}{\val{p24.gumbel.two.den}}$ that is
$\frac{7}{10}$ back again.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
For four tokens there is no such shortcut on the page, so the claim has to be
checked some other way. The obvious way is to draw a great many samples and
compare the frequencies with $p$.

Say why this book does not do that here.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Because the claim is \emph{exactness}, and a sample cannot establish it.
\end{ansblock}

A sampled check produces an estimate with its own error bar, and
\enquote{the estimate agreed to within the error bar} is a demonstration that
the trick is approximately right \dash{} which is precisely the reading the
frame above exists to refuse. So the script integrates instead. Integrating the probability that one token's
Gumbel beats every other shifted one reproduces all four probabilities, at all
three temperatures, to better than $10^{-11}$.

\begin{aibox}
\textbf{Where this is actually used.} The trick turns a draw into an
$\argmax$, and an $\argmax$ is differentiable-adjacent in a way a draw is not
\dash{} which is what Program~\ref{prog:P21}'s reparameterised estimator
needed and what the categorical case had been missing. It is also how you
sample $k$ tokens without replacement in one pass, by taking the $k$ largest
perturbed scores instead of the largest one.
\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
And the two halves of this section meet, on a number you can now predict.

At $T = 2$, top-$\val{p24.topk.k}$ deletes $\val{p24.topk.del.t20}$ per cent
of the weight. Suppose instead you add the Gumbel noise first and take the
$\argmax$ of everything, truncating nothing. How often do those same two
tokens win?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p24.topk.del.t20}$ per cent of the time}

The same figure, and it is the theorem rather than a coincidence: Gumbel-max
reproduces $p$ exactly, so the frequency with which a token wins is its
probability, and its probability is what truncation deletes. \textbf{Truncation
does not discourage a token. It removes it}, and the mass it removes is
precisely the frequency you would otherwise have seen.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
That is the program.

A random variable is a function on the space Program~\ref{prog:P23} built. Its
expectation is Program~\ref{prog:F13}'s accumulation with the name attached,
and it passes through sums with no conditions, which is why later derivations
are short. Its variance is the second summary, in units squared, and its
square root is the one to quote. A covariance is a variance of a pair, a
correlation is a covariance with the units divided out, and neither sees a
dependence that is not straight.

What is not here is what happens when you \emph{add} random variables:
variances of independent ones add, averages concentrate, and the rate is
$1/\sqrt{n}$. That is Program~\ref{prog:P25}, and it is where
Program~\ref{prog:P21}'s batch-size arithmetic and the $1/\sqrt{d_k}$ in
attention both come from.

\mermaidfig{p24-three-knobs}{Three edits to one distribution, and which of
them is reversible.}{reweight or delete}
\end{fr}

\begin{summarybox}
\sumitem{1--3}{A \textbf{random variable} is a \emph{function} from the sample
  space to numbers. It is not random and it is not a variable: the randomness
  is in which outcome the space hands it. Capital letter for the rule, small
  letter for a value it took.}
\sumitem{4--5}{One space carries as many random variables as you define, and
  they all ride on the same draw. The space says what can happen; a random
  variable is one question you decided to ask of it.}
\sumitem{6--8}{$\Ex[X] = \sum_{\omega}\Prob(\omega)X(\omega)$, which is
  Program~\ref{prog:F13}'s value-times-weight with a name attached. It is a
  \emph{number} and need not be a value $X$ can take.}
\sumitem{9--10}{\textbf{The expectation of an indicator is the probability of
  the event.} Every probability is an expectation and every expectation is a
  weighted sum of probabilities \dash{} one object read two ways.}
\sumitem{11--14}{$\Ex[aX + bY] = a\Ex[X] + b\Ex[Y]$ \textbf{with no condition
  whatever}, checked on $X$ and $X^{2}$, which are as dependent as two
  variables get. The proof is a rearranged finite sum.}
\sumitem{15--17}{Expectation passes through sums and constant multiples and
  \emph{nothing else}: $\Ex[XY] = \val{p24.ex.xy}$ against
  $\val{p24.ex.xtimesy}$, and Program~\ref{prog:P19} owns the fact that it
  does not pass through a convex function either.}
\sumitem{18--20}{$\Var(X) = \Ex[(X - \Ex[X])^{2}] = \Ex[X^{2}] - (\Ex[X])^{2}$,
  the same number by two routes, $\val{p24.var.x}$ here. The second reads the
  data once, and is the one Program~\ref{prog:P02} shows failing.}
\sumitem{21}{$\Var(X)$ is $D^{2}(X)$ and $\Ex[X]$ is $M(X)$; the square in
  $D^{2}$ records that a variance is in units squared.}
\sumitem{22--23}{$\Var(aX) = a^{2}\Var(X)$, so minutes to seconds multiplies a
  variance by $\val{p24.var.scale.sq}$ and a spread by
  $\val{p24.var.scale}$. Quote the square root, $\val{p24.sd.x}$ here, because
  it is in the units of the quantity.}
\sumitem{24--25}{That repays Program~\ref{prog:P21}, which borrowed the word.
  What P21 did with it \dash{} the variance of an average falling like $1/B$
  \dash{} is about \emph{sums} and belongs to Program~\ref{prog:P25}. And a
  standard deviation is not the average distance from the mean.}
\sumitem{26--27}{$\Cov(X,Y) = \Ex[XY] - \Ex[X]\Ex[Y]$, and
  $\Cov(X,X) = \Var(X)$ \dash{} \textbf{a variance is a covariance of
  something with itself.}}
\sumitem{28--29}{$\Corr$ divides the covariance by both spreads and lands in
  $\intcc{-1}{1}$. For $X$ and $X^{2}$ it is $\val{p24.corr.xy}$, for two
  quantities where one determines the other: \textbf{a correlation sees the
  straight-line part and nothing else.}}
\sumitem{30--31}{$Z$ on $\{-1,0,1\}$ and $W = Z^{2}$ have covariance
  \emph{exactly} zero and complete dependence:
  $\Prob(W = 0) = \frac{1}{\val{p24.tri.n}}$ against
  $\Prob(W = 0 \mid Z = 0) = 1$. Independence gives zero correlation; the
  converse is false.}
\sumitem{32--33}{$\Cov(aX, bY) = ab\Cov(X,Y)$ and the correlation does not
  move, so \textbf{reading a covariance matrix as a correlation matrix is
  reading your units} \dash{} and the two look identical on screen.}
\sumitem{34--36}{Program~\ref{prog:P07}'s $2\Cov(p,t)$ and
  Programs \ref{prog:P10} and \ref{prog:P11}'s covariance matrix were owed the
  word rather than the facts: \textbf{a forward reference whose facts can be
  proved locally should be}. The bound on a correlation is
  Program~\ref{prog:P05}'s geometry on a different space.}
\sumitem{37--38}{\textbf{Bernoulli}: $\Ex = p$, $\Var = p(1-p)$, largest at
  $p = \num{0.5}$. An indicator is a Bernoulli and its expectation is a
  probability, so a rare-class column carries almost no variance.}
\sumitem{39--40}{\textbf{Binomial}: $nq$ and $nq(1-q)$, checked against every
  enumerated outcome. Over $\val{p24.million}$ requests the detector raises
  $\val{p24.alarms.million}$ alarms of which $\val{p24.real.million}$ are
  real, and their ratio is Program~\ref{prog:P23}'s $\val{p23.ppv.pct}$ per
  cent \dash{} a probability turned into the counts people argue about.}
\sumitem{41--42}{\textbf{Categorical} is what a softmax produces, by
  Program~\ref{prog:P23}'s own two rules, and what a token is drawn from.}
\sumitem{43--44}{\textbf{Poisson} counts arrivals in a window and
  \textbf{exponential} times the wait between them; the exponential is
  memoryless, so \enquote{it is due} is never true.}
\sumitem{45--46}{\textbf{Gaussian} by its role: what a sum of many small
  independent contributions becomes. $\val{p24.gauss.one}$ within one spread
  and $\val{p24.gauss.two}$ within two. The theorem is Program~\ref{prog:P25}'s.}
\sumitem{47--48}{Almost nothing in this field is normal \dash{} token
  frequencies are not, latencies are not \dash{} and \emph{averages} of them
  are. Ask which of the two a stated normality is about.}
\sumitem{49--51}{\textbf{Sampling a token is a draw from a categorical
  distribution.} Temperature reweights and cannot move the $\argmax$
  (Program~\ref{prog:F05}); every token stays possible and the edit is
  reversible.}
\sumitem{52--53}{\textbf{Top-$k$ removes.} At $T = 2$ it deletes
  $\val{p24.topk.del.t20}$ per cent of the weight, and afterwards
  \enquote{unlikely} and \enquote{impossible} are the same thing.}
\sumitem{54--55}{The two knobs \textbf{fight}: the same $k$ deletes
  $\val{p24.topk.del.t05}$ per cent at $T = \num{0.5}$ and
  $\val{p24.topk.del.t20}$ at $T = 2$, a factor of
  $\val{p24.topk.del.ratio}$, because temperature moves weight into the tail
  and the tail is what $k$ cuts.}
\sumitem{56--57}{\textbf{Top-$p$ keeps a count that is an outcome}: the same
  $p = \val{p24.topp.mass}$ keeps $\val{p24.topp.kept.t05}$ tokens at
  $T = \num{0.5}$ and $\val{p24.topp.kept.t20}$ at $T = 2$, and between
  $\val{p24.topp.sweep.lo}$ and $\val{p24.topp.sweep.hi}$ over the sweep.}
\sumitem{58--59}{$\argmax(\ln p + \text{Gumbel})$ is \emph{exact} categorical
  sampling, and the two-token case is Program~\ref{prog:F07}'s identity read
  backwards.}
\sumitem{60--61}{The four-token case is \emph{integrated} rather than sampled,
  to better than $10^{-11}$, because a sample can only ever show
  that the trick is approximately right.}
\sumitem{62--63}{The mass top-$k$ deletes and the frequency those tokens would
  have won are \emph{the same number}, and that is the theorem. Truncation does
  not discourage a token; it removes it.}
\sumitem{64}{Not here: what happens when random variables are \emph{added}.
  Variances of independent ones add and averages concentrate at
  $1/\sqrt{n}$ \dash{} Program~\ref{prog:P25}, which is where
  Program~\ref{prog:P21}'s batch arithmetic comes from.}
\end{summarybox}

  \answerto{$Z$ uniform on $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ and $W = Z^{2}$: the correlation is
  exactly zero and $W$ is determined by $Z$. A correlation sees the
  straight-line part only. Ask for the conditional distributions \dash{}
  whether being told one score moves the distribution of the other \dash{}
  which is Program~\ref{prog:P23}'s definition of independence and what
  Program~\ref{prog:P31} measures.}
\item A sampler is configured with a temperature and a top-$k$. The
  temperature is raised to make outputs more varied and nothing much changes.
  Give the most likely explanation in one sentence.
  \answerto{The top-$k$ is deleting most of what the temperature moved into
  the tail. Raising $T$ shifts weight away from the top and top-$k$ then throws
  that weight away, so the two settings work against each other: on this
  program's four tokens, $k = \val{p24.topk.k}$ deletes
  $\val{p24.topk.del.t05}$ per cent of the distribution at $T = \num{0.5}$ and
  $\val{p24.topk.del.t20}$ per cent at $T = 2$.}
\end{testexercises}

\begin{furtherproblems}
\item Show that $\Var(X + c) = \Var(X)$ for any constant $c$, directly from
  the definition.
  \answerto{$\Ex[X + c] = \Ex[X] + c$ by linearity, so each distance
  $(X + c) - \Ex[X + c]$ is $X - \Ex[X]$ unchanged, and the squares and their
  average are unchanged with it. A variance is a statement about spread and a
  shift moves no two points closer together.}
\item Show that $\Var(X + Y) = \Var(X) + \Var(Y) + 2\Cov(X, Y)$, and say what
  has to be true for the variances simply to add.
  \answerto{Expand $\Ex[((X - \Ex X) + (Y - \Ex Y))^{2}]$ and use linearity on
  the three terms; the cross term is twice the covariance by definition. The
  variances add exactly when the covariance is zero, which independence
  guarantees and which can also happen without it \dash{} as
  $Z$ and $Z^{2}$ show. Program~\ref{prog:P25} builds on this.}
\item A Bernoulli variable has variance $p(1-p)$. At what $p$ is it largest,
  and what does that say about a label column in which one class is rare?
  \answerto{At $p = \num{0.5}$, where it is $\num{0.25}$; it falls to zero at
  both ends. A column that is $99$ per cent one class has variance
  $\num{0.0099}$, so almost nothing about a model's output can be explained by
  it \dash{} which is the same arithmetic behind
  Program~\ref{prog:P23}'s point that accuracy on an unbalanced population is
  uninformative.}
\item Program~\ref{prog:F05} shows temperature cannot move the $\argmax$.
  Can top-$k$ or top-$p$ move it? Say why, and then say why that is not the
  reassurance it looks like.
  \answerto{Neither can: both keep the most likely token by construction, so
  the $\argmax$ survives every one of the three knobs. It is not reassurance
  because the $\argmax$ was never the thing being changed \dash{} what changes
  is the set of tokens that can be drawn at all, and reading the top token to
  check a sampling setting is reading the one quantity all three leave alone.}
\item Suppose you apply top-$k$ \emph{after} adding the Gumbel noise rather
  than before. Say what distribution you are now sampling from, and whether
  the two orders can be made to agree.
  \answerto{Truncating first makes the deleted tokens impossible; adding the
  noise first and truncating the perturbed scores leaves them able to win,
  exactly as often as their original probability, because Gumbel-max
  reproduces $p$. So the second order is not a truncated sampler at all
  \dash{} it is the untruncated one with extra steps, and no choice of $k$
  makes them agree except $k$ equal to the vocabulary size.}
\end{furtherproblems}
