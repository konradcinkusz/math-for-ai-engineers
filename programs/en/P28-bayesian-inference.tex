\program[Bayesian inference]{Bayesian inference}
\label{prog:P28}
\index{inference!Bayesian}
\index{posterior}

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{1--5}{put a distribution on a parameter and say what a point estimate
  throws away}
\outcome{6--11}{update a Beta prior with counts, and say what a prior is worth
  in observations}
\outcome{12--18}{read a credible interval, and say how it differs from a
  confidence interval when the two agree}
\outcome{19--26}{compute the probability that one model is better than
  another}
\outcome{27--30}{route traffic under uncertainty, and say what a fixed
  exploration rate assumes}
\outcome{31--37}{check a judge model's stated probability, and price it when
  it is wrong}
\end{outcomes}

\begin{quiz}
\item Program~\ref{prog:P26} finds the parameter that maximises the posterior
  and reports it. Name something that is thrown away. \teachesat{2--3}
  \answerto{Everything except the location: how sure the estimate is, whether
  the maximum was sharp or nearly flat, and any way of carrying the
  uncertainty into a later calculation.}
\item You start from a $\mathrm{Beta}(a, b)$ prior and observe $k$ successes
  in $n$ trials. What is the posterior? \teachesat{6--7}
  \answerto{$\mathrm{Beta}(a + k,\ b + n - k)$. The update adds the counts,
  which is what conjugacy means here.}
\item In what units is a prior's strength measured? \teachesat{7--8}
  \answerto{Observations. A $\mathrm{Beta}(a, b)$ prior is worth exactly
  $a + b$ of them, because the posterior mean is a weighted average of the
  prior mean and the data mean with weights $a+b$ and $n$.}
\item A credible interval and a confidence interval on the same data come out
  nearly identical. Does that mean they say the same thing?
  \teachesat{14--15}
  \answerto{No. One says the parameter is in the interval with probability
  $95$ per cent given the data; the other says the procedure covers the truth
  $95$ per cent of the time. The agreement is a property of this case.}
\item A judge model states $\val{p28.judge.says.pct}$ per cent confidence and
  is right $\val{p28.judge.means.pct}$ per cent of the time. What has it done
  to your odds? \teachesat{31--32}
  \answerto{Multiplied them by about $\val{p28.judge.factor}$ times too much,
  every judgement \dash{} and Program~\ref{prog:P23}'s odds form is a
  multiplication, so the error compounds.}
\end{quiz}

\section{A distribution on the parameter}
\label{sec:P28-distribution}
\index{posterior!as a distribution}
\index{prior}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:P23} put a posterior on two hypotheses \dash{} the machine
is faulty or it is not \dash{} and wrote Bayes' theorem in the form that makes
accumulating evidence a multiplication:

\[ \underbrace{\frac{\Prob(A \mid B)}{\Prob(\bar{A} \mid B)}}_{\text{posterior odds}}
   \;=\;
   \underbrace{\frac{\Prob(A)}{\Prob(\bar{A})}}_{\text{prior odds}}
   \times
   \underbrace{\frac{\Prob(B \mid A)}{\Prob(B \mid \bar{A})}}_{\text{likelihood ratio}} \]

Nothing in the theorem cares that there were two. Program~\ref{prog:P27}
ended by handing over a question it could not answer \dash{} the probability
that model B is better than model A \dash{} and the obstacle there was that
the thing you are uncertain about is not a pair of hypotheses but a
\emph{rate}, somewhere in $\intcc{0}{1}$.

So what has to change?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Put a distribution on the rate, rather than a probability on each of two
hypotheses.
\end{ansblock}

That is the whole of the idea and everything else in this program is
arithmetic. The theorem reads the same:

\[ \underbrace{p(\theta \mid \text{data})}_{\text{posterior}}
   \;\propto\;
   \underbrace{p(\theta)}_{\text{prior}}
   \times
   \underbrace{\Prob(\text{data} \mid \theta)}_{\text{likelihood}} \]

with a \emph{density} on the left and on the right where
Program~\ref{prog:P23} had numbers, which is Program~\ref{prog:F13}'s
distinction between a probability and a probability per unit.

Program~\ref{prog:P26} already went as far as the top of that curve: a MAP
estimate is the $\theta$ that maximises the right-hand side. It then said,
in a warning box, that doing so discards something. Say what.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Everything except the location \dash{} how sure the estimate is, whether the
maximum was sharp or nearly flat, and any way of carrying the uncertainty
forward into a later calculation.
\end{ansblock}

\mermaidfig{p28-point-or-shape}{What a maximum keeps and what it
  discards}{point or shape}

The move this program makes is to keep the whole curve. Everything a reader
wants from an evaluation \dash{} an interval, a probability that one model
beats another, a routing decision \dash{} is then a question you ask of that
curve rather than a separate technique.

The prior has to be a distribution on $\intcc{0}{1}$, because that is where a
rate lives. There are many; one of them makes every question in this program
answerable in closed form. Before reading on, what would you want of it?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
That multiplying it by the likelihood gives something of the same shape
\dash{} so that the answer can be updated again without the algebra growing.
\end{ansblock}

That property has a name, \textbf{conjugacy}, and for a rate observed through
successes and failures the family that has it is the \textbf{Beta}:

\[ p(\theta) \;\propto\; \theta^{a-1}\,(1-\theta)^{b-1},
   \qquad 0 \le \theta \le 1 \]

Two numbers, $a$ and $b$, and section 2 says what they mean in the units of
the problem. Note the shape of that expression, because it is why every figure
in this program is exact: with whole-number $a$ and $b$ the density is a
\textbf{polynomial}, so an area under it is a rational number and
Program~\ref{prog:P12}'s coefficient computes it with no rounding anywhere.

The uniform distribution, which says every rate is equally plausible, is
$\mathrm{Beta}(\val{p28.prior.a}, \val{p28.prior.b})$ \dash{} both exponents
zero, the density flat.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{The prior is arbitrary, so the answer is arbitrary.}}

It is the objection everybody raises and it is answerable rather than
philosophical, which is why this program answers it with a measurement
instead of an argument.

The reason the objection feels strong is that a prior is genuinely a choice
and nothing forces one. What it misses is that the choice has a
\emph{magnitude}, and the magnitude is computable: section 2 shows that a
prior is worth a definite number of observations, and section 3 shows what
that is worth against a real evaluation set. On
$\val{p28.n}$ items it moves the answer by less than one item; on
$\val{p28.small.n}$ it moves it a great deal.

So the question \enquote{does the prior matter here} has an arithmetic answer
every time it is asked, and the argument people have instead is usually being
had about the case where it does not.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\section{The update is adding counts}
\label{sec:P28-conjugacy}
\index{conjugacy}
\index{prior!what it is worth in observations}

\begin{fr}
Take the prior $\mathrm{Beta}(a, b)$ and observe $k$ successes in $n$ trials.
The likelihood of that data at a rate $\theta$ is
$\theta^{k}(1-\theta)^{n-k}$, times a coefficient that does not involve
$\theta$ and therefore cannot affect the shape.

Multiply the two, and read off what you get.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\mathrm{Beta}(a + k,\ b + n - k)$}

\[ \theta^{a-1}(1-\theta)^{b-1} \times \theta^{k}(1-\theta)^{n-k}
   \;=\; \theta^{a+k-1}(1-\theta)^{b+n-k-1} \]

which is a Beta again, with the counts added. That is the whole update, it is
one line of index arithmetic from Program~\ref{prog:F01}, and there is no
integral in it.

It also makes the prior's meaning concrete, and this is the sentence worth
taking away from the section. A $\mathrm{Beta}(a, b)$ prior enters the
posterior in exactly the same way $a$ successes and $b$ failures would.

So say what a prior is worth, in the units the problem is actually measured
in.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Observations \dash{} $a + b$ of them}

And it is an identity rather than an analogy. The posterior mean is a
\textbf{weighted average} of the prior mean and the data mean:

\[ \frac{a+k}{a+b+n}
   \;=\;
   \frac{(a+b)\cdot\frac{a}{a+b} \;+\; n\cdot\frac{k}{n}}{(a+b) + n} \]

The script checks that at four priors and four sample sizes over fractions,
because it is the claim the whole of the prior argument rests on.

The uniform prior is therefore worth $\val{p28.prior.worth}$ observations.
Set that against Program~\ref{prog:P27}'s evaluation \dash{}
$\val{p28.k}$ of $\val{p28.n}$ items \dash{} and say what it does to the
answer.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Almost nothing: $\val{p28.post.mean.pct}$ against
$\val{p28.mle.pct}$ per cent}

Two observations against two hundred, so the prior gets a weight of
$\val{p28.prior.worth}$ parts in $\val{p28.n} + \val{p28.prior.worth}$, and
the two answers differ by less than the $\val{p27.grid.pts}$-point grid
Program~\ref{prog:P27} measured. \textbf{On this evaluation set the choice of
prior cannot move the answer by as much as one item.}

\mermaidfig{p28-prior-in-items}{A prior's strength, in the units the problem
  is measured in}{a prior in items}

Now take the data away. The same uniform prior, and an evaluation of
$\val{p28.small.n}$ items on which the model got $\val{p28.small.k}$ right.
What happens to the two numbers?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p28.small.post.pct}$ against $\val{p28.small.mle.pct}$ per cent}

The prior is now worth $\val{p28.prior.worth}$ of the
$\val{p28.small.n} + \val{p28.prior.worth}$ observations in play, and it moves
the answer by nearly three points. A prior asserting more \dash{}
$\mathrm{Beta}(\val{p28.strong}, \val{p28.strong})$, worth
$\val{p28.strong}+\val{p28.strong}$ observations centred on a half \dash{}
pulls it to $\val{p28.strong.post.pct}$ per cent.

So the honest statement is not that priors matter or that they do not.

\begin{center}
\emph{A prior matters in exact proportion to how little data you have,\\
and the proportion is $\frac{a+b}{a+b+n}$, which you can compute.}
\end{center}

That is why the objection in section 1 is answerable: it asks a question with
a number in it, and the number is available before the argument starts.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{I used a uniform prior, so I did not assume anything.}}

A uniform prior is a prior, and it is worth $\val{p28.prior.worth}$
observations rather than none. What it asserts is that a model's success rate
is as likely to lie between $\num{0.01}$ and $\num{0.02}$ as between
$\num{0.50}$ and $\num{0.51}$, which is a strong claim about a system somebody
has already trained and evaluated informally.

That is usually harmless, for the reason the section just measured: at
$\val{p28.n}$ items two observations are lost in the noise. It stops being
harmless exactly where the data is thin \dash{} a rare failure mode, a
sub-benchmark of twenty items, the first hour of an A/B test \dash{} which is
also where people reach for a Bayesian answer because nothing else works.

\textbf{The check is not philosophical.} Compute $\frac{a+b}{a+b+n}$; if it is
small, stop arguing about the prior and read the data.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\section{A credible interval}
\label{sec:P28-credible}
\index{inference!credible interval}

\begin{fr}
A posterior is a distribution, so anything you can ask of a distribution you
can ask of it. The most useful question is the one
Program~\ref{prog:P27} could not answer: \emph{where is the rate, and how
sure am I?}

Cut the posterior at $\num{2.5}$ and $\num{97.5}$ per cent, and the middle
$95$ per cent of it runs from $\val{p28.cred.lo}$ to $\val{p28.cred.hi}$ per
cent. That is a \textbf{credible interval}.

Write down, in one sentence, what you think it claims.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{The rate is between $\val{p28.cred.lo}$ and $\val{p28.cred.hi}$ per cent
with probability $\num{0.95}$, given this data and this prior}

That is the sentence almost everybody wants, and here it is available,
because the posterior is a distribution over the rate and an interval is a
region of it. There is nothing subtle in the derivation: two quantiles of a
Beta.

Program~\ref{prog:P27} §2 built the other kind on the same evaluation and got
$\val{p28.conf.lo}$ to $\val{p28.conf.hi}$ per cent.

Compare the two pairs of numbers. What is the largest difference between
them?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Half a point at the outside \dash{} no larger than the grid one
evaluation item moves the score by}

$\val{p28.cred.lo}$ against $\val{p28.conf.lo}$, and $\val{p28.cred.hi}$
against $\val{p28.conf.hi}$. Program~\ref{prog:P27} §1 measured that this
evaluation cannot resolve anything finer than $\val{p27.grid.pts}$ points,
because that is what one item of $\val{p28.n}$ is worth, and \textbf{neither}
gap is larger than that.

\textbf{So the contrast between the two intervals cannot be made on the
digits.} They agree to better than the instrument can measure, on the case
people usually meet, which is precisely why they are conflated.

It has to be made on what each one claims instead. The credible interval's
sentence is above. What does the confidence interval claim?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Nothing about \emph{this} interval \dash{} it is a statement about the
\emph{procedure}}

A $95$ per cent confidence procedure is one whose intervals contain the true
rate $95$ per cent of the time, over evaluations you did not run. The
interval in front of you either contains the rate or does not; there is no
probability left in it once the numbers are on the page, because nothing in
that framework is random except the data, and the data has been observed.

The credible interval puts the probability on the rate. The confidence
interval puts it on the procedure. Two sentences, two objects, the same six
digits.

\mermaidfig{p28-two-sentences}{The two intervals agree on this evaluation and
  claim different things}{two sentences}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{The $95$ per cent confidence interval is $65$ to $78$, so
there is a $95$ per cent chance the rate is in there.}}

That is the credible interval's sentence attached to the confidence
interval's numbers, and on this evaluation it happens to be nearly true
\dash{} which is what keeps it alive. It is nearly true because the two
intervals agree to half a point here, and they agree because
$\val{p28.n}$ items swamp a prior worth $\val{p28.prior.worth}$.

Where they do not agree, the habit is expensive, and section 2 already said
where that is: thin data. The check is the same one, and it is arithmetic
rather than philosophy \dash{} $\frac{a+b}{a+b+n}$.

\textbf{And notice what the misreading is evidence of.} Nobody misreads a
credible interval as a confidence interval. The traffic is all one way,
because the Bayesian sentence is the one the reader wanted in the first
place.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Take the data away again and the two priors separate, as section 2's
arithmetic says they must. On $\val{p28.small.k}$ of $\val{p28.small.n}$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
prior & credible interval & width \\
\midrule
$\mathrm{Beta}(\val{p28.prior.a}, \val{p28.prior.b})$
  & $\val{p28.small.cred.lo}$ to $\val{p28.small.cred.hi}$
  & $\val{p28.width.small}$ \\
$\mathrm{Beta}(\val{p28.strong}, \val{p28.strong})$
  & $\val{p28.strong.cred.lo}$ to $\val{p28.strong.cred.hi}$ & \\
\midrule
$\val{p28.k}$ of $\val{p28.n}$, uniform prior
  & $\val{p28.cred.lo}$ to $\val{p28.cred.hi}$
  & $\val{p28.width.big}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The first row spans two thirds of everything a rate could be. Which of the
three numbers in that table is the one to put in a report?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{The width}

$\val{p28.width.small}$ points against $\val{p28.width.big}$: the small
evaluation's interval is more than five times wider, and it is wide enough to
contain both \enquote{this model is barely better than a coin} and
\enquote{this model is excellent}.

An interval that wide is not a weak result. It is a \textbf{measurement of the
evaluation} rather than of the model, and it says the evaluation is too
small to have an opinion \dash{} which is the same conclusion
Program~\ref{prog:P27} reached by a different route and stated as an item
count.

\begin{note}
Nothing here says which prior was right. The two disagree by three points on
five items and by nothing at all on two hundred, so on the thin evaluation the
prior is the argument and on the thick one it is not. Reporting the width
first makes that visible without anybody having to raise it.
\end{note}
\end{fr}

\section{The probability that B is better}
\label{sec:P28-better}
\index{inference!model comparison}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:P27} §4 ends by naming a quantity and declining to compute
it. Two models were compared on the same $\val{p28.n}$ prompts;
$\val{p28.disc}$ items were \textbf{discordant}, meaning exactly one model got
them right, and of those $\val{p28.to.b}$ went to B and $\val{p28.to.a}$ to A.
The concordant items cancel exactly and carry no information about the
difference.

A $p$-value answers \emph{how surprising is this under a tie}. The question
everybody actually asked is \emph{how likely is it that B is better}.

Section 2 turns the second into arithmetic the moment you can name the
parameter. What is it here?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{The probability that a discordant item goes to B}

Call it $\theta$. B is better exactly when $\theta > \frac{1}{2}$, because a
discordant item is one the two models disagreed on and $\theta$ is B's share
of them. So the whole comparison is one rate, and section 2 already knows what
to do with a rate and some counts.

Notice what that parameter is conditioned on. It is defined \emph{given} that
the two models disagreed, so the size of the evaluation enters the answer only
through how many disagreements it produced. Two evaluations of very different
sizes that produced the same discordant counts give the same posterior, and
the larger one buys nothing extra \dash{} which is the same statement as
Program~\ref{prog:P27}'s about the concordant items, arrived at from the
definition rather than from the arithmetic.

Start from the same uniform prior. What is the posterior?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\mathrm{Beta}(\val{p28.bpost.a}, \val{p28.bpost.b})$}

$\val{p28.prior.a}+\val{p28.to.b}$ and
$\val{p28.prior.b}+\val{p28.to.a}$, by the rule in section 2 and by nothing
else. And the answer is the posterior mass above a half:

\[ \Prob\bigl(\theta > \tfrac{1}{2} \mid \text{data}\bigr)
   \;=\; \val{p28.p.better.pct}\ \text{per cent} \]

\transcript{p28-how-sure}

That is the quantity Program~\ref{prog:P27} said no $p$-value could supply, on
Program~\ref{prog:P27}'s own data, and it took one line.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:P27} got $p = \val{p28.p27.p}$ on the same items \dash{} the
largest a $p$-value can be, and the least informative outcome its arithmetic
permits.

Two numbers, one dataset: a $p$-value of $\val{p28.p27.p}$ and a
$\val{p28.p.better.pct}$ per cent probability that B is better. Are they in
tension?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{No \dash{} they are answers to different questions, and both are
unsurprising}

\enquote{The data would not be surprising if the two were tied} and
\enquote{B is slightly more likely than not to be ahead} are entirely
compatible statements, and on evidence this thin they are what you should
expect to see together. A single item's lead is worth a nudge, and a nudge is
what $\val{p28.p.better.pct}$ per cent is: it is nearer to a coin flip than to
a finding.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{$p = \val{p28.p27.p}$, so the two models are the same.}}

A $p$-value near one says the data is unremarkable under a tie. It does not say
a tie is true, and it cannot, because it never conditions on the data at all
\dash{} which is Program~\ref{prog:P23}'s inversion, and
Program~\ref{prog:P27} §4 names it as such.

The posterior does condition on the data, and it says B is ahead by a nose.
Both readings are right. The one that reports \emph{no difference} to a
review meeting is neither.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Suppose the same evaluation continues, every new discordant item goes to B,
and you keep asking until the posterior probability reaches $95$ per cent.

Roughly how many more items do you think that takes \dash{} five, ten, fifty,
five hundred?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p28.more.needed}$}

$\val{p28.reach.b}$ of $\val{p28.reach.disc}$ discordant items, and the
posterior crosses $95$ per cent. Ten more observations, every one of them
favourable, to get from \enquote{a nudge} to \enquote{worth acting on}.

Two things fall out of that number and both are worth carrying.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{It is the same size as the frequentist threshold, and that is
  not a coincidence.} Program~\ref{prog:P27} asked a different question of the
  same data \dash{} how lopsided would these $\val{p28.disc}$ items have to
  be before a fair coin would rarely produce them \dash{} and answered
  $\val{p27.net.needed}$. Both are sums of binomial coefficients over
  $2^{m}$, read two ways, so the two frameworks disagree about what the number
  \emph{means} and hardly at all about how much evidence it takes.
\item \textbf{It is a question you can ask before running anything.} The
  posterior after a hypothetical result is computable now, so \enquote{how
  much more evidence would change my mind} has a number, and it is the number
  to put in the plan rather than in the retrospective.
\end{itemize}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The paired comparison is the one to run when the two models saw the same
prompts. When they did not, the same integral does the job with two
parameters instead of one \dash{} a posterior for each model's rate, and the
probability that one draw exceeds the other.

On the same one-item gap \dash{} $\val{p28.unp.b.k}$ against
$\val{p28.unp.a.k}$ out of $\val{p28.unp.n}$ each, unpaired \dash{} it gives
$\val{p28.unp.better.pct}$ per cent against the paired
$\val{p28.p.better.pct}$.

\begin{center}
\emph{The same observed difference, and the paired analysis is more
confident,\\
because it never had to explain the $\val{p28.n} - \val{p28.disc}$ items
both models agreed on.}
\end{center}

That is Program~\ref{prog:P27} §3's whole argument arriving in a posterior
rather than in an item count, and it did not have to be re-derived: pairing is
worth something for the same reason in both frameworks, because the
agreement carries no information about the difference either way.
\end{fr}

\section{Routing under uncertainty}
\label{sec:P28-routing}
\index{inference!Thompson sampling}

\begin{fr}
Section 4 produced a number and stopped. Suppose instead of stopping you have
to keep serving traffic while the evidence accumulates, and you would rather
not send half of it to whichever model is losing.

Here is a rule that needs no threshold and no schedule. \textbf{Draw one
sample from each posterior and route the request to whichever sample came out
larger.}

What fraction of traffic does that send to B?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p28.route.b.pct}$ per cent \dash{} the posterior probability that B
is better}

\emph{By construction}, and that is the whole of the argument: the rule routes
to B exactly when B's draw is larger, and the probability of that event is
$\Prob(\theta_B > \theta_A)$, which is the number section 4 already computed.

So the rule needs no experiment to describe and no parameter to set. It
\textbf{explores in exact proportion to how likely each arm is to be the best
one}, and the proportion is a quantity you were computing anyway.

\mermaidfig{p28-route-by-belief}{Routing by drawing from each posterior}{routing by belief}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
And it corrects itself without being told to. Give B another
$\val{p28.route.more}$ successes and the traffic follows the evidence, from
$\val{p28.route.b.pct}$ to $\val{p28.route.b2.pct}$ per cent.

The usual alternative is the $\varepsilon$-greedy rule: send
$\val{p28.eps.pct}$ per cent of traffic to the arm you currently believe is
worse, whatever the evidence, and the rest to the leader.

That rule needs a second decision nobody states, which is who counts as the
leader. Near a half the label flips on a single observation, so the traffic
split jumps between two fixed values exactly where the evidence is weakest.
The posterior rule has no such discontinuity: it moves a little for a little
evidence, because the share it sends is a probability rather than a label.

Set beside the rule above, what does that fixed fraction assume?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{That the evidence never becomes strong enough to stop paying for it}

The two rules agree while the posterior is near a half, because
$\val{p28.route.b.pct}$ per cent and \enquote{half the traffic, minus a bit}
are the same instruction. They part company as evidence arrives, and the
crossing point is computable rather than a matter of taste: after
$\val{p28.eps.crossover}$ more successes for B the posterior probability that
A is better falls below $\val{p28.eps.pct}$ per cent, and from that point on
the fixed rule is spending more on the worse arm than the evidence supports.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{The exploration rate is a hyperparameter, so I will tune
it.}}

It is a hyperparameter in the $\varepsilon$-greedy rule and it is \emph{not
one} in the rule above, which has no free parameter at all \dash{} the
exploration comes out of the posterior, and the posterior comes out of the
data.

That is worth noticing because of what tuning it would mean. The right
exploration rate depends on how much evidence you have, and how much evidence
you have changes every hour. A constant cannot track it, so tuning
$\varepsilon$ is choosing one point on a curve and then living with the curve
moving underneath it.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\section{A judge model's stated probability}
\label{sec:P28-judge}
\index{inference!calibration}

\begin{fr}
A judge model scores candidate answers and reports a confidence with each
one. On the answers where it says $\val{p28.judge.says.pct}$ per cent, it
turns out to be right $\val{p28.judge.means.pct}$ per cent of the time.

Twenty points is not obviously alarming. Program~\ref{prog:P23}'s odds form
says how bad it is, and it is worth converting both before reading on.

What are the two statements in odds?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p28.judge.odds.says}$ to one against
$\val{p28.judge.odds.means}$ to one}

$\frac{p}{1-p}$ in each case, and the ratio of the two is
$\val{p28.judge.factor}$. \textbf{The judge is overstating the evidence by
nearly a factor of four}, which is a much larger error than twenty points of
probability sounds like \dash{} because a probability near a boundary
compresses, exactly as Program~\ref{prog:P05} and Program~\ref{prog:P21} each
found from their own ends.

That is the first reason to work in odds. Here is the second, and it is the
one that hurts.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:P23} states Bayes in odds as a \textbf{multiplication}:
posterior odds are prior odds times the likelihood ratio, and independent
pieces of evidence multiply.

So a judge whose stated odds are $\val{p28.judge.factor}$ times too large is
wrong by that factor \emph{on every judgement}, and a pipeline that combines
several of them multiplies the errors together.

Over $\val{p28.judge.rounds}$ independent judgements, by what factor are the
combined odds overstated?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p28.judge.compound}$}

$\val{p28.judge.factor}^{\,\val{p28.judge.rounds}}$. Five rounds of a judge
that is \enquote{a bit overconfident} and the combined belief is wrong by
nearly three orders of magnitude.

\begin{center}
\emph{Slight overconfidence is not a small problem,\\
because Bayes multiplies and small factors compound.}
\end{center}

That is the same arithmetic as Program~\ref{prog:F12}'s vanishing gradient and
Program~\ref{prog:P25}'s per-layer variance factor \dash{} a number near one
raised to the depth \dash{} arriving in a place nobody expects it, which is a
chain of automated judgements rather than a chain of layers.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The version an engineer actually sees is smaller and sharper. Filter the
candidates on \enquote{the judge said $\val{p28.judge.says.pct}$ per cent} and
ship what survives.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule
error rate you believe you shipped & $\val{p28.judge.err.believed.pct}$ per cent \\
error rate you shipped & $\val{p28.judge.err.true.pct}$ per cent \\
\midrule
ratio & $\val{p28.judge.err.ratio}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Three times the errors you budgeted for}, from a judge whose stated
probability was twenty points out. Nothing in the pipeline reports it: the
filter did what it was told, the judge answered every call, and the only
symptom is a defect rate somebody attributes to the generator.

So how do you find out? Not by asking the judge, and not by reading its
prompt.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Count. Take the answers where it said
$\val{p28.judge.says.pct}$ per cent, check how many were right, and put a
posterior on the rate}

$\val{p28.bucket.right}$ of $\val{p28.bucket.n}$, uniform prior, and the
credible interval runs from $\val{p28.bucket.lo}$ to $\val{p28.bucket.hi}$ per
cent. \textbf{The stated $\val{p28.judge.says.pct}$ is outside it}, so the
miscalibration is measured rather than suspected, and the width says how sure
the measurement is.

That is section 3's machinery applied to the one number in a pipeline nobody
audits, and it needs the two counts Program~\ref{prog:F10} owns and nothing
else: what it said, and what happened.

\begin{note}
This section prices a bad judge in \emph{odds}, which is
Program~\ref{prog:P23}'s form and needs nothing new. There is a second reading
\dash{} a stated probability that is wrong costs you a measurable number of
nats, and that number is what a calibration loss minimises \dash{} and it
belongs to Program~\ref{prog:P30}, which defines the units.
\end{note}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Look back at what changed. No new mathematics arrived in this program: the
posterior is Program~\ref{prog:P23}'s theorem with a continuum where it had two
hypotheses, the update is counting, and every number is a sum of binomial
coefficients or one integral of a density.

What changed is the \emph{object}. Program~\ref{prog:P26} ends with a
parameter; this program ends with a distribution over it, and every question
that felt out of reach in Program~\ref{prog:P27} \dash{} how sure am I, is B
better, how much more evidence do I need, how should I route while I find out
\dash{} turns out to be a question about that distribution.

Program~\ref{prog:P29} starts a different question: not how sure you are, but
how much a message is worth.
\end{fr}

\begin{summarybox}
\sumitem{1--3}{\textbf{A point estimate is a summary of something larger.}
  Program~\ref{prog:P26}'s MAP is the location of the posterior's peak, and it
  discards the width, the shape and any way of carrying the uncertainty
  forward.}
\sumitem{4--5}{A $\mathrm{Beta}(a, b)$ prior on a rate is a distribution over
  $\intcc{0}{1}$, and the objection \enquote{the prior is arbitrary} is
  answerable with a number rather than an argument.}
\sumitem{6--7}{\textbf{The update adds the counts.} $k$ successes in $n$
  trials takes $\mathrm{Beta}(a, b)$ to $\mathrm{Beta}(a + k,\ b + n - k)$,
  which is what conjugacy buys and it is the whole of the arithmetic.}
\sumitem{8--10}{\textbf{A prior is worth $a + b$ observations}, so it moves
  the answer by $\frac{a+b}{a+b+n}$ of the way. On $\val{p28.n}$ items a
  uniform prior moves it less than one item; on $\val{p28.small.n}$ it moves
  it by three points.}
\sumitem{12--16}{\textbf{A credible interval says the rate is in there with
  probability $\num{0.95}$}; a confidence interval says the procedure covers
  $95$ per cent of the time. On $\val{p28.n}$ items the two agree to within
  half a point, which is why they are conflated, and it is always the
  Bayesian sentence attached to the frequentist numbers.}
\sumitem{17--18}{\textbf{Report the width.} $\val{p28.width.small}$ points on
  $\val{p28.small.n}$ items against $\val{p28.width.big}$ on $\val{p28.n}$: an
  interval that wide measures the evaluation rather than the model.}
\sumitem{19--21}{\textbf{The probability that B is better is one integral},
  and on Program~\ref{prog:P27}'s own data \dash{} $\val{p28.to.b}$ of
  $\val{p28.disc}$ discordant items \dash{} it is
  $\val{p28.p.better.pct}$ per cent.}
\sumitem{22--23}{That is consistent with $p = \val{p28.p27.p}$ rather than in
  tension with it. Two questions, two answers, both unremarkable.}
\sumitem{24--25}{\textbf{How much more evidence would change your mind is
  computable now.} $\val{p28.more.needed}$ more discordant items, all to B,
  take the posterior past $95$ per cent.}
\sumitem{26}{Unpaired, the same one-item gap gives
  $\val{p28.unp.better.pct}$ per cent against the paired
  $\val{p28.p.better.pct}$: Program~\ref{prog:P27}'s pairing argument arriving
  in a posterior.}
\sumitem{27--29}{\textbf{Routing to whichever posterior sample is larger sends
  traffic to B with probability $\Prob(\theta_B > \theta_A)$}, by
  construction. It has no free parameter, and it follows the evidence
  \dash{} $\val{p28.route.b.pct}$ to $\val{p28.route.b2.pct}$ per cent after
  $\val{p28.route.more}$ more successes.}
\sumitem{30}{A fixed $\varepsilon$ assumes the evidence never gets strong
  enough to stop paying for exploration. After
  $\val{p28.eps.crossover}$ more successes it is spending more on the worse
  arm than the data supports.}
\sumitem{31--32}{\textbf{Convert a judge's stated confidence to odds before
  judging it.} $\val{p28.judge.says.pct}$ against
  $\val{p28.judge.means.pct}$ per cent is
  $\val{p28.judge.odds.says}$ against $\val{p28.judge.odds.means}$ \dash{} a
  factor of $\val{p28.judge.factor}$, not twenty points.}
\sumitem{33--35}{\textbf{Bayes multiplies, so the error compounds}:
  $\val{p28.judge.compound}$ over $\val{p28.judge.rounds}$ judgements, and
  $\val{p28.judge.err.ratio}$ times the errors you budgeted for on the accept
  set.}
\sumitem{36}{\textbf{Calibration is measured by counting}, not by asking. On
  $\val{p28.bucket.right}$ of $\val{p28.bucket.n}$ the credible interval is
  $\val{p28.bucket.lo}$ to $\val{p28.bucket.hi}$ per cent, and the stated
  $\val{p28.judge.says.pct}$ is outside it.}
\end{summarybox}

\begin{testexercises}
\item A retrieval filter is right on $18$ of $20$ spot-checked queries. Start
  from a uniform prior. What is the posterior, and what is its mean?
  \answerto{$\mathrm{Beta}(19, 3)$, mean $\frac{19}{22} = \num{0.8636}$. Note
  it is not $\frac{18}{20} = \num{0.90}$: the prior's two observations pull it
  down, and on twenty items they are worth a tenth of the evidence.}
\item Your colleague reports a $95$ per cent confidence interval of
  $\intcc{62}{74}$ and says there is a $95$ per cent chance the accuracy is in
  it. Say what is wrong with the sentence, and when it does not matter.
  \answerto{The probability belongs to the procedure, not to that interval:
  once the numbers are on the page the rate is either in it or not. It does
  not matter when the evaluation is large enough for the prior to be
  negligible \dash{} $\frac{a+b}{a+b+n}$ small \dash{} because then the
  credible interval, whose sentence that is, has nearly the same endpoints.}
\item Two models are compared on the same $500$ prompts. They disagree on
  $60$ items, of which $36$ go to B. Is B better?
  \answerto{The posterior is $\mathrm{Beta}(37, 25)$ and
  $\Prob(\theta > \frac{1}{2})$ is about $\num{0.93}$ \dash{} suggestive and short
  of the conventional bar. The $440$ concordant items are irrelevant to the
  comparison and should not appear in it.}
\item A judge states $\num{0.8}$ and is right $\num{0.6}$ of the time on those
  answers. By what factor are its odds overstated, and what does that become
  over three independent judgements?
  \answerto{$\frac{4}{1}$ against $\frac{3}{2}$, so $\frac{8}{3} =
  \num{2.667}$; cubed, about $19$. Convert to odds first: the probabilities
  differ by twenty points and the odds by a factor of nearly three.}
\item You are asked to pick $\varepsilon$ for an exploration policy. Give the
  argument for not having one.
  \answerto{Routing on a draw from each posterior explores with probability
  $\Prob(\theta_B > \theta_A)$, which is large when the evidence is thin and
  small when it is not. A constant cannot track a quantity that changes with
  every observation, so tuning it is choosing one point on a moving curve.}
\end{testexercises}

\begin{furtherproblems}
\item Show that the posterior mean is a weighted average of the prior mean and
  the sample proportion, and identify the weights.
  \answerto{$\frac{a+k}{a+b+n} = \frac{a+b}{a+b+n}\cdot\frac{a}{a+b} +
  \frac{n}{a+b+n}\cdot\frac{k}{n}$. The weights are $a+b$ and $n$ over their
  sum, which is why a prior's strength is measured in observations.}
\item A uniform prior is $\mathrm{Beta}(1, 1)$ and is worth two observations.
  Which prior is worth none, and what goes wrong with it?
  \answerto{$\mathrm{Beta}(0, 0)$, which is not a probability distribution
  \dash{} its density is not integrable. With $k = 0$ or $k = n$ the posterior
  is improper too, so a rate observed as $0$ of $5$ has no posterior at all.
  Wanting a prior worth nothing is wanting an answer from no assumptions.}
\item The credible and confidence intervals agree to within half a point on
  $\val{p28.n}$ items. Predict, without computing, whether they agree better
  or worse at $n = 20$, and say why.
  \answerto{Worse. The gap is driven by the prior's weight
  $\frac{a+b}{a+b+n}$, which is $\frac{2}{202}$ at
  $\val{p28.n}$ and $\frac{2}{22}$ at $20$ \dash{} nine times larger. The
  normal approximation behind the confidence interval also degrades as $n$
  falls, so both sources of disagreement grow together.}
\item You have posteriors for three models rather than two. Write down what
  Thompson sampling routes to each, and say why no pairwise comparison
  suffices.
  \answerto{The probability that arm $i$'s draw is the largest of the three,
  which is an integral over the joint. Pairwise probabilities do not determine
  it: A can beat B and B beat C with C beating A often enough to lead, which is
  the same non-transitivity a leaderboard's pairwise cells can hide.}
\item Program~\ref{prog:P27} says a $p$-value of $\val{p28.p27.p}$ is the least
  informative outcome its arithmetic permits. State the corresponding
  least-informative outcome for the posterior, and say which of the two you
  would rather report.
  \answerto{A posterior of exactly a half, which happens when the discordant
  items split evenly \dash{} impossible here, because an odd count cannot
  split evenly. That is the point: the posterior always has a direction and a
  size, so it degrades gracefully where a two-sided $p$-value saturates at $1$
  and stops distinguishing anything.}
\end{furtherproblems}
