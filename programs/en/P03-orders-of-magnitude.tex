\label{prog:P03}
\index{cost!counting operations and bytes}
\index{O-notation}

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{1--7}{read a growth rate off a loop, and say what the notation for it
  claims}
\outcome{8--12}{say what the notation leaves out, and decide between two
  implementations with it}
\outcome{13--17}{price a piece of work in operations, from a count you already
  have}
\outcome{18--24}{size the memory a model needs, in training and in serving,
  and say which term grows with what}
\outcome{25--34}{divide operations by bytes, and say which of the two a piece
  of work is waiting on}
\end{outcomes}

\begin{quiz}
\item An algorithm is $O(n)$. Is it also $O(n^{2})$? \teachesat{3--4}
  \answerto{Yes. $O$ is an upper bound on growth, not a description of it, and
  a bound that is loose is still a bound.}
\item Algorithm A is $O(n^{2})$ and algorithm B is $O(n\mfalogplain n)$. Which do you
  ship? \teachesat{8--9}
  \answerto{Not enough information. The notation discards the constants, and
  the constants decide everything below the crossover.}
\item Two implementations cost $\val{p03.cross.c1} n^{2}$ and
  $\val{p03.cross.c2} n\logb{2} n$. At which $n$ do they trade places?
  \teachesat{9--10}
  \answerto{$\val{p03.cross.n}$. Below it the quadratic one wins, by
  $\val{p03.cross.ratio.100}$ times at $n = 100$.}
\item A model has $\val{f01.params}$ parameters held in two bytes. How much
  memory does training it need? \teachesat{19--20}
  \answerto{About $\val{p03.mem.total}$~GB \dash{}
  $\val{p03.mem.multiple}$ times the weights, which are only
  $\val{p03.mem.weights.pct}\%$ of the bill.}
\item A served conversation grows from $\val{p03.kv.seq}$ tokens to twice
  that. What happens to the key--value cache? \teachesat{22--23}
  \answerto{It doubles, from $\val{p03.kv.gib}$ to
  $\val{p03.kv.double.gib}$~GiB. The cache is \emph{linear} in the sequence
  even though attention's arithmetic is quadratic.}
\item What is arithmetic intensity, and what do you compare it against?
  \teachesat{26--27}
  \answerto{Operations divided by bytes moved, and you compare it against the
  device's own ratio of operations per byte delivered \dash{}
  $\val{p03.dev.ratio}$ under this program's assumptions.}
\item Is a matrix multiply always compute-bound? \teachesat{30--31}
  \answerto{No. Its intensity grows with the size, so it is compute-bound only
  above about $n = \val{p03.ai.smalln}$ on the device assumed here. A small one
  is memory-bound like anything else.}
\item Three elementwise operations run one after another. What does fusing
  them into one pass buy? \teachesat{32--33}
  \answerto{A factor of $\val{p03.fuse.factor}$ in bytes moved, for exactly
  the same operations \dash{} but the result is still far below the device's
  ratio, so it helps and does not rescue.}
\end{quiz}

\section{What the notation says}
\label{sec:P03-says}
\index{O-notation}

\begin{fr}
Three programs, one arithmetic. Program~\ref{prog:P01} asked what it is.
Program~\ref{prog:P02} asked which algorithms survive it. This one asks the
question a design review actually opens with: \textbf{what does it cost?}

Program~\ref{prog:F10} did the counting and handed over the notation by name,
along with a warning that the notation needs its own definition, its own list
of what it does \emph{not} say, and a worked case where the asymptotically
worse algorithm is the one to ship.

Start with the count. One loop does $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ units of work; another
does $3n^{2} + 5n + 100$. Which is larger at $n = 10^{6}$, and by roughly what
factor?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
The second, by about six.
\end{ansblock}

$\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ is about $\frac{n^{2}}{2}$ and $3n^{2} + 5n + 100$ is about
$3n^{2}$, so the ratio heads for $6$ and the $5n$ and the $100$ contribute
nothing you could measure.

Two things happened there and both are the notation's whole content. The
\emph{lower-order terms stopped mattering}, because $n^{2}$ outgrows $n$ and
outgrows a constant. And the \emph{leading coefficient survived} as a factor of
six, which does not grow with $n$ and never will.

$O$-notation keeps the first observation and throws away the second. Both loops
are $O(n^{2})$.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The definition, stated once so that the rest of this program can lean on it.

\medskip
\noindent
$f(n)$ is $O(g(n))$ when there is a constant $c$ and a size $n_0$ such that
$f(n) \le c\,g(n)$ for every $n \ge n_0$.

\medskip
\noindent
Read the two quantifiers. \emph{Some} constant, so the constant is not
recorded. \emph{Beyond some size}, so nothing at all is claimed about small
inputs.

Here is the first thing people get wrong, and it follows directly. An algorithm
takes exactly $n$ steps. Is it $O(n^{2})$?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Yes.}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{$O$ is an upper bound on growth, not a description of it.} A linear
algorithm is $O(n^{2})$, and $O(n^{3})$, and $O(2^{n})$: every one of those is
a true statement, and every one is looser than necessary.

Saying an algorithm \enquote{is $O(n^{2})$} therefore tells you it is no worse
than quadratic. It does not tell you it is quadratic. When somebody means
\emph{exactly this growth, no better}, the notation for it is $\Theta$, and it
is the one they usually meant.

The practical consequence is not pedantry. \enquote{Our retrieval is $O(n)$} is
a claim that cannot be falsified by a benchmark, because it is compatible with
any constant whatsoever \dash{} and the constant is what you are paying.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The hierarchy, in the order that matters, with the reason each step is a step:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
growth & what it means for doubling the input \\
\midrule
$O(1)$ & nothing changes \\
$O(\mfalogplain n)$ & one more step \\
$O(n)$ & twice the work \\
$O(n \mfalogplain n)$ & a little over twice \\
$O(n^{2})$ & four times \\
$O(n^{3})$ & eight times \\
$O(2^{n})$ & squares the work \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Program~\ref{prog:F10} measured the $O(n^{2})$ row and found the ratio
approaching four \emph{from above}; the last row is its subsets count, and
\enquote{squares the work} is why one more item can be the one that ends the
project.

Reading it off a loop is mechanical: \textbf{nested loops multiply, sequential
loops add.} A pass of $n$ inside a pass of $n$ is $n^{2}$; a pass of $n$
followed by a pass of $n$ is $2n$, which is $O(n)$.

\begin{notationbox}
\textbf{A logarithm with no base, in a book that forbids them.} This program
writes $O(n \mfalogplain n)$, and Appendix B says a bare logarithm is a build
error here, because the two readerships this book serves read it as two
different functions.

Inside an $O$ the omission is not a convention, it is a theorem. Changing the
base multiplies by a constant:
\[ \logb{a} n = \frac{\logb{b} n}{\logb{b} a} \]
and a constant is exactly what $O$ discards. So $O(n \mfalogplain n)$ names one
class of growth for every base at once, and writing a base inside it would
suggest a distinction that does not exist.

Outside an $O$ the base is back and it matters, which is why
$\val{p03.cross.c2}\,n\logb{2}n$ two sections on is written with its base.
\end{notationbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Two loops, and this is the last of the mechanical part.

The first walks a list of $n$ documents and for each one walks a list of $m$
query terms. The second walks the documents, then separately walks the terms.
Give the growth of each.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$O(nm)$ and $O(n + m)$.
\end{ansblock}

Nested multiplies, sequential adds, and the difference between the two is the
difference between a system that works and one that does not \dash{} which is
worth noticing is a statement the notation \emph{can} make.

That is $O$-notation's honest use: comparing shapes that differ by a factor
that grows. What it cannot do is the subject of the next section, and it is
where most of the harm is done.
\end{fr}

\section{What the notation does not say}
\label{sec:P03-not}
\index{O-notation!what it omits}
\index{measurement!against asymptotics}

\begin{fr}
Algorithm A is $O(n^{2})$. Algorithm B is $O(n \mfalogplain n)$. You have to ship one
of them tomorrow.

Which, and what would you need to know?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Not enough information \dash{} you need the constants and the size of $n$.
\end{ansblock}

The definition threw the constants away on purpose, and they are what you are
paying. Suppose A costs $\val{p03.cross.c1} n^{2}$, because its inner loop is
an add on data already in cache, and B costs $\val{p03.cross.c2} n\logb{2} n$,
because each of its steps allocates, calls through a pointer and misses.

Below some size A wins. Find that size.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$n = \val{p03.cross.n}$.}
\transcript{p03-crossover}

There is no formula for it \dash{} $n^{2} = c\,n\logb{2} n$ has no elementary
solution \dash{} so it is found by search, which is what you would do anyway.

The numbers either side are the point:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrl}
\toprule
at & the slower one costs & and it is the \\
\midrule
$n = 100$ & $\val{p03.cross.ratio.100}\times$ & $n \mfalogplain n$ \\
$n = 1000$ & $\val{p03.cross.ratio.1000}\times$ & $n \mfalogplain n$ \\
$n = 100\,000$ & $\val{p03.cross.ratio.100000}\times$ & $n^{2}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{The asymptotically worse algorithm is
$\val{p03.cross.ratio.100}$ times faster at $n = 100$}, and it stays ahead all
the way to $\val{p03.cross.n}$. If your inputs
are documents in a request, or tokens in a prompt, or features in a row, you
may never reach the crossover at all.
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{p03-crossover}{Two growth rates, two constants, and why the
notation cannot settle it.}{growth against constants}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{Asymptotically better} is a claim about a limit, and you get
to decide whether you ever go there.}

The habit this corrects is treating a growth rate as a ranking. It is not one:
it is a statement about which algorithm wins \emph{eventually}, and eventually
may be past every input your system will see.

Every sorting library in wide use makes exactly this trade. They run an
$O(n^{2})$ insertion sort on short subarrays inside an $O(n \mfalogplain n)$ merge or
quicksort, because below a threshold of a few dozen elements the quadratic
algorithm is faster, and a sort of a million elements is mostly sorts of a few
dozen elements. Nobody regards that as a compromise.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The second thing the notation does not say is what an operation is.

A growth rate counts \emph{steps}, and treats every step as costing the same.
On a machine they do not: an addition on a value already in a register and a
read that misses every cache differ by a factor in the hundreds, and neither
appears in $O(n)$.

That is why an $O(n)$ pass over a linked list can lose to an $O(n \mfalogplain n)$ pass
over an array, and why the two lists in the last section \dash{} both $O(n)$
\dash{} are not the same program at all.

\medskip
\noindent
So the two facts are separate and you need both. \textbf{The notation says how
the cost scales; a measurement says what it is.} The rest of this program is
the second one.
\end{fr}

\section{Counting operations}
\label{sec:P03-flops}
\index{cost!FLOPs}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:F04} counted the pairs in a causal attention layer \dash{}
$\val{p03.flop.pairs}$ of them for its context \dash{} and said this program
would price the work in FLOPs rather than in pairs. Here is the translation,
and it needs nothing you do not have.

Each pair costs one dot product between a query and a key, and
Program~\ref{prog:F09} says what a dot product costs: for vectors of length
$d$, $d$ multiplications and $d - 1$ additions, which is counted as $2d$
operations.

With a head width of $\val{p03.flop.dhead}$, give the operations for one head's
scores.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$2 \times \val{p03.flop.pairs} \times \val{p03.flop.dhead}
  = \val{p03.flop.perhead}$}
And with $\val{p03.flop.heads}$ heads, $\val{p03.flop.allheads}$ operations
\dash{} for the scores alone, in one layer, for one sequence.

\begin{note}
\textbf{A FLOP is one floating-point operation, and the convention is that a
multiply-and-add is two.} Some hardware performs the pair as a single
instruction and some vendors count it as one FLOP for that reason, so a factor
of two between two people's figures is usually this and not a disagreement
about the model. Say which convention you are using and the argument stops.
\end{note}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
For a whole training run the count has a shortcut, and Program~\ref{prog:F01}
already used it: about $6$ FLOPs per parameter per token.

The $6$ is worth unpacking, because it is three separate twos. A forward pass
costs about $2$ per parameter per token \dash{} one multiply and one add, which
is the dot product above. The backward pass computes two gradients, one with
respect to the input and one with respect to the weight, and each costs about
as much as the forward pass. Two forward, four backward, six in total.

For $\val{f01.params}$ parameters over $\val{f01.tokens}$ tokens that is
$\val{p03.train.flops}$, which is the number Program~\ref{prog:F01} computed
and this program's script re-derives so that the two cannot drift apart.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:F02} asked for one habit and this is the frame that owes it:
\textbf{compute the shape at every layer before you run anything, and check it
against what the framework reports.}

The arithmetic is the same as above. A layer that turns a vector of length $d$
into another vector of length $d$ does $d$ dot products of length $d$, so
$2d^{2}$ operations per position \dash{} and the memory it needs for its own
parameters is $d^{2}$ numbers, which is the same $d^{2}$ wearing a different
unit.

That coincidence is worth holding on to and it does not survive contact with
attention, where the parameters are $O(d^{2})$ and the work is
$O(d\,n^{2})$ in the sequence length. \textbf{A count of parameters is not a count of
work}, and confusing the two is how a model that fits is found not to run.

\begin{rigourbox}
\textbf{What this program does not do is count a transformer block's
parameters.} That needs shapes, and shapes need what a matrix is, which is
Program~\ref{prog:P06}. The count itself is Program~\ref{prog:P32}'s, where
every piece exists. What is available here is the arithmetic and the sigma
\dash{} which, as Program~\ref{prog:F04} said, is enough to price work.
\end{rigourbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{A FLOP count is a count. It is not a time.}

Program~\ref{prog:F01} turned its $\val{p03.train.flops}$ into device-days by
dividing by a device's rate and then dividing again by a utilisation of
$\val{f01.device.util.pct}\%$, and that second division is the whole subject of
the last section of this program.

Nobody achieves a device's advertised rate. The gap is not waste and it is not
a bug: it is the time the arithmetic spends waiting for the numbers to arrive,
and it is predictable from the ratio the last section computes. A FLOP count
with no utilisation beside it is an estimate of the best case, quoted as though
it were a plan.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\section{Counting bytes}
\label{sec:P03-bytes}
\index{cost!memory}
\index{memory!training against serving}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:F01} computed a model's weight memory and then said, in a
warning box, that the weights are a \emph{floor} \dash{} that training also
holds optimiser state and activations, that serving holds a growing cache, and
that both are counted properly here. This section is that debt.

Start with what is resident. A model is being trained. Its
$\val{f01.params}$ parameters are held in two bytes each, which is
$\val{p03.mem.weights}$~GB.

Name everything else that has to exist at the same time.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
A gradient for every parameter, and whatever the optimiser keeps for every
parameter.
\end{ansblock}

The gradients are the easy half: one per weight, alive for the whole backward
pass, and in the same two bytes \dash{} another
$\val{p03.mem.grads}$~GB.

The optimiser is the half that surprises people. A moment-based optimiser keeps
two running averages per parameter \dash{} Program~\ref{prog:F04} built exactly
those \dash{} and mixed-precision training keeps a full-width master copy of
the weights as well, because a sixteen-bit weight cannot absorb a small update,
which is Program~\ref{prog:P01}'s threshold arriving in a training loop. Three
copies at four bytes each.
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{p03-memory-bill}{What is resident while a model trains, and why the
weights are the smallest term.}{the training memory bill}

\begin{fr}
The bill, for $\val{f01.params}$ parameters:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
 & GB \\
\midrule
weights, two bytes & $\val{p03.mem.weights}$ \\
gradients, two bytes & $\val{p03.mem.grads}$ \\
master copy, four bytes & $\val{p03.mem.master}$ \\
first moment, four bytes & $\val{p03.mem.moment1}$ \\
second moment, four bytes & $\val{p03.mem.moment2}$ \\
\midrule
total & $\val{p03.mem.total}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{The weights are $\val{p03.mem.weights.pct}\%$ of the training bill, and
the whole is $\val{p03.mem.multiple}$ times the number on the model card.}

\begin{aibox}
That factor is the answer to a question people ask constantly: why a model that
loads and runs on a card will not fine-tune on it. A $\val{p03.mem.weights}$~GB
model on a $24$~GB card has room to serve and needs
$\val{p03.mem.total}$~GB to train.

Each of the usual remedies removes one row rather than shrinking all of them.
Training a small number of extra parameters instead of all of them removes
almost all of the last four rows and leaves the first. An eight-bit optimiser
halves the two moment rows. Sharding across devices divides the rows rather
than removing them. Knowing which row a technique attacks is what lets you
predict whether it will be enough, and the arithmetic above is all it takes.
\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
One term is missing from that table on purpose, because it does not scale with
the parameters at all.

\textbf{Activations} are the intermediate values kept from the forward pass
because the backward pass needs them \dash{} which is Program~\ref{prog:F12}'s
finding about the chain rule, arriving as a memory bill. Their size depends on
the batch size and the sequence length and the depth, and not on the parameter
count, so the same model has a different activation bill on every workload.

That makes them the term you tune. Halving the batch halves them and changes
nothing else in the table. And \emph{gradient checkpointing} keeps only some of
them and recomputes the rest during the backward pass, which trades operations
for bytes \dash{} the two quantities this program is about, exchanged
deliberately.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Now serving, where the bill has a different shape.

A conversation is being generated one token at a time. Each new token attends
to every previous one, so the keys and values for every previous position have
to be available \dash{} and rather than recompute them they are kept. That is
the \emph{key--value cache}.

For a model with $\val{p03.kv.layers}$ layers and a width of
$\val{p03.kv.dmodel}$, in two bytes, it is
\[ 2 \times \text{layers} \times \text{width} \times \text{positions}
   \times \text{bytes} \]
Compute it for a context of $\val{p03.kv.seq}$ tokens, and say what happens
when the conversation reaches twice that length.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p03.kv.gib}$~GiB, and at twice the length it is
$\val{p03.kv.double.gib}$~GiB.}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{The cache is linear in the sequence, and almost everybody expects it to
be quadratic.}

The expectation comes from somewhere real: attention's \emph{arithmetic} is
quadratic in the sequence, because every position attends to every earlier one,
and Program~\ref{prog:F04} counted exactly those pairs. So the mind supplies
$n^{2}$ for the memory as well.

It is $n$. The cache holds one key and one value \emph{per position}, not per
pair \dash{} the pairs are formed and consumed inside the computation and never
stored. Two quantities, two exponents, one layer.

The practical form is per token: $\val{p03.kv.per.token.mib}$~MiB of cache for
every token in flight, at these shapes. Multiply by the tokens and by the
concurrent conversations, and you have the capacity plan. It is usually larger
than the weights, and it is the term that decides how many users a card
serves.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
One line of housekeeping, because it accounts for most of the disagreements
between an estimate and a monitoring dashboard.

Program~\ref{prog:F01} settled it: a GB is $10^{9}$ bytes and a GiB is
$2^{30}$, and the second is about seven per cent larger. Every number in this
section is in one or the other and says which. A capacity plan that is seven
per cent out is nearly always this, and the fix is to write the unit rather
than to re-do the arithmetic.
\end{fr}

\section{Operations against bytes}
\label{sec:P03-intensity}
\index{cost!arithmetic intensity}
\index{cost!compute-bound and memory-bound}

\begin{fr}
Two pieces of work. One multiplies two large square arrays of numbers together.
The other adds two arrays of the same size elementwise.

Both take time. Predict which of the two spends most of that time doing
arithmetic, and which spends most of it waiting.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
The multiply is doing arithmetic; the elementwise add is waiting.
\end{ansblock}

And it is a ratio rather than an intuition. Count the operations, count the
bytes that have to be moved to and from memory, and divide:

\medskip
\noindent
\textbf{arithmetic intensity} $=$ operations $\div$ bytes moved

\medskip
\noindent
Then compare it against a number belonging to the machine rather than to your
code: how many operations that device can perform for every byte its memory
can deliver. Above that ratio you are waiting on arithmetic; below it you are
waiting on memory.
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{p03-roofline}{One ratio for the work, one for the machine, and the
comparison that decides.}{operations against bytes}

\begin{fr}
The device's ratio, and it is a \emph{stated assumption} in this book rather
than a measurement, as Program~\ref{prog:F01}'s device figures are.

Take $\val{p03.dev.flops}$ operations per second and
$\val{p03.dev.bytes}$ bytes per second of memory bandwidth. The ratio is
\[ \frac{\val{p03.dev.flops}}{\val{p03.dev.bytes}}
   = \val{p03.dev.ratio} \text{ operations per byte} \]

Nothing below depends on those two figures being right for your hardware, and
you should substitute your own. What matters is that the number is in the
\emph{hundreds}, and that it has been climbing for two decades: arithmetic has
got cheaper far faster than memory has got quicker, which is why an argument
that used to be about operations is now nearly always about bytes.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Now the two pieces of work.

An elementwise add of two arrays reads two values and writes one for every
operation it performs. At two bytes each that is six bytes per operation, so
its intensity is $\frac{1}{6} = \val{p03.ai.elementwise}$ \dash{} and $n$ does
not appear anywhere, so \textbf{it is the same however large the arrays are.}

A multiply of two $n$-square arrays does about $2n^{3}$ operations, because it
is $n^{2}$ dot products of length $n$, and moves about $3n^{2}$ values. Its
intensity is
\[ \frac{2n^{3}}{3n^{2} \times 2} = \frac{n}{3} \]
which \emph{grows with $n$}. At $n = \val{p03.ai.matmul.n}$ it is
$\val{p03.ai.matmul}$.
\end{fr}

The gap between the two rows is a factor of $\val{p03.ai.gap}$, and the gap
between the second row and the device is $\val{p03.ai.short}$. \textbf{An
elementwise operation uses about one part in $\val{p03.ai.short}$ of the
arithmetic the machine can do while it runs}, and the rest of the machine is idle waiting
for memory.

That is the justification for kernel fusion, for keeping activations in fast
memory, and for every optimisation that looks like it is about data movement
rather than about mathematics. They are all attacks on the denominator.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{About $n = \val{p03.ai.smalln}$.}
\begin{warning}
\textbf{A small matrix multiply is memory-bound like anything else.} Below
$n = \val{p03.ai.smalln}$ on the device assumed here, the multiply is waiting
on memory too, and making its arithmetic faster does nothing at all.

\enquote{Matrix multiplies are compute-bound} is a statement about the large
ones, and it is repeated as though it were about the operation. A batch of many
small multiplies \dash{} which is what a stack of small heads, or a set of
low-rank adapters, or a mixture of experts with narrow experts produces
\dash{} sits on the wrong side of that line, and its performance is governed by
how the data is laid out rather than by how many operations it needs.
\end{warning}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Fusion, as a number rather than as an argument.

Three elementwise operations run one after another, each reading two arrays and
writing one. Unfused, that is $\val{p03.fuse.chain}$ round trips to memory for
$\val{p03.fuse.chain}$ operations per element. Fused into a single pass, the
data is read once, all three operations are applied, and the result is written
once.

The operations are unchanged. The bytes are divided by
$\val{p03.fuse.factor}$, so the intensity is multiplied by
$\val{p03.fuse.factor}$, from $\val{p03.ai.elementwise}$ to
$\val{p03.fuse.after}$.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
And the honest reading of that number, which is the one worth carrying.

$\val{p03.fuse.after}$ is still far below $\val{p03.dev.ratio}$. The fused
chain is memory-bound, exactly as the unfused one was, and it will stay
memory-bound however many operations are folded into it, because the bytes have
a floor: the input has to be read once and the output written once, whatever
happens in between.

\textbf{Fusion helps and does not rescue.} What it does is remove the round
trips that were pure overhead, which is worth a factor of
$\val{p03.fuse.factor}$ here and can be worth more in a longer chain. What it
cannot do is turn a memory-bound operation into a compute-bound one, and a plan
that assumes it will is a plan that will be disappointed by a profile.
\end{fr}

\begin{summarybox}
\sumitem{1--2}{\result{\textbf{$O$-notation keeps how the cost grows and throws away
  the constant.} $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ and $3n^{2} + 5n + 100$ differ by about
  six at large $n$, and the notation calls both $O(n^{2})$: the lower-order
  terms genuinely stop mattering, and the leading coefficient does not}.}
\sumitem{3--4}{\result{$f$ is $O(g)$ when $f(n) \le c\,g(n)$ beyond some $n_0$
  \dash{} \emph{some} constant, \emph{beyond} some size. So \textbf{$O$ is an
  upper bound, not a description}: a linear algorithm is honestly $O(n^{2})$.
  $\Theta$ is the notation for \emph{exactly this growth}, and it is usually
  what was meant}.}
\sumitem{5--7}{\result{Doubling the input: $O(\mfalogplain n)$ adds a step, $O(n)$ doubles,
  $O(n^{2})$ quadruples, $O(2^{n})$ \emph{squares} the work.
  \textbf{Nested loops multiply, sequential loops add} \dash{} $O(nm)$ against
  $O(n+m)$, which is a difference the notation \emph{can} state}.}
\sumitem{8--10}{\result{\textbf{Two growth rates do not rank two implementations.}
  $\val{p03.cross.c1}n^{2}$ against $\val{p03.cross.c2}n\logb{2}n$ trade places
  at $n = \val{p03.cross.n}$, found by search because there is no formula. At
  $n = 100$ the asymptotically better one costs
  $\val{p03.cross.ratio.100}$ times as much; at $n = 100\,000$ the other one
  costs $\val{p03.cross.ratio.100000}$ times as much}.}
\sumitem{11--12}{\result{\enquote{Asymptotically better} is a claim about a limit you
  may never reach \dash{} every sorting library runs an $O(n^{2})$ insertion
  sort on short subarrays for exactly this reason. And \textbf{the notation
  does not say what an operation is}: a register add and a cache miss differ
  by a factor in the hundreds and both count as one step. The notation says how
  the cost scales; a measurement says what it is}.}
\sumitem{13--14}{\result{Program~\ref{prog:F04}'s $\val{p03.flop.pairs}$ pairs priced
  as work: each is a dot product of length $\val{p03.flop.dhead}$, so
  $\val{p03.flop.perhead}$ operations per head and
  $\val{p03.flop.allheads}$ across $\val{p03.flop.heads}$ heads \dash{} scores
  alone, one layer, one sequence. A multiply-and-add is \textbf{two} FLOPs by
  convention, and a factor of two between two people's counts is usually that}.}
\sumitem{15--16}{\result{Training costs about $6$ FLOPs per parameter per token: two
  forward, and two each for the backward pass's two gradients. That gives
  $\val{p03.train.flops}$ for $\val{f01.params}$ parameters over
  $\val{f01.tokens}$ tokens, which is Program~\ref{prog:F01}'s number
  re-derived. \textbf{A count of parameters is not a count of work}: attention
  has $O(d^{2})$ parameters and $O(d\,n^{2})$ work}.}
\sumitem{17}{\result{\textbf{A FLOP count is a count, not a time.} The gap to the
  device's advertised rate is the time the arithmetic spends waiting for
  numbers, and it is what the last section predicts. A FLOP count with no
  utilisation beside it is a best case quoted as a plan}.}
\sumitem{18--20}{\result{\textbf{The weights are the smallest term.} For
  $\val{f01.params}$ parameters: $\val{p03.mem.weights}$~GB of weights,
  $\val{p03.mem.grads}$ of gradients and $\val{p03.mem.optimiser}$ of optimiser
  state \dash{} $\val{p03.mem.total}$~GB in all, which is
  $\val{p03.mem.multiple}$ times the model card's number and leaves the weights
  at $\val{p03.mem.weights.pct}\%$. That factor is why a model that serves on a
  card will not fine-tune on it, and each remedy removes a \emph{row}}.}
\sumitem{21}{\result{\textbf{Activations do not scale with the parameters} \dash{}
  they scale with batch, sequence and depth, so the same model has a different
  bill on every workload. They are kept because the backward pass needs them,
  which is Program~\ref{prog:F12}'s chain rule as a memory bill, and
  checkpointing recomputes them instead: operations traded for bytes on
  purpose}.}
\sumitem{22--23}{\result{\textbf{The key--value cache is linear in the sequence while
  attention's arithmetic is quadratic}, and nearly everybody expects both to be
  quadratic. It holds one key and value per \emph{position}, not per pair:
  $\val{p03.kv.gib}$~GiB at $\val{p03.kv.seq}$ tokens,
  $\val{p03.kv.double.gib}$ at twice that, and
  $\val{p03.kv.per.token.mib}$~MiB per token in flight. It is usually larger than
  the weights and it decides how many users a card serves}.}
\sumitem{24}{\result{GB is $10^{9}$ and GiB is $2^{30}$, about seven per cent larger.
  A capacity plan that is seven per cent out is nearly always this}.}
\sumitem{25--29}{\result{\textbf{Arithmetic intensity is operations divided by bytes
  moved}, compared against the device's own operations-per-byte \dash{}
  $\val{p03.dev.ratio}$ under this program's stated assumptions. An elementwise
  add is $\val{p03.ai.elementwise}$ \emph{whatever $n$ is}; an $n$-square
  multiply is $\frac{n}{3}$, which \emph{grows}, and is
  $\val{p03.ai.matmul}$ at $n = \val{p03.ai.matmul.n}$}.}
\sumitem{30--31}{\result{The elementwise operation is short of the device by
  $\val{p03.ai.short}$, so that is the fraction of the available arithmetic it
  uses. And \textbf{a matrix multiply is compute-bound only
  above $n \approx \val{p03.ai.smalln}$} \dash{} a batch of small multiplies is
  memory-bound like anything else, which is what a stack of narrow heads or
  experts produces}.}
\sumitem{32--34}{\result{Fusing $\val{p03.fuse.chain}$ elementwise operations divides
  the bytes by $\val{p03.fuse.factor}$ for exactly the same operations, taking
  the intensity to $\val{p03.fuse.after}$ \dash{} still far below
  $\val{p03.dev.ratio}$. \textbf{Fusion helps and does not rescue}, because the
  input must be read once and the output written once whatever happens between.} \textbf{This closes Part II}: three questions about one arithmetic, answered
  with nothing beyond arithmetic, algebra and a sigma.}
\end{summarybox}

\begin{testexercises}
\item An algorithm takes exactly $7n + 3$ steps. Give the tightest $O$ and say
  whether $O(n^{2})$ is also true of it.
  \answerto{$O(n)$, and yes \dash{} $O$ is an upper bound, so a looser one is

  \answerto{$O(mn)$. The two sequential passes add to $O(n)$; nesting that

  \answerto{A. The crossover is at $n = \val{p03.cross.n}$, and at $n = 100$

  a limit these inputs never reach.}
\item Give the operations for one head's attention scores from a pair count and
  a head width, and say what convention you used.
  \answerto{$2 \times \text{pairs} \times \text{width}$, taking a

  \answerto{Two for the forward pass, and two each for the backward pass's two

  weight.}

\begin{furtherproblems}
\item Show that $O(n^{2})$ and $O(n^{2} + n)$ are the same claim, and say what
  that tells you about writing lower-order terms inside an $O$.
  \answerto{$n^{2} + n \le 2n^{2}$ for $n \ge 1$, so each bounds the other up
  to a constant. Writing the lower-order term inside an $O$ adds nothing and
  suggests a precision the notation does not have.}
\item An algorithm is $O(n \mfalogplain n)$ and another is $O(n \mfalogplain ^{2} n)$. Give the
  size at which the second is ten times the first, and comment.
  \answerto{The ratio is $\logb{2} n$, so it reaches ten at $n = 2^{10}$,
  which is $1024$. The extra logarithm costs a factor that grows very slowly
  indeed \dash{} a thousandfold increase in $n$ buys it a factor of ten.}
\item The crossover in this program was found by search. Explain why bisection
  works here, and what property of the two cost functions it needs.
  \answerto{The ratio $c\,n\logb{2}n / n^{2} = c\logb{2}n / n$ falls
  monotonically once $n > e$, so the predicate \enquote{the quadratic is
  cheaper} switches exactly once. Bisection needs that single switch and
  nothing else.}
\item A model is trained with an optimiser holding one running average instead
  of two. Recompute the bill and the multiple.
  \answerto{$\val{p03.mem.weights} + \val{p03.mem.grads} +
  \val{p03.mem.master} + \val{p03.mem.moment1}$, so $84$~GB, and six times the
  weights instead of $\val{p03.mem.multiple}$. Still far more than the model
  card suggests.}
\item Two serving configurations use the same model: one holds four
  conversations of $\val{p03.kv.seq}$ tokens, the other one conversation of
  four times that. Compare their cache and their arithmetic per token.
  \answerto{The cache is identical \dash{} it is linear in total positions
  held, and both hold the same number. The arithmetic per generated token is
  four times higher in the long conversation, because each new token attends
  to four times as many previous ones.}
\item Derive the size at which an $n$-square multiply becomes compute-bound on
  a device with a ratio of $R$, in a format of $b$ bytes.
  \answerto{Intensity is $2n^{3} / (3n^{2}b) = 2n/(3b)$, so it exceeds $R$ when
  $n > 3bR/2$. Note it grows with $b$: a wider format pushes the boundary
  \emph{up}, because it moves more bytes for the same operations.}
\item A chain of $k$ elementwise operations is fused. Give the intensity as a
  function of $k$ and say what it approaches.
  \answerto{$k$ operations for the same three arrays moved, so $k/(3b)$, which
  grows without bound in $k$. In practice the chain is short and the input must
  still be read once, so the achievable range is small \dash{} which is why
  fusion is a factor and not a fix.}
\item Argue that a technique which halves the parameter count does not halve
  the training memory, using this program's table.
  \answerto{Every row scales with the parameters, so halving them halves the
  whole bill \dash{} except the activations, which are not in the table and
  scale with batch, sequence and depth. Whether the halving is enough therefore
  depends on a term the parameter count does not touch.}
\end{furtherproblems}
