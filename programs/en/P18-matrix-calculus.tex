\label{prog:P18}
\index{matrix calculus}
\index{layout convention}

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{1--7}{say what numerator layout is, and why the choice of layout is
  the only thing a derivative by a vector adds}
\outcome{8--14}{differentiate a scalar loss with respect to a weight matrix,
  and check the answer by its shape alone}
\outcome{15--21}{write the derivative of $\softmax$ and say what its rows sum
  to and why}
\outcome{22--31}{derive that the gradient of cross-entropy through a
  $\softmax$ is $p - y$ \dash{} the definition of cross-entropy is
  \emph{given} here and justified in Program~\ref{prog:P30}}
\outcome{32--39}{give the two reasons the two operations are fused, and say
  which of them is about speed and which is not}
\end{outcomes}

\begin{quiz}
\item A scalar loss depends on a weight matrix $W$. What shape is
  $\partial L / \partial W$? \teachesat{8--9}
  \answerto{The shape of $W$. One partial derivative per entry, arranged as
  the entries were, which makes the shape a check that costs nothing.}
\item Two accounts of backpropagation give formulas differing by a transpose.
  Which is wrong? \teachesat{5--6}
  \answerto{Neither, probably. They are written in the two layout
  conventions, which differ by exactly one transpose. What is wrong is mixing
  them inside one derivation, and that is what produces a shape error nobody
  can debug.}
\item What shape is the derivative of $\softmax$ on $n$ scores, and what do
  its rows sum to? \teachesat{15--19}
  \answerto{$n \times n$, one row per output and one column per input by
  Program~\ref{prog:P16}'s rule. Every row sums to zero, which is
  \enquote{the probabilities sum to one} differentiated.}
\item Cross-entropy through a $\softmax$: what is the gradient with respect to
  the scores? \teachesat{24--25}
  \answerto{$p - y$ \dash{} the predicted distribution minus the one-hot
  target. It is the most reused derivation in applied machine learning and it
  is four lines from the $\softmax$ Jacobian.}
\item Why is that pair fused in every serious implementation?
  \teachesat{32--35}
  \answerto{Two reasons of different kinds. It is about
  $\val{p18.fuse.ratio}$ times cheaper, and \dash{} which matters more
  \dash{} it is defined on rows where the honest two-step route divides by
  zero, because a probability underflows and $p - y$ never forms the
  reciprocal.}
\end{quiz}

\section{One number, many inputs, and a choice}
\label{sec:P18-layout}
\index{layout convention!numerator and denominator}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:P15} gave you the gradient and Program~\ref{prog:P16} gave
you the Jacobian, so every derivative in this program already exists. The
partial derivatives are the same numbers whatever anybody writes.

There is one thing left to decide before you can write them down at all, and
almost every difficulty a reader has with matrix calculus is that nobody said
which was chosen. What is it?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
How to \emph{arrange} them \dash{} which index runs down the rows and which
runs along the columns.
\end{ansblock}

That is a \textbf{layout convention}, and there are two in use.

\begin{notationbox}
\textbf{This book uses numerator layout}, and says so here rather than
assuming it. In numerator layout the derivative of $\vect{y}$ with respect to
$\vect{x}$ has \textbf{one row per output} and one column per input, which is
exactly Program~\ref{prog:P16}'s shape rule. Denominator layout is that table
transposed.

Neither is more correct and both are common: it is a split between
disciplines rather than between countries. What matters is that a derivation
picks one and holds it, because a formula from one convention dropped into a
derivation in the other is off by a transpose and nothing warns you.
\end{notationbox}

\mermaidfig{p18-one-transpose}{The same derivative, written two ways.}{two layouts}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Take the simplest case there is. $\vect{y} = W\vect{x}$, with $W$ of shape
$\val{p18.n.out} \times \val{p18.n.in}$.

What is $\partial \vect{y} / \partial \vect{x}$, in numerator layout?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$W$ itself.
\end{ansblock}

Because $y_i = \sum_j W_{ij}x_j$, so $\partial y_i / \partial x_j = W_{ij}$,
and numerator layout puts $i$ down the rows \dash{} which is where $W$ already
had it. The script checks every entry against a central difference and the
worst disagreement is under $\val{p18.layout.bound}$.

\textbf{That is the whole case for the convention.} It is the one under which
the derivative of a linear map is the map, and a convention that makes the
easiest case an identity is the one to hold.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Now the price of the other one, because you will meet it this week.

Under denominator layout the same derivative is written the other way up.
Give both shapes, and say what the two conventions therefore differ by.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$\val{p18.n.out} \times \val{p18.n.in}$ against $\val{p18.n.in} \times \val{p18.n.out}$ \dash{} one transpose,
and nothing else.
\end{ansblock}

So a formula quoted from a paper in the other convention is not wrong. It is
correct in its own book and transposed in this one, and the two libraries of
identities are the same library read in a mirror.

The failure this causes is specific and worth naming: not a wrong answer, but
a \emph{shape} error partway through a derivation, at a point far from where
the two conventions were mixed.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{note}
Program~\ref{prog:P06} noted that real frameworks stack a batch along the
first axis and compute $XW\T$, where this book writes $W\vect{x}$. That is
the same choice one level up: the arrangement is a convention and the
arithmetic is not, and $XW\T$ is the transpose of $W X\T$.

Program~\ref{prog:P07} is where the shapes themselves live. This program
takes them as given and asks only what a derivative does to them.
\end{note}
\end{fr}

\section{Differentiating by a matrix}
\label{sec:P18-by-a-matrix}
\index{matrix calculus!gradient of a scalar}

\begin{fr}
Now the case the reader actually meets. A loss $L$ is \emph{one number}, and
it is built out of a weight matrix $W$ with $\val{p18.n.out}$ rows and
$\val{p18.n.in}$ columns.

Before deriving anything: what shape is $\partial L / \partial W$? Say how you
know.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
The shape of $W$. There is one partial derivative per entry of $W$, and they
arrange in the arrangement the entries had.
\end{ansblock}

\textbf{And that is a check that costs nothing.} A gradient of a scalar has
the shape of the thing it is taken with respect to \dash{} always, in either
convention, because the convention decides the order of two indices and a
scalar output supplies neither.

\mermaidfig{p18-shape-is-the-check}{One derivative per entry, arranged as the
entries were.}{shape is the check}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{It is some shape or other; I will transpose until it runs.}}
That works, and it is why the habit survives: with two shapes to try, guessing
terminates.

It stops working the moment two of the dimensions are equal, which for a
square weight matrix, an attention head with as many keys as queries, or a
batch size that happens to match a width is most of the time. Then the wrong
transpose \emph{also} runs, and what you have is a program that trains to a
loss that will not go down, with nothing in it that raises.

The shape is not a thing to guess at until the error stops. It is the check,
and it is available before you write the derivative down.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Derive one. With $\vect{r} = W\vect{x} - \vect{t}$ and
$L = \vect{r} \cdot \vect{r}$, differentiate $L$ with respect to $W_{ij}$.

Take it one entry at a time, which is all \enquote{matrix calculus} ever is.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial W_{ij}} = 2 r_i x_j \]
\end{ansblock}

Because $L = \sum_k r_k^{2}$, so $\partial L/\partial W_{ij} =
2 r_i \, \partial r_i/\partial W_{ij}$, and $r_i = \sum_j W_{ij}x_j - t_i$
contributes $x_j$. Every other row of $r$ has no $W_{ij}$ in it.

Collected up, that is an \textbf{outer product}:
\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial W} = 2\,\vect{r}\,\vect{x}\T \]
which has the shape of $W$, as the previous frame said it must. The script
checks all $\val{p18.n.out}\times\val{p18.n.in}$ entries against a central
difference, worst disagreement under $\val{p18.sq.bound}$.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
That outer-product shape is not a coincidence of this loss, and noticing why
is worth more than the identity.

Every weight matrix in a network is used the same way: it multiplies an input
vector. So say what $\partial L/\partial W$ will look like for \emph{any}
loss, given whatever the layer above sends back.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
The outer product of the incoming gradient with the layer's own input:
$\vect{g}\,\vect{x}\T$.
\end{ansblock}

That is the one identity worth memorising in this program, and it is the
matrix form of Program~\ref{prog:F12}'s chain rule: the gradient arriving at
the layer's output, times the derivative of the output with respect to the
weight, which is the input.

\begin{note}
It also says where the memory goes. To form $\vect{g}\,\vect{x}\T$ you need
$\vect{x}$, which is the layer's input \dash{} so the backward pass needs the
forward pass's activations, which is Program~\ref{prog:P16}'s checkpointing
arithmetic arriving from the other direction. The identity and the memory
bill are the same fact.
\end{note}
\end{fr}

\section{The derivative of a softmax}
\label{sec:P18-softmax-jacobian}
\index{softmax!its Jacobian}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:F07} defined
\[ \softmax(\vect{z})_j = \frac{e^{z_j}}{\sum_k e^{z_k}} \]
and never differentiated it. It takes $n$ scores to $n$ probabilities.

What shape is its derivative, and why can it not be a vector?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$n \times n$: one row per output and one column per input, by
Program~\ref{prog:P16}'s rule. Every output depends on every input, because
they share a denominator.
\end{ansblock}

That shared denominator is the whole difficulty. An elementwise function has
a diagonal derivative and you can pretend it is a vector; $\softmax$ cannot,
because raising one score lowers every other probability.

Differentiate it. Take $\partial p_i / \partial z_j$ in the two cases
$i = j$ and $i \neq j$.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
\[ \frac{\partial p_i}{\partial z_j} = p_i(\delta_{ij} - p_j) \]
\end{ansblock}

The quotient rule twice: with $i = j$ the numerator contributes $p_i$ and the
denominator takes back $p_i^{2}$; with $i \neq j$ only the denominator moves,
giving $-p_i p_j$. The single expression covers both because $\delta_{ij}$ is
$1$ on the diagonal and $0$ off it.

As a matrix,
\[ J = \diag(\vect{p}) - \vect{p}\,\vect{p}\T \]
checked entry by entry against a central difference, worst disagreement under
$\val{p18.jac.bound}$.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
That matrix has a property you can predict without computing anything, from
one fact about $\softmax$ you already have.

The probabilities sum to $1$, whatever the scores are. What does that force
about each row of $J$?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Every row sums to $0$.
\end{ansblock}

Because a quantity that is $1$ for every input does not change when an input
changes, so its derivative is zero in every direction. Measured at under
$\val{p18.jac.rowsum}$, which is rounding.

\textbf{So a $\softmax$ can move probability around and can never make more of
it}, and the row sums are that sentence written as arithmetic. It is also the
cheapest check on an implementation of the Jacobian anybody will ever write.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
And one gate, because two programs must not be able to disagree about one
computation.

Program~\ref{prog:F07} proved that $\softmax$ on two scores is the logistic
of their difference. Differentiating both sides of an identity gives an
identity \dash{} so what must $J$ reduce to when $n = 2$?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$\sigma'(a - b) = \sigma(a-b)\bigl(1 - \sigma(a-b)\bigr)$.
\end{ansblock}

The script checks it over $\val{p18.gate.f07.pairs}$ pairs rather than at a
point, worst disagreement under $\val{p18.gate.f07.bound}$, and it reads
Program~\ref{prog:F07}'s own committed probabilities to confirm the two
programs are computing one $\softmax$.

That is worth having for the reason Program~\ref{prog:P04} gives: a gate is
worth its lines when the two programs quote \emph{one} computation, and this
one is F07's identity differentiated rather than two numbers that resemble
each other.
\end{fr}

\section{Cross-entropy, and the gradient everybody uses}
\label{sec:P18-cross-entropy}
\index{cross-entropy!gradient through a softmax}

\begin{fr}
\begin{note}
\textbf{A definition given here and justified later.} For a predicted
distribution $\vect{p}$ and a target $\vect{y}$,
\[ L = -\sum_j y_j \ln p_j \]
is the \textbf{cross-entropy}. When $\vect{y}$ is one-hot at class $c$ every
term but one is zero and $L = -\ln p_c$.

\emph{Why} that is the right thing to minimise is
Program~\ref{prog:P26}'s (it is the negative log-likelihood of the observed
tokens) and \emph{what it measures} is Program~\ref{prog:P30}'s (the cost of
coding one distribution with another's code). Neither is needed here. This
program needs only its derivative, and the derivative is the most reused
result in applied machine learning.
\end{note}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
So: scores $\vect{z}$, then $\vect{p} = \softmax(\vect{z})$, then
$L = -\ln p_c$ for the true class $c$.

You have every piece. Differentiate $L$ with respect to $p_c$ first \dash{}
just that one step.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$\partial L/\partial p_c = -1/p_c$, and $\partial L / \partial p_j = 0$ for
every other $j$.
\end{ansblock}

Which is where the whole thing could go wrong, and section 5 is about that
$1/p_c$. For now, carry it.

Now push it through the Jacobian. The chain rule for the scores is
\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_j}
   = \sum_i \frac{\partial L}{\partial p_i}\,
     \frac{\partial p_i}{\partial z_j} \]
and only one term of that sum survives. Carry it out.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_j}
   = -\frac{1}{p_c}\,p_c(\delta_{cj} - p_j) = p_j - \delta_{cj} \]
\end{ansblock}

\textbf{The $p_c$ cancels.} That is the whole derivation, and everything that
matters about it is in that cancellation: the reciprocal that the first step
introduced is removed by the $p_i$ that the Jacobian's diagonal carried.

In vector form, with $\vect{y}$ the one-hot target,
\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \vect{z}} = \vect{p} - \vect{y} \]
checked against a central difference, worst disagreement under
$\val{p18.ce.bound}$.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
It is worth saying out loud what that expression \emph{means}, because it
reads as a piece of luck and it is not.

$\vect{p} - \vect{y}$ is the predicted distribution minus the target one.
Read it as an instruction to the scores. What is it telling each one to do?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Lower every score in proportion to the probability it currently has, and
raise the true one by however much probability it is missing.
\end{ansblock}

So the size of the correction is the size of the mistake, in the units of the
thing being predicted, and a model that is already right gets a gradient of
almost exactly zero without anything being switched off.

\begin{note}
The same cancellation happens with a sigmoid and a binary cross-entropy, and
it is the same calculation: Program~\ref{prog:F07} showed that
$\softmax$ on two scores \emph{is} the logistic, so the binary case is this
one at $n = 2$ rather than a second result.
\end{note}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
One layer further back, because that is where a training loop uses it. The
scores are $\vect{z} = W\vect{x}$.

Combine what you have: what is $\partial L/\partial W$?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial W} = (\vect{p} - \vect{y})\,\vect{x}\T \]
\end{ansblock}

Section 2's outer product, with the incoming gradient supplied by section 4.
It has the shape of $W$, and the script checks it against a central
difference at worst $\val{p18.cew.bound}$.

\textbf{Two identities and a shape rule reach the gradient of a classifier's
output layer}, and neither of them needed anything this book had not already
derived.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The brief for this program names a fifth identity, and it is the one people
get wrong: layer normalisation. Its output is
\[ \hat{z}_i = \frac{z_i - \mu}{\sqrt{\operatorname{var} + \varepsilon}} \]
with $\mu$ and the variance both computed over the same vector.

Do not derive it. Say why its derivative cannot be elementwise.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Because $\mu$ and the variance depend on \emph{every} $z_i$, so changing one
input moves every output \dash{} exactly as with $\softmax$.
\end{ansblock}

That is the shape of the answer, and it is why the gradient carries two
correction terms rather than one factor. With $\vect{g}$ arriving from above
and $s$ the standard deviation,
\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_i} = \frac{1}{s}\Bigl(g_i
   - \overline{g} - \hat{z}_i\,\overline{g\hat{z}}\Bigr) \]
where the two bars are means over the vector. The script checks it at
worst $\val{p18.ln.bound}$.

\begin{note}
All five identities in this program are checked the same way, against a
central difference at the step Program~\ref{prog:F11} measured, and each
clears a bound of its own under a shared ceiling of
$\val{p18.check.ceiling}$. The ceiling is the method's rather than a
threshold chosen to make the checks pass: a dropped term disagrees by
something of order one, not by a decimal place.
\end{note}
\end{fr}

\section{Why the two are fused}
\label{sec:P18-fusion}
\index{cross-entropy!fused with softmax}

\begin{fr}
Every serious implementation computes the $\softmax$ and the cross-entropy as
one operation rather than two, and gives it a single name.

There are two reasons and they are different in kind. Start with the cheap
one. A vocabulary of $\val{p18.vocab}$: how much work is the honest two-step
route, and how much is $\vect{p} - \vect{y}$?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
About $\val{p18.fuse.twostep}$ operations against $\val{p18.fuse.fused}$
\dash{} a factor of $\val{p18.fuse.ratio}$.
\end{ansblock}

The two-step route forms the Jacobian, which has one entry per \emph{pair} of
classes, and applies it. The fused route is $\val{p18.vocab}$ subtractions
and no multiplication at all.

And the Jacobian is $\val{p18.fuse.gib}$ GiB at two bytes a number, so it is
Program~\ref{prog:P16}'s \enquote{nobody forms a Jacobian} arriving in the
most reused derivation in the field. The difference from that program's case
is worth noticing: there the product people wanted was cheap, and here the
product collapses to a subtraction, which is better still.

\mermaidfig{p18-two-routes}{Two ways to the same gradient.}{two routes}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Now the reason that survives, and it is not about speed at all.

At ordinary scores the two routes agree to $\val{p18.agree.bound}$, so this
is not a claim that one is more accurate. Look again at the first step of the
two-step route \dash{} $\partial L/\partial p_c = -1/p_c$ \dash{} and say
what a training run will eventually hand it.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
A $p_c$ of zero. Early in a run the model puts almost no probability on the
right answer, and \enquote{almost none} underflows.
\end{ansblock}

Here is a score $\val{p18.bad.logit}$ below the largest, with the true class
last:

\transcript{p18-fused-or-not}

\textbf{The fused route returns an ordinary gradient and the two-step route
raises.} And the gradient it returns is the most informative one available:
the model put no mass on the right answer, so the correction is as large as
it can be.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The threshold is not a property of $\softmax$. Say where it comes from, and
what would move it.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
From the exponential underflowing: below about $\val{p18.cliff}$ under the
largest score, $e^{z_c - \max z}$ is exactly zero in binary64, so $p_c$ is
too. A narrower format moves it much closer in.
\end{ansblock}

That is Program~\ref{prog:P01}'s floor arriving in the field's most reused
derivation, and the script finds the threshold by bisection rather than
quoting it, so a change of format changes the number on the page.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{The fused version is an optimisation.}} It is cheaper, and
if that were the whole story you could keep the two-step route for clarity
and pay for it.

You cannot, because the two routes are not the same function. One is defined
on rows the other is not, and the rows where they differ are the rows a run
meets \emph{early}, when the model is worst and the gradient matters most.
\enquote{Numerically stable} means what Program~\ref{prog:P02} established it
means: not more accurate, but safe on inputs you have not tried.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
One last thing, and it closes section 1's loop.

The fused gradient is $\vect{p} - \vect{y}$, and the fused gradient for the
weights is $(\vect{p} - \vect{y})\vect{x}\T$. Under the other layout
convention the second of those is written the other way up.

Does that change what a program computes?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
No. It changes what you have to type to make the shapes agree, and nothing
about the arithmetic.
\end{ansblock}

Which is this program's thesis in one line: the mathematics is the partial
derivatives, the convention is how they are arranged, and the reason matrix
calculus has a reputation is that the two get confused for one another.

\textbf{The three things worth carrying out of this program are that the
gradient of a scalar has the shape of what it is taken with respect to, that
a weight's gradient is an outer product, and that cross-entropy through a
$\softmax$ is $\vect{p} - \vect{y}$.} Everything else here was derived from
those in a line or two.
\end{fr}

  easiest case an identity is the one to hold}.}
\sumitem{5--6}{\result{\textbf{The two conventions differ by one transpose},
  $\val{p18.n.out} \times \val{p18.n.in}$ against $\val{p18.n.in} \times \val{p18.n.out}$. Neither is wrong;
  mixing them inside one derivation is, and it shows up as a shape error far
  from the mixing}.}
\sumitem{8--9}{\result{\textbf{The gradient of a scalar has the shape of the thing it
  is taken with respect to}, in either convention. That is a check available
  before the derivative is written down}.}
\sumitem{10}{\result{\textbf{Transposing until it runs terminates}, which is why the
  habit survives \dash{} and it stops working the moment two dimensions are
  equal, where the wrong transpose runs too}.}
\sumitem{11--12}{\result{$\partial L/\partial W = 2\vect{r}\vect{x}\T$ for a squared
  error, checked to $\val{p18.sq.bound}$}.}
\sumitem{13--14}{\result{\textbf{A weight's gradient is an outer product},
  $\vect{g}\vect{x}\T$, for any loss \dash{} and it needs the layer's input,
  which is why the backward pass needs the forward pass's activations}.}
\sumitem{15--16}{\result{\textbf{The $\softmax$ derivative is $n\times n$}, because
  every output shares a denominator with every input}.}
\sumitem{16--17}{\result{$J = \diag(\vect{p}) - \vect{p}\vect{p}\T$, checked to
  $\val{p18.jac.bound}$}.}
\sumitem{18--19}{\result{\textbf{Every row of $J$ sums to zero}: the probabilities sum
  to $1$ whatever the scores do, differentiated. A $\softmax$ moves
  probability and never makes more}.}
\sumitem{20--21}{\result{At $n = 2$ it reduces to $\sigma'(a-b)$, over
  $\val{p18.gate.f07.pairs}$ pairs \dash{} Program~\ref{prog:F07}'s identity
  differentiated, and a gate rather than a resemblance}.}
\sumitem{22}{\result{\textbf{Cross-entropy is defined here and justified later}:
  $-\sum_j y_j \ln p_j$, which is $-\ln p_c$ for a one-hot target.} Program~\ref{prog:P26} says why it is the right thing to minimise and
  Program~\ref{prog:P30} what it measures.}
\sumitem{23--25}{\result{\textbf{The gradient is $\vect{p} - \vect{y}$}, and the
  derivation is one cancellation: the $1/p_c$ from the first step meets the
  $p_c$ on the Jacobian's diagonal}.}
\sumitem{26--27}{\result{Read as an instruction: lower every score in proportion to
  the probability it has, raise the true one by the probability it lacks. The
  correction is the size of the mistake}.}
\sumitem{28--29}{\result{One layer back, $\partial L/\partial W =
  (\vect{p}-\vect{y})\vect{x}\T$, checked to $\val{p18.cew.bound}$}.}
\sumitem{30--31}{\result{\textbf{Layer normalisation is not elementwise either}, because
  the mean and the variance depend on every input; its gradient carries two
  correction terms, checked to $\val{p18.ln.bound}$}.}
\sumitem{32--33}{\result{\textbf{Fusion, reason one: cost.}
  $\val{p18.fuse.ratio}$ times fewer operations, and the Jacobian nobody
  forms would be $\val{p18.fuse.gib}$ GiB}.}
\sumitem{34--35}{\result{\textbf{Reason two, and it is not about speed}: the two-step
  route forms $-1/p_c$ and $p_c$ underflows to zero, where
  $\vect{p}-\vect{y}$ never forms the reciprocal}.}
\sumitem{36--37}{\result{The cliff is at about $\val{p18.cliff}$ under the largest
  score, which is Program~\ref{prog:P01}'s floor. \textbf{The two routes are
  not the same function}, and they differ exactly where a run is worst}.}
\sumitem{38--39}{\result{\textbf{The mathematics is the partial derivatives and the
  convention is how they are arranged}; matrix calculus has a reputation
  because the two get confused for one another}.}
\end{summarybox}

\begin{testexercises}
\item A layer computes $\vect{z} = W\vect{x}$ with $W$ of shape
  $256 \times 1024$. Give the shape of $\partial L/\partial W$ and of
  $\partial \vect{z}/\partial\vect{x}$ in numerator layout.
  \answerto{$\partial L/\partial W$ is $256 \times 1024$, the shape of $W$,
  because $L$ is a scalar. $\partial\vect{z}/\partial\vect{x}$ is
  $256 \times 1024$ as well \dash{} one row per output, one column per input
  \dash{} and it is $W$ itself. The two agreeing here is a coincidence of
  this layer, not a rule.}
\item A classifier over $10$ classes predicts
  $\vect{p} = (\num{0.7}, \num{0.2}, \num{0.1}, 0, \ldots)$ and the true class
  is the second. Give the gradient with respect to the scores.
  \answerto{$\vect{p} - \vect{y} = (\num{0.7}, -\num{0.8}, \num{0.1}, 0,
  \ldots)$. The true class is pushed up by the $\num{0.8}$ of probability it
  is missing and every other class down by what it holds; the entries sum to
  zero, which is the $\softmax$ Jacobian's row sums showing through.}
\item Why can you not check a $\softmax$ Jacobian by checking that it is
  diagonal?
  \answerto{Because it is not diagonal, and that is the point of it: every
  output shares the denominator with every input, so raising one score lowers
  every other probability. The cheap check is the row sums, which must be
  zero because the probabilities sum to one for every input.}
\item Somebody reports that their gradient check passes for a squared error
  and fails for the same network with a cross-entropy loss, on a batch where
  the model is badly wrong. Give the first thing you would look at.
  \answerto{Whether the $\softmax$ and the cross-entropy are computed
  separately. The two-step route forms $-1/p_c$, and on a batch where the
  model is badly wrong $p_c$ underflows to zero. It is not a bug in the
  gradient check and it is not a bug in the network: it is two operations
  that must be one.}
\item Write out why $\partial L/\partial W = \vect{g}\,\vect{x}\T$ says
  something about memory as well as about arithmetic.
  \answerto{Because forming it needs $\vect{x}$, the layer's own input, which
  the forward pass produced and the backward pass consumes. So the
  activations have to be kept or recomputed, which is
  Program~\ref{prog:P16}'s checkpointing arithmetic \dash{} the identity and
  the memory bill are one fact seen twice.}
\end{testexercises}

\begin{furtherproblems}
\item Show that $\vect{p} - \vect{y}$ sums to zero for any prediction and any
  one-hot target, and say what that means for the scores.
  \answerto{The entries of $\vect{p}$ sum to $1$ and the entries of $\vect{y}$
  sum to $1$, so the difference sums to $0$. Gradient descent therefore moves
  the scores in directions that cancel: probability is redistributed rather
  than created, which is the $\softmax$'s row sums appearing at the level of
  the update. It also means adding a constant to every score changes nothing,
  which Program~\ref{prog:F07} proved from the other end.}
\item Derive the gradient of \code{log\_softmax} with respect to the scores,
  and say why an implementation prefers it to taking a logarithm afterwards.
  \answerto{$\partial(\ln p_i)/\partial z_j = \delta_{ij} - p_j$, which is the
  $\softmax$ Jacobian divided by $p_i$ row-wise \dash{} and notice that the
  reciprocal cancels here as well. An implementation prefers it because
  $\ln \softmax$ is $z_j - \logsumexp(\vect{z})$, which never forms a
  probability that could underflow before the logarithm sees it. It is the
  same argument as this program's section 5, one step earlier.}
\item If a paper's formula for a weight gradient is
  $\vect{x}\,\vect{g}\T$ where this book writes $\vect{g}\,\vect{x}\T$, what
  should you conclude?
  \answerto{Almost certainly that the paper uses denominator layout, in which
  case the two are the same statement. Check by looking at any \emph{other}
  derivative in the paper \dash{} whether its Jacobian of a linear map is the
  matrix or its transpose settles it in one line. What you should not do is
  transpose one formula to match and leave the rest of the derivation in the
  other convention.}
\item The $\softmax$ Jacobian is symmetric. Show it, and say whether that is
  a general property of a Jacobian.
  \answerto{$p_i(\delta_{ij} - p_j) = p_i\delta_{ij} - p_ip_j$, and both terms
  are unchanged when $i$ and $j$ swap. It is not general at all \dash{} a
  Jacobian need not even be square. It happens here because the map is a
  gradient of something: $\softmax$ is the gradient of $\logsumexp$, and
  Program~\ref{prog:P17} showed the derivative of a gradient is symmetric.}
\item Why does this program derive five identities rather than giving a table
  of them?
  \answerto{Because a table has to be trusted and a derivation can be
  checked, and every one of the five is three lines from a partial derivative
  taken one entry at a time. The reader who has done that once can derive the
  sixth; the reader who has a table has to find the sixth in somebody else's
  table, written in a convention nobody stated.}
\end{furtherproblems}
