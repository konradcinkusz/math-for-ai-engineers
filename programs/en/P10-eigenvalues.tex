\section{A direction the matrix only stretches}
\label{sec:P10-eigenvector}
\index{eigenvector}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:P09} closed on a promise. Rank counts how many directions
survive a matrix and the determinant measures how much of the space does
\dash{} and then it said the next question is sharper: not how much survives,
but \emph{which directions come through unturned}.

Here is what that means. Apply a matrix to a vector. In general the arrow that
comes out points somewhere else: the matrix has turned it as well as resized
it.

Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and apply it to
$(1, 1)$. What comes out, and what is remarkable about it?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$(3, 3)$. Which is $3 \times (1, 1)$ \dash{} the same direction, three times as
long.
\end{ansblock}

The matrix turned that arrow through no angle at all. It could have been
replaced, for that one input, by the number $3$.

A direction with that property is an \textbf{eigenvector} of the matrix, and
the number is its \textbf{eigenvalue}. Written down:
\[ Av = \lambda v, \qquad v \ne 0 \]
and the condition $v \ne 0$ is doing real work, because the zero vector
satisfies that equation for every $\lambda$ and would make the definition say
nothing.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The words are unhelpful and it is worth saying so once. \emph{Eigen} is German
for \emph{own}: an eigenvector is a direction the matrix owns, in the sense
that it does nothing to it but scale it.

What makes them worth finding is not that they are special cases. It is that
along one of them a matrix \dash{} an object with $n^{2}$ numbers in it \dash{}
behaves exactly like a single number. Everything hard about a matrix is that it
mixes the coordinates together. Along an eigenvector it does not.
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{p10-only-stretched}{What is left when a matrix turns a direction
through no angle.}{an eigenvector defined}

\begin{fr}
The same matrix has a second one. Try $(1, -1)$.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$(1, -1)$ again \dash{} eigenvalue $\val{p10.sym.lo}$.
\end{ansblock}

So $A$ has two eigenvectors, with eigenvalues $\val{p10.sym.hi}$ and
$\val{p10.sym.lo}$, and they point along $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$.

Two things about that pair are worth noticing now, and they will be the whole
of the next two sections. Look at the two directions and at how many of them
there are, and name both.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{They are \textbf{perpendicular}, and there are \textbf{two of them in a
two-dimensional space} \dash{} which by Program~\ref{prog:P04} makes them a
basis.}
Finding them is a small piece of algebra rather than a search.

$Av = \lambda v$ says $(A - \lambda I)v = 0$ with $v \ne 0$, so the matrix
$A - \lambda I$ sends something non-zero to zero. By
Program~\ref{prog:P08} that means it has a null space, and by
Program~\ref{prog:P09} that means its determinant is $0$.

So the eigenvalues are exactly the numbers $\lambda$ for which
$\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$.

Write that determinant out for a general $2 \times 2$ matrix
$\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$, and collect the powers of
$\lambda$. Program~\ref{prog:P09} gave you the formula you need.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$(a - \lambda)(d - \lambda) - bc = \lambda^{2} - (a + d)\lambda + (ad - bc)$.
\end{ansblock}

Which is $\lambda^{2} - (\operatorname{tr}A)\lambda + \det A$, where
$\operatorname{tr}A$, the \textbf{trace}, is the sum of the diagonal. Nothing
new was needed: two programs supplied the whole derivation.

Apply it to the worked matrix, whose trace is $\val{p10.sym.trace}$ and whose
determinant is $\val{p10.sym.det}$:
\[ \lambda^{2} - \val{p10.sym.trace}\lambda + \val{p10.sym.det} = 0 \]

Solve it. You have known how since Program~\ref{prog:F06}.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$\lambda = \val{p10.sym.hi}$ and $\lambda = \val{p10.sym.lo}$.
\end{ansblock}

Which are the two numbers the previous frames found by trying vectors, arrived
at without trying any.

And now look at that quadratic again, because it is telling you two things you
would otherwise have to remember separately. The roots of
$\lambda^{2} - t\lambda + d$ multiply to $d$ and add to $t$.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
So, for every square matrix:

\begin{itemize}
\item the \textbf{determinant is the product of the eigenvalues};
\item the \textbf{trace is their sum}.
\end{itemize}

Here $\val{p10.sym.hi} \times \val{p10.sym.lo} = \val{p10.sym.det}$ and
$\val{p10.sym.hi} + \val{p10.sym.lo} = \val{p10.sym.trace}$.

That first line is the promise Program~\ref{prog:P09} made on this program's
behalf, and the script keeps it as a gate rather than as a sentence: it
computes the determinant with Program~\ref{prog:P09}'s own routine and requires
it to equal the product. Break either program's arithmetic and the build says
so and names both.

It also settles a question Program~\ref{prog:P09} left standing, and in one
line rather than in a section. A matrix is singular exactly when its
determinant is zero; the determinant is the product of the eigenvalues; and a
product is zero exactly when one of its factors is.

So: when is a matrix singular?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Exactly when it has an eigenvalue of zero.
\end{ansblock}

And that eigenvalue's eigenvector is precisely a vector the matrix sends to
zero \dash{} which is Program~\ref{prog:P08}'s null space, arriving for the
third time under a third name.

Three programs, three routes, one condition. Program~\ref{prog:P08} reached it
by counting directions, Program~\ref{prog:P09} by measuring volume, and this
one by multiplying a list of numbers.
\end{fr}

\section{Three ways the promise fails}
\label{sec:P10-failures}
\index{eigenvector!not orthogonal}

\begin{fr}
The worked matrix gave two perpendicular eigenvectors spanning the plane, and
it would be convenient if that were the general case.

It is not, and this section is the three ways it fails, because every one of
them is a real matrix an engineer meets.

Start with the one almost everybody gets wrong. Here is
$N = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$. Its eigenvalues are
$\val{p10.nonsym.hi}$ and $\val{p10.nonsym.lo}$, with eigenvectors $(1, 1)$ and
$(1, 0)$.

Are those two perpendicular? Answer before reading on.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
No. Their dot product is $1$, not $0$, and they meet at
$\val{p10.nonsym.angle}$ degrees.
\end{ansblock}

Check it: $(1,1) \cdot (1,0) = 1$. Both vectors have length at least $1$, so by
Program~\ref{prog:P05} the cosine of the angle between them is
$1/\sqrt{2}$, and that is $\val{p10.nonsym.angle}$ degrees.

\transcript{p10-orthogonal}

Two matrices, the same computation, run rather than argued: zero for the
symmetric one, one for this one.
\end{fr}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{Eigenvectors are orthogonal.}}

They are for a \textbf{symmetric} matrix, and that case is common enough to
have taught the habit \dash{} a covariance matrix is symmetric, a Gram matrix
is symmetric, the Hessian of a smooth function is symmetric.

The matrices an engineer actually differentiates and multiplies mostly are not.
An attention matrix is not symmetric. A transition matrix is not symmetric. A
Jacobian is not symmetric. A weight matrix is not symmetric, and is usually not
even square.

The reasoning that produces the error is sound as far as it goes: every
example anybody works by hand is symmetric, because those are the ones with
tidy answers. The habit is acquired from a biased sample.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{fr}
The second failure is worse than non-perpendicular. Take a quarter turn,
\[ R = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \]
which Program~\ref{prog:P09} showed has determinant $1$ and turns every arrow
through a right angle.

Which directions does it leave pointing the way they came in?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
None. Every direction is turned through a right angle, so none survives.
\end{ansblock}

The algebra agrees and says it more usefully. The trace is $0$ and the
determinant is $1$, so the characteristic equation is $\lambda^{2} + 1 = 0$,
which has no real solution at all.

So say what a library is telling you when it hands back a complex eigenvalue
for a real matrix.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{That the map rotates. A real matrix can have \textbf{no real eigenvalue};
what it has instead is a pair of complex ones, and that pair is the algebra's
way of saying so \dash{} it is not an error.}
The third failure is the subtlest, and it is Program~\ref{prog:P09}'s shear
again:
\[ S = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \]

Its trace is $2$ and its determinant is $1$, so $\lambda^{2} - 2\lambda + 1 =
(\lambda - 1)^{2}$ and the only eigenvalue is $1$, twice over.

So the algebra has already said something the previous two failures did not.
The characteristic equation has a real root, and it has only one of them,
where a $2 \times 2$ matrix has room for two. That repetition is the whole of
what makes this case different, and it is visible before any eigenvector is
computed.

How many independent directions does it leave unturned?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
One. The horizontal.
\end{ansblock}

$S$ sends $(1, 0)$ to $(1, 0)$ and nothing else survives: any vector with a
non-zero second component gets slid sideways.

So an eigenvalue can arrive twice and bring only one direction with it. A
matrix like that is called \textbf{defective}, and it has no basis of
eigenvectors at all \dash{} which is a real limitation, because almost
everything anybody does with eigenvectors starts by writing a vector in terms
of them.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Three failures, and it is worth having them as a list, because the theorem in
the next section is the statement that all three are impossible at once for one
kind of matrix.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Failure} & \textbf{Example} \\
\midrule
eigenvectors not perpendicular & $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$,
  at $\val{p10.nonsym.angle}$ degrees \\
no real eigenvalue at all & a quarter turn \\
too few directions for the eigenvalues & the shear \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

Every one of those three is a matrix you will meet. None of them is exotic.
\end{fr}

\section{The spectral theorem}
\label{sec:P10-spectral}
\index{spectral theorem}

\begin{fr}
One condition rules out all three failures at once, and it is a condition you
can check by looking.

\textbf{A symmetric matrix} \dash{} one with $A\T = A$, so that the entry in
row $i$ column $j$ equals the entry in row $j$ column $i$ \dash{} has a
\textbf{full set of real eigenvalues, and its eigenvectors can be chosen
perpendicular to one another}.

That is the \emph{spectral theorem}. It is why the worked matrix of section 1
behaved so well: it is symmetric, and nothing about it was a coincidence.
\end{fr}

\begin{rigourbox}
Stated and used here, not proved. The proof is short but it needs two things
this book does not have: that a real symmetric matrix's characteristic
polynomial has only real roots, which is easiest through complex numbers, and
an induction on dimension. Any first course in linear algebra has it.

What is worth carrying instead is where the perpendicularity comes from, in
one line. If $Av = \lambda v$ and $Aw = \mu w$ with $\lambda \ne \mu$, then
$\lambda(v \cdot w) = (Av) \cdot w = v \cdot (Aw) = \mu(v \cdot w)$, using
$A\T = A$ in the middle step. Two different numbers times the same quantity are
equal, so that quantity is zero.
\end{rigourbox}

\begin{fr}
That middle step is the whole of it and it deserves a second look, because it
is the only place symmetry is used.

$(Av) \cdot w = v \cdot (Aw)$ says you may move the matrix from one side of a
dot product to the other. It is true exactly when $A\T = A$, and it fails
otherwise \dash{} which is precisely why the non-symmetric matrix two sections
ago had eigenvectors at $\val{p10.nonsym.angle}$ degrees.

So symmetry is not a convenience that makes the sums nicer. It is the property
the conclusion is made of.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
A theorem stated and not proved is worth checking, and this one can be checked
\emph{exactly} rather than to a tolerance \dash{} which matters, because the
claim contains the word \emph{exactly}.

The construction is Program~\ref{prog:P09}'s. That program built a rotation out
of the $3$--$4$--$5$ triangle whose entries are all fractions:
$Q = \begin{pmatrix} 3/5 & -4/5 \\ 4/5 & 3/5 \end{pmatrix}$, orthogonal, with
$Q\T Q = I$ exactly.

The reason for building the example rather than picking one is worth a
sentence. Go the usual way round \dash{} choose a symmetric matrix and solve for
its eigenvalues \dash{} and the discriminant is almost never a perfect square,
so the answers are irrational and there is nothing exact left to compare. Going
backwards from the answer is what keeps every quantity a fraction.

Now take any diagonal matrix $D$ of two fractions and form $Q D Q\T$. What can
you say about that matrix before computing a single entry?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
It is symmetric: $(QDQ\T)\T = Q D\T Q\T = Q D Q\T$, because $D$ is diagonal.
\end{ansblock}

And more than that: its eigenvalues \emph{are} $D$'s two entries and its
eigenvectors \emph{are} $Q$'s two columns, which you can check in one line.
Apply $QDQ\T$ to the first column of $Q$; the $Q\T$ picks out $(1,0)$ because
the columns are orthonormal, $D$ multiplies it by its first entry, and $Q$ puts
it back.

So this is a rational symmetric matrix whose answers are known \textbf{by
construction rather than found by a solver} \dash{} which is what makes the
check worth running. On $\val{p10.spectral.trials}$ such matrices the script
confirms every eigenvalue, every eigenvector and every right angle, over
fractions, with no rounding anywhere.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
It is worth being clear about what those $\val{p10.spectral.trials}$ trials are
and are not, because Program~\ref{prog:P04} had to say the same thing about its
own random draws.

They are not evidence for the theorem. A search that found a counterexample
would have refuted a proof, and the sensible conclusion would have been that
the code was wrong.

\textbf{They are testing the code}, and they are worth the lines for a second
reason: they are the only place in this book where a claim containing
\emph{exactly} is checked with arithmetic that is itself exact. A float test
would have had to pick a tolerance, and a tolerance is precisely what the word
\emph{exactly} refuses.

Now the payoff, and you have both halves of it. A full orthogonal set of
eigenvectors is, in Program~\ref{prog:P04}'s vocabulary, one thing, and in
Program~\ref{prog:P09}'s it is another. Name both.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
A \textbf{basis} \dash{} and an \textbf{orthogonal change of basis}, which
costs nothing and loses nothing.
\end{ansblock}

That is the reason symmetric matrices are everywhere in this subject.

So for a symmetric matrix there is a coordinate system in which the matrix is
\emph{diagonal}: it does nothing but scale each axis by its own number. Every
hard thing a matrix does \dash{} mixing the coordinates together \dash{}
disappears in the right coordinates, and the eigenvectors are the right
coordinates.
\end{fr}

\section{A bowl, a saddle or a ridge}
\label{sec:P10-forms}
\index{quadratic form}

\begin{fr}
Now the object the eigenvalues turn out to describe.

A \textbf{quadratic form} is what you get by feeding a vector into a matrix and
then dotting the result back with the vector it came from:
\[ q(x) = x \cdot Ax = x\T A x \]
For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and $x = (x_1, x_2)$
that is $2x_1^{2} + 2x_1x_2 + 2x_2^{2}$: every term has total degree two, which
is where the name comes from.

It takes a vector and returns one number, so it has a value in every direction
you can leave the origin along, and the question worth asking is what those
values look like taken together.

But first a question that decides whether the last section applies at all.
$x\T Ax$ is a single number, so it equals its own transpose. What does that
force about the part of $A$ that is \emph{not} symmetric?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
It can never affect $q$ at all.
\end{ansblock}

$x\T A x = (x\T A x)\T = x\T A\T x$, so $x\T A x = x\T \big(\frac{A + A\T}{2}
\big) x$.

Any quadratic form can therefore be written with a symmetric matrix, and from
here they always are.

The Hessian, a covariance and a Gram matrix are all symmetric. Say why that is
one fact rather than three.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{They are all quadratic forms in disguise, and the non-symmetric part of
any of them was never doing anything.}
Take a symmetric $A$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$, and write
$x$ in the eigenvector basis of the previous section, so that $x = c_1v_1 +
c_2v_2$.

Writing $x$ that way is allowed for exactly the reason the previous section
gave: the eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix are a basis, so every $x$ has
coordinates in them and no vector is left out. That step is silent in most
treatments and it is the one carrying the spectral theorem.

Then $Ax = \lambda_1c_1v_1 + \lambda_2c_2v_2$, the $v$'s are perpendicular unit
vectors, and the whole form collapses to
\[ q(x) = \lambda_1c_1^{2} + \lambda_2c_2^{2} \]

There are no cross terms left. In the right coordinates a quadratic form is a
weighted sum of squares, and the weights are the eigenvalues.

Now the question the section exists for. Suppose both eigenvalues are positive.
What can $q(x)$ be, for $x \ne 0$?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Positive. Always, in every direction.
\end{ansblock}

Two positive numbers times two squares, not both zero. There is nothing to
check and no direction to try: the coordinates in which the matrix is diagonal
make the answer visible.

A form with that property is \textbf{positive definite}, and its matrix is
called positive definite too. Note what kind of claim it is \dash{} a statement
about \emph{every} direction, of which there are infinitely many, settled by
looking at two numbers.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
And the rest of the classification falls out of the same expression. Fill in
the shape each sign pattern gives.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}llX@{}}
\toprule
$\lambda_1$ & $\lambda_2$ & \textbf{the form} \\
\midrule
$+$ & $+$ & positive definite: a bowl, up in every direction \\
$+$ & $-$ & \blank \\
$+$ & $0$ & \blank \\
$-$ & $-$ & negative definite: a dome, down in every direction \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$+,-$ is a \emph{saddle}: up along one eigenvector, down along the other.
$+,0$ is a \emph{ridge}: up one way and dead flat along the other.
\end{ansblock}

The script classifies four such forms from their eigenvalues alone and then
samples each in $\val{p10.form.dirs}$ directions round the circle to confirm
the sign pattern. The bowl is positive in every one of them, which is what
\emph{definite} is a claim about.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The ridge is worth one more frame, because sampling it taught the script's
author something and the frame would be poorer without it.

The assertion first written was that the smallest sampled value of a
semi-definite form is zero. It failed immediately, and it deserved to: the flat
direction is \emph{one} direction out of infinitely many, and
$\val{p10.form.dirs}$ samples will never land on it. The smallest sampled value
is small and positive, and no amount of extra sampling changes that.

\textbf{You cannot find the flat direction by looking for it.} You find it by
computing an eigenvector, which is exactly the argument for computing them at
all \dash{} and the script now asserts non-negativity by sampling and the zero
by evaluating exactly where the construction says the flat direction is.
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{p10-bowl-or-saddle}{Why two numbers settle the shape without
trying any direction.}{eigenvalues classify a form}

\begin{fr}
That zero eigenvalue is not a new object either.

Its eigenvector $v$ satisfies $Av = 0v = 0$, so $v$ is in the null space, so
$A$ is singular, so $\det A = 0$.

Four descriptions are now on the table: \enquote{has a zero eigenvalue},
\enquote{is singular}, \enquote{has a non-trivial null space} and \enquote{has
a flat direction in its quadratic form}. Say how many conditions that is.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{\textbf{One, said four ways} \dash{} by Program~\ref{prog:P08}, by
Program~\ref{prog:P09}, and twice here. A reader who meets one of the four in
an error message now knows the other three.}
One more piece of vocabulary, and then the section's payoff.

\textbf{Positive semi-definite} means every eigenvalue is $\ge 0$ \dash{} so
the form is never negative, and may be zero in some directions. The bowl and
the ridge are both positive semi-definite; only the bowl is positive definite.

The distinction sounds pedantic and is not. A positive definite matrix is
invertible, because none of its eigenvalues is zero. A positive semi-definite
one need not be. The word \emph{semi} is carrying that entire difference.
\end{fr}

\section{The shape of something}
\label{sec:P10-shape}
\index{eigenvalue!spectral norm}

\begin{fr}
Three things in this subject are a symmetric matrix that somebody wants the
shape of, and this section is all three. They are the same mathematics and
almost nobody meets them as the same mathematics.

Start with the simplest. A layer multiplies a vector by a matrix. By how much
can it lengthen one, at most, over every input there is?

Note what kind of question that is. Not \emph{how much does this layer usually
stretch a vector}, which is a fact about the inputs it happens to receive, but
\emph{how much could it}, which is a fact about the layer alone. The second is
the one worth having, because it holds for the batch nobody has sent yet.

It is also the only one of the two that composes. Stack two layers and the
worst cases multiply, so a bound on each gives a bound on the pair; average
behaviour gives nothing of the sort, because the second layer does not receive
an average input from the first.

For a symmetric $A$ you have the answer already: write $x$ in the eigenvector
basis and $Ax$ has components $\lambda_ic_i$. Say what the largest possible
lengthening is, and in which direction it happens.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
The largest $\lvert\lambda\rvert$, in the direction of its eigenvector.
\end{ansblock}

That value has a name \dash{} the \textbf{spectral norm} \dash{} and a
use. It is the number $L$ with $\lVert Ax \rVert \le L\lVert x \rVert$ for
every $x$, which is to say it is the factor by which the layer can amplify, in
the worst case, over all inputs including the ones nobody has tried.

\begin{warning}
\textbf{That the two coincide is a fact about a symmetric matrix, not about
every matrix.} Program~\ref{prog:P09}'s shear
$\left(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right)$ has both
eigenvalues equal to $1$, so its largest $\lvert\lambda\rvert$ is $1$ \dash{}
and it sends $(0, 1)$ to $(1, 1)$, so it amplifies by at least $\sqrt{2}$. Its
spectral norm is $\num{1.618}$, over half again what the eigenvalues report.
In general the spectral norm is the largest \emph{singular} value, and
Program~\ref{prog:P11} builds those; here it is the largest
$\lvert\lambda\rvert$ because the matrix is symmetric, which is exactly the
case the spectral theorem covers.
\end{warning}

The script takes a symmetric matrix with eigenvalues $\val{p10.norm.max}$ and
$-\val{p10.norm.min}$ and measures the stretch in $\val{p10.norm.dirs}$
directions round the circle. The largest is $\val{p10.norm.max}$, the smallest
is $\val{p10.norm.min}$, and the direction the largest is attained in is the
top eigenvector \dash{} all three asserted.

Note the sweep is not the proof. It is there because the claim is about every
direction, and a reader who tries a few is entitled to see the maximum sitting
where the theory puts it.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The second is the one the rest of the book will spend.

A loss surface near a minimum looks like a quadratic form \dash{} that is
Program~\ref{prog:P17}'s statement and this program is not making it. What this
program can say is what a quadratic form \emph{looks like}, and the answer is
an ellipse.

The set of points where a positive definite form takes one fixed value is an
ellipse whose axes lie along the eigenvectors. Longer along the small
eigenvalue, shorter along the large one, because a large eigenvalue means the
value climbs quickly and so the level set is reached sooner.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Now a number worth predicting before it is given.

A basin has eigenvalues $\val{p10.basin.hi}$ and $\val{p10.basin.lo}$ \dash{} a
ratio of $\val{p10.basin.eigratio}$. Roughly how many times longer than wide is
its level ellipse?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$\val{p10.basin.axisratio}$ times. The \emph{square root} of
$\val{p10.basin.eigratio}$, not $\val{p10.basin.eigratio}$.
\end{ansblock}

The half-axis along an eigenvector is $1/\sqrt{\lambda}$, because the form
carries the coordinate \emph{squared}. So the ratio of the axes is the square
root of the ratio of the eigenvalues, and the script measures both by sweeping
the level set rather than quoting the formula.

Most people answer $\val{p10.basin.eigratio}$. The square root is a large
correction in the reassuring direction, and it is the reason a basin with a
hundredfold spread in curvature still looks merely oval rather than like a
crack in the floor.
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{p10-ellipse-axes}{The level set of a positive definite form, and
what one ratio says about it.}{eigenvalues as the axes of a basin}

\begin{fr}
That ratio is where three pieces of workshop vocabulary become one statement,
and this is the sentence the program was built to reach.

A \emph{ravine} is a basin whose eigenvalue ratio is large. A \emph{sharp
minimum} is one whose largest eigenvalue is large. \emph{The learning rate is
too high} is a claim about the largest eigenvalue and the step size together.
Three complaints, one matrix, three different numbers read off it.

This program stops there, and deliberately. Turning the third into an
inequality needs the second-order Taylor expansion, which is
Program~\ref{prog:P17}'s, and living with the first is what
Program~\ref{prog:P20}'s optimisers are for. What is collected here is the
shape.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The third symmetric matrix is the one with the biggest name attached to it.

Take some data, subtract the mean from each column so it is centred, and form
$C = \frac{1}{n}X\T X$. That is the \textbf{covariance matrix}, and
Program~\ref{prog:P24} is where it gets its meaning. Two facts about its
\emph{shape} are all this program needs, and both follow from that one line.

Say what they are \dash{} you have everything required.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
It is symmetric, and it is positive semi-definite.
\end{ansblock}

Symmetric because $C\T = \frac{1}{n}(X\T X)\T = \frac{1}{n}X\T X = C$.

Positive semi-definite because for any direction $v$,
\[ v\T C v = \tfrac{1}{n}v\T X\T X v = \tfrac{1}{n}\lVert Xv \rVert^{2} \ge 0 \]
which is a sum of squares and cannot be negative. The script checks that
identity, not merely the inequality, at two thousand directions over fractions.

So the spectral theorem applies, every eigenvalue is real and non-negative, and
the eigenvectors are perpendicular. All of that is now known about a matrix
whose \emph{meaning} the book has not yet given.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
And $v\T Cv$ is not an arbitrary quantity. The line above shows it is
$\frac{1}{n}\lVert Xv \rVert^{2}$, the average squared length of the data's
shadow on the direction $v$.

Say what that measures about the data, in ordinary words \dash{} and then what
the top eigenvector of $C$ must therefore be.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
How spread out the data looks from that direction \dash{} so the top
eigenvector is the direction it is most spread out along.
\end{ansblock}

That is \textbf{principal component analysis}, and it is not an algorithm. It
is the observation that a question about spread and a question about
eigenvectors are the same question.

Measured, on $\val{p10.pca.n}$ points, because the claim deserves a number.

The script sweeps $\val{p10.pca.dirs}$ directions, computes the spread in each,
and finds the largest agrees with the top eigenvalue of $C$. That direction
carries $\val{p10.pca.top.pct}\%$ of the total spread, leaving
$\val{p10.pca.rest.pct}\%$ for the other one.

Two ways got to that answer: try twenty thousand directions, or solve a
quadratic. They agree. Say which one still works in seven hundred dimensions.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{PCA found the important directions.}}

It found the directions of greatest \emph{variance}, which is a fact about the
units the columns happen to be in. Measure one feature in metres and the same
feature in millimetres and its variance changes by a factor of a million, so
the top component changes with it.

That is why standardising the columns first is not a nicety, and why
\enquote{PCA on my feature matrix} is an incomplete sentence: it needs to say
whether the data was centred, which the derivation above assumed outright, and
whether it was scaled, which the derivation says nothing about at all.

Nothing here is wrong with the mathematics. The matrix answers exactly the
question it was asked, and the question contained the units.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Solving for the eigenvector. Sweeping directions samples a sphere, and no
feasible number of samples covers one in seven hundred dimensions \dash{}
where an eigenvector is the answer to an equation whatever the dimension is.}
The eigenvalues turned out to be the shape of everything this section touched:
the most a layer can stretch a vector, how elongated a basin is, and which way
a cloud of data lies.

That is one idea used three times, and the reason it is one idea is the
spectral theorem \dash{} every matrix in this section was symmetric, so every
one of them had perpendicular eigenvectors and a coordinate system in which it
was merely a list of numbers.

Program~\ref{prog:P11} asks what is left when the matrix is \emph{not}
symmetric, and not even square. The answer keeps almost all of this: every
matrix, of any shape, turns out to be a rotation, then a stretch along
perpendicular axes, then another rotation \dash{} so the picture survives even
where the theorem does not, and the numbers it produces are the ones this
program's non-symmetric examples had no eigenvalues to give.
\end{fr}

\begin{summarybox}
\sumitem{1--3}{\result{\textbf{An eigenvector is a direction the matrix only
  stretches}, $Av = \lambda v$ with $v \ne 0$, and the point of one is that
  along it a matrix with $n^{2}$ numbers in it behaves like a single number.
  Everything hard about a matrix is that it mixes the coordinates together;
  along an eigenvector it does not}.}
\sumitem{4--5}{\result{The worked matrix has two, at $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$ with
  eigenvalues $\val{p10.sym.hi}$ and $\val{p10.sym.lo}$ \dash{} and they are
  \textbf{perpendicular}, and there are \textbf{two of them in two
  dimensions}, which by Program~\ref{prog:P04} makes them a basis. Neither is
  a coincidence and the next two sections are why}.}
\sumitem{6--8}{\result{$Av = \lambda v$ means $A - \lambda I$ sends something non-zero
  to zero, so \textbf{the eigenvalues are the roots of
  $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$}, which is
  $\lambda^{2} - (\operatorname{tr}A)\lambda + \det A$ for a $2 \times 2$.
  Nothing new was needed: Program~\ref{prog:P08} supplied the null space and
  Program~\ref{prog:P09} the determinant}.}
\sumitem{9}{\result{\textbf{The determinant is the product of the eigenvalues and the
  trace is their sum}, straight off that quadratic. The first is the promise
  Program~\ref{prog:P09} made on this program's behalf, and it is kept as a
  gate: the script computes the determinant with that program's own routine
  and requires it to equal the product}.}
\sumitem{10}{\result{So \textbf{singular, zero eigenvalue and non-trivial null space
  are one condition}, because a product is zero exactly when a factor is.
  Three programs, three routes \dash{} counting directions, measuring volume,
  multiplying a list}.}
\sumitem{11--12}{\result{\textbf{Eigenvectors are perpendicular only for a symmetric
  matrix.} $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvectors at
  $\val{p10.nonsym.angle}$ degrees, and attention matrices, transition
  matrices and Jacobians are none of them symmetric. The habit comes from a
  biased sample: the tidy hand-worked examples are the symmetric ones}.}
\sumitem{13--17}{\result{Two further failures. \textbf{A quarter turn has no real
  eigenvalue at all}, because nothing survives a right-angle turn \dash{} a
  complex eigenvalue is the algebra saying \emph{this rotates}. And
  \textbf{the shear has one eigenvalue twice but only one direction}, which is
  what \emph{defective} means}.}
\sumitem{18--19}{\result{\textbf{The spectral theorem}: a symmetric matrix has real
  eigenvalues and perpendicular eigenvectors. Stated, not proved \dash{} but
  the perpendicularity is one line, and it uses $A\T = A$ exactly once, to
  move the matrix across a dot product. \textbf{Symmetry is not a convenience;
  it is what the conclusion is made of.}}}
\sumitem{20--22}{Checked \emph{exactly} on $\val{p10.spectral.trials}$
  matrices built as $QDQ\T$ from Program~\ref{prog:P09}'s rational rotation,
  so the answers are known by construction rather than found by a solver. As
  in Program~\ref{prog:P04}, \textbf{the trials are testing the code} \dash{}
  and a float test would have needed a tolerance, which is what the word
  \emph{exactly} refuses.}
\sumitem{23}{\result{The payoff: a full orthogonal set of eigenvectors is a basis and
  an orthogonal change of basis, so \textbf{a symmetric matrix is diagonal in
  the right coordinates}, and the eigenvectors are the right coordinates}.}
\sumitem{24--25}{\result{A \textbf{quadratic form} is $x\T Ax$, and \textbf{only the
  symmetric part of $A$ can affect it} \dash{} which is why a Hessian, a
  covariance and a Gram matrix are all symmetric, and why the spectral theorem
  always applies to one}.}
\sumitem{26--27}{\result{In the eigenvector basis the form collapses to
  $\lambda_1c_1^{2} + \lambda_2c_2^{2}$, so two positive eigenvalues make it
  positive in every direction. That is \textbf{positive definite}: a claim
  about infinitely many directions, settled by looking at two numbers}.}
\sumitem{28--29}{\result{$+,-$ is a saddle and $+,0$ a ridge, confirmed in
  $\val{p10.form.dirs}$ directions each. \textbf{The signs of the eigenvalues
  settle the whole shape without trying a single direction.}}}
\sumitem{30}{\result{\textbf{You cannot find a flat direction by sampling.} The
  assertion that a semi-definite form's sampled minimum is zero failed at
  once, because the flat direction is one direction out of infinitely many.
  That is the argument for computing eigenvectors rather than trying
  directions}.}
\sumitem{31--32}{\result{\enquote{Zero eigenvalue}, \enquote{singular}, \enquote{null
  space} and \enquote{flat direction} are \textbf{one condition said four
  ways}. And \emph{semi}-definite allows a zero, so a positive definite matrix
  is invertible and a positive semi-definite one need not be \dash{} the word
  \emph{semi} carries that entire difference}.}
\sumitem{33--34}{\result{The spectral norm is the factor by which a layer can
  amplify in the worst case over every input including the untried ones, and
  \textbf{for a SYMMETRIC matrix it is the largest $\lvert\lambda\rvert$}
  \dash{} in general it is the largest singular value, which is
  Program~\ref{prog:P11}'s. Over $\val{p10.norm.dirs}$ directions: largest
  $\val{p10.norm.max}$, smallest $\val{p10.norm.min}$, attained at the top
  eigenvector}.}
\sumitem{35--37}{\result{A level set of a positive definite form is an ellipse with
  its axes along the eigenvectors, and \textbf{the ratio of the axes is the
  SQUARE ROOT of the ratio of the eigenvalues} \dash{}
  $\val{p10.basin.axisratio}$ for a ratio of $\val{p10.basin.eigratio}$, where
  most people answer $\val{p10.basin.eigratio}$}.}
\sumitem{38}{\result{\enquote{Ravine}, \enquote{sharp minimum} and \enquote{the
  learning rate is too high} are \textbf{three numbers read off one matrix}.} The shape is collected here; the inequality is Program~\ref{prog:P17}'s and
  the optimisers are Program~\ref{prog:P20}'s.}
\sumitem{39--40}{\result{$C = \frac{1}{n}X\T X$ is \textbf{symmetric and positive
  semi-definite}, both proved from that one line rather than borrowed, so the
  spectral theorem applies to a matrix whose \emph{meaning} is
  Program~\ref{prog:P24}'s and has not been given yet}.}
\sumitem{41--43}{\result{$v\T Cv$ is the spread of the data along $v$, so \textbf{the
  direction of greatest spread is the top eigenvector}. That is PCA, an
  observation rather than an algorithm: $\val{p10.pca.top.pct}\%$ of the
  spread on one direction, found two ways that agree, only one of which
  survives seven hundred dimensions}.}
\end{summarybox}

\begin{testexercises}
\item A $2 \times 2$ matrix has trace $7$ and determinant $12$. Give its
  eigenvalues.
  \answerto{$\lambda^{2} - 7\lambda + 12 = (\lambda - 3)(\lambda - 4)$, so $3$
  and $4$. Check: they multiply to $12$ and add to $7$.}
\item $A$ is $3 \times 3$ with eigenvalues $2$, $0$ and $-5$. Give
  $\det A$, and say whether $A$ has an inverse.
  \answerto{$\det A = 2 \times 0 \times (-5) = 0$, so no inverse. The zero
  eigenvalue's eigenvector is a non-zero vector sent to zero, which is exactly
  what stops the map being undoable.}
\item Give a $2 \times 2$ matrix whose eigenvectors are not perpendicular, and
  say why yours works.
  \answerto{Any non-symmetric one with two distinct real eigenvalues will do,
  for example the triangular matrix with rows $(2, 1)$ and $(0, 3)$: its
  eigenvectors are $(1,1)$ and $(1,0)$, whose dot product is $1$. The
  perpendicularity argument needs $(Av)\cdot w = v\cdot(Aw)$, which needs
  $A\T = A$.}
\item A symmetric matrix has eigenvalues $9$ and $4$. Give the spectral norm
  \dash{} and say which word in this sentence the answer depends on,
  and the ratio of the axes of the ellipse $x\T Ax = 1$.
  \answerto{The spectral norm is $9$, and the word it depends on is
  \emph{symmetric}: for a symmetric matrix the largest $\lvert\lambda\rvert$
  is the spectral norm, and for anything else it is the largest singular value
  and can be strictly larger. The axis ratio is
  $\sqrt{9/4} = \frac{3}{2}$, because the half-axes go as $1/\sqrt{\lambda}$
  \dash{} not $9/4$.}
\item Is $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ positive definite?
  Answer from the trace and the determinant alone.
  \answerto{No. The determinant is $1 - 9 = -8$, which is the product of the
  eigenvalues, so one of them is negative and the form is a saddle. Positive
  definite needs both positive, hence a positive determinant \emph{and} a
  positive trace.}
\item Data is measured with one column in metres and another in millimetres.
  Say what PCA will report and why that is not a fault in PCA.
  \answerto{The top component will lie almost entirely along the millimetre
  column, because that column's variance is a million times larger for reasons
  that have nothing to do with the data. PCA answers the question it was asked
  \dash{} which direction has the greatest variance \dash{} and the units were
  part of the question. Standardise the columns first.}
\end{testexercises}

\begin{furtherproblems}
\item Show that if $v$ is an eigenvector of $A$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$, then
  $v$ is an eigenvector of $A^{2}$ with eigenvalue $\lambda^{2}$.
  \answerto{$A^{2}v = A(Av) = A(\lambda v) = \lambda(Av) = \lambda^{2}v$, using
  only Program~\ref{prog:P06}'s composition. It follows that a symmetric matrix
  with every $\lvert\lambda\rvert < 1$ has $A^{k} \to 0$, which is why the
  spectral norm predicts whether a repeated map dies away.}
\item An invertible $A$ has eigenvalue $\lambda$ with eigenvector $v$. Give an
  eigenvalue of $A^{-1}$.
  \answerto{$1/\lambda$, with the same $v$: apply $A^{-1}$ to
  $Av = \lambda v$ and divide. Note $\lambda \ne 0$ is guaranteed, because a
  zero eigenvalue would have made $A$ singular.}
\item Why can a symmetric matrix never be defective?
  \answerto{Because the spectral theorem gives it a full set of perpendicular
  eigenvectors, and perpendicular non-zero vectors are independent by
  Program~\ref{prog:P04}. Defective means too few independent directions, which
  the theorem forbids.}
\item A matrix is positive definite. Say what that makes its determinant, and
  whether the converse holds.
  \answerto{Positive, because it is the product of positive eigenvalues. The
  converse fails in even dimensions: two negative eigenvalues also give a
  positive determinant, and the matrix with rows $(-1, 0)$ and $(0, -1)$ is a
  dome rather than a bowl.}
\item Program~\ref{prog:P09} showed a rotation has determinant $1$. Use the
  product rule for determinants to say what the eigenvalues of a
  two-dimensional rotation must multiply to, and reconcile that with the
  section-2 finding that it has none.
  \answerto{They must multiply to $1$. There are no real ones, so the pair is
  complex, and a complex pair of the form $\cos\theta \pm i\sin\theta$
  multiplies to $\cos^{2}\theta + \sin^{2}\theta = 1$ \dash{}
  Program~\ref{prog:F08}'s identity, arriving where it was least expected.}
\end{furtherproblems}
