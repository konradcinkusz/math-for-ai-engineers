\section{One step, and six programs}
\label{sec:P33-one-step}
\index{training run!one step of}

\begin{fr}
Everything in this program has been derived somewhere else in this book. That
is Part IX's contract, and this program is where it is easiest to check,
because a training step is short enough to write out.

Before reading on: a step takes a batch of examples and returns a changed set
of weights. Name the things that happen in between \dash{} there are six, and
you have met every one of them.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Compute a loss. Differentiate it. Turn the gradient into a step. Keep the
arithmetic representable. Decide how far to go. And do all of it on a
\emph{sample} of the data rather than on the data.
\end{ansblock}

Six pieces, and not one of them is new here.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Take them in order and name the owner of each.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The loss.} Program~\ref{prog:P26}: it is the negative
  log-likelihood of the observed tokens, so training a language model is
  maximum likelihood and was never anything else. The number itself is
  Program~\ref{prog:F02}'s, at $\val{p33.loss.nats}$ nats.
\item \textbf{Its gradient.} Program~\ref{prog:P18}: through a $\softmax$ and
  a cross-entropy together it is $\vect{p} - \vect{y}$, and the two-step route
  that computes them separately is a different function rather than a slower
  one.
\item \textbf{The step.} Program~\ref{prog:P20} for the update, with
  Program~\ref{prog:F04}'s exponential moving average and its bias correction
  inside it.
\item \textbf{The arithmetic.} Program~\ref{prog:P01}: the gap between
  representable numbers grows with magnitude, so a contribution below half
  the gap at the running total moves nothing at all.
\item \textbf{How far.} Program~\ref{prog:P17}:
  $\eta < 2/\lambda_{\max}$, which is a statement about a geometric sequence
  with nothing about networks in it.
\item \textbf{The sample.} Program~\ref{prog:P21}: a minibatch gradient is an
  estimator, and almost every complaint about training is a statement about
  its variance.
\end{itemize}
\end{ansblock}
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{p33-six-pieces}{%
  One step of one training run, with the program that owns each piece written
  beside it. Part IX assembles; it does not derive.}{%
  a step, and who owns each piece}

\begin{fr}
Two of those carry numbers this program is about to use, so put them on the
page now.

Program~\ref{prog:P17}'s boundary on the bowl it and Program~\ref{prog:P10}
share is $\eta < \val{p33.eta.bound}$, and the step size that gets there
fastest is $\val{p33.eta.best}$. By what fraction do the best step size and
the fatal one differ?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p33.eta.margin}$ per cent}
That is the whole band a schedule lives in. Below it the run is slower than
it needs to be; above it the run does not get worse, it leaves.

\begin{aibox}
It is also why a learning-rate warm-up exists. Early in training the curvature
is not the curvature the schedule was tuned against, so the same $\eta$ can
sit on the wrong side of a boundary that has moved. Program~\ref{prog:P20}
measured the warm-up's own factor; what this frame adds is that the margin it
is protecting is a few per cent wide.
\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The second number is the noise. Program~\ref{prog:P21} measured a minibatch
gradient's spread directly; here the quantity is the \emph{loss}, and it is a
mean over the batch, so Program~\ref{prog:P25}'s rate applies unchanged.

Take a per-example loss with a spread of $\val{p33.sigma.token}$ \dash{}
which is Program~\ref{prog:P19}'s widest measured row \dash{} and a batch of
$\val{p33.batch}$. What is the spread of the number the dashboard prints?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p33.sigma.step}$}
$\sigma/\sqrt{B}$, which is Program~\ref{prog:P25} §1 and needs nothing else.

Hold onto it. Every claim in the middle of this program is that number
against the size of the thing you are trying to see.
\end{fr}

\section{Which of the six fail silently}
\label{sec:P33-silent}
\index{training run!silent failures}

\begin{fr}
Now the question the assembly makes available and no one of the six owners
could ask on its own.

Get each of the six wrong. How many of them raise an error, a warning or an
exception?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p33.loud}$ of $\val{p33.pieces}$}
\begin{ansblock}
The gradient's honest two-step route forms $-1/p_c$, and once a logit falls
about $\val{p33.grad.cliff}$ below the largest, $p_c$ underflows to exactly
zero and it divides by zero. That is loud, and Program~\ref{prog:P18}
measured it.

A step size above $\val{p33.eta.bound}$ multiplies the steepest direction by
more than one every step. That is loud too, eventually, and
Program~\ref{prog:P17} says exactly where the boundary is.
\end{ansblock}

Name the other four, and say what each one costs when it is wrong.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
The loss, the average inside the optimiser, the arithmetic and the sample.
Each was measured by the program that owns it.
\end{ansblock}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Piece} & \textbf{What being wrong costs, and what it says} \\
\midrule
the loss & Average the per-sequence perplexities instead of exponentiating
  the average loss and the reported figure is $\val{p33.fail.ppl}$ times the
  true one at Program~\ref{prog:P19}'s widest spread. \\
the average & Carry a step size across the two conventions for momentum and
  Program~\ref{prog:P20}'s $\val{p33.conv.same}$-step walk takes
  $\val{p33.conv.fold}$ times as long, or diverges. \\
the arithmetic & In \code{bf16} a contribution under
  $\val{p33.fail.swamp}$ per cent of the running total moves nothing, so a
  gradient can be added and have no effect. \\
the sample & Accumulate micro-batches that have each divided by their own
  token count and the gradient is of a different objective, by a factor of
  $\val{p33.fail.acc}$. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{If any of that were happening the framework would tell me.}}
It would tell you about $\val{p33.loud}$ of the $\val{p33.pieces}$, and only
one of those two promptly.

The reason is not that the tooling is careless. Every one of the four silent
failures is a \emph{correct computation of the wrong quantity}: a mean of
means is a mean, a rounded sum is a sum, a rescaled step is a step. There is
no invalid operation anywhere in them for anything to catch.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
So the four that fail silently are the four you have to detect from the
outside, and there is exactly one instrument for that.

What is it?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{the loss curve}
Which is one number per step, and \emph{every one of those numbers has a
spread of $\val{p33.sigma.step}$ around what it is measuring}.

That is the whole of the rest of this program. The four shapes people read
off a loss curve are a plateau, a spike, a suspiciously smooth descent and a
divergence, and the question asked of each is the same one:
\textbf{is this evidence, or is it what noise does?}
\end{fr}

\section{A plateau}
\label{sec:P33-plateau}
\index{training run!plateau}

\mermaidfig{p33-flat-or-stuck}{%
  The same observation, and the two things it can be. The rest of this
  section is one arithmetic that tells them apart \dash{} or says that at
  this scale nothing does.}{%
  a flat stretch, and what it can be}

\begin{fr}
You have been watching for $\val{p33.watch}$ steps and the loss has not gone
below where it was when you started watching.

Suppose the run is entirely healthy and the loss is genuinely falling by
$\val{p33.improve}$ per step. How likely is it that you would see exactly
that?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
The true fall over $\val{p33.watch}$ steps is $\val{p33.watch}$ times
$\val{p33.improve}$. The two readings each carry a spread of
$\val{p33.sigma.step}$, so their difference carries
$\val{p33.sigma.step}\sqrt{2}$, and the chance of no visible improvement is
one minus the normal distribution function at their ratio.

It is $\val{p33.p.flat}$ per cent.
\end{ansblock}

Nearly half the time. A healthy run looks stuck for $\val{p33.watch}$ steps
about as often as a coin comes up heads.

One number in that arithmetic was measured and one was chosen. Which is
which?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
The spread was measured; the fall was chosen.
\end{ansblock}

It is worth saying exactly how far each goes. The fall of $\val{p33.drop}$ over $\val{p33.drop.steps}$ steps is a
choice made here to have something concrete to divide by. The spread is not
a choice \dash{} it is Program~\ref{prog:P19}'s measured per-token spread
through Program~\ref{prog:P25}'s $\sqrt{B}$.

What the arithmetic says is a \emph{ratio}, and the ratio is the point: the
progress per step is far smaller than the noise per step, and everything
below follows from that alone.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
So turn the question round, which is the only useful form of it.

How many steps must pass with no improvement before the flatness is evidence
\dash{} before a healthy run would show it less than
$\val{p33.alpha}$ of the time?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{about $\val{p33.plateau.k}$ steps}
\begin{ansblock}
Set $k \times \val{p33.improve}$ against
$\val{p33.z.alpha} \times \val{p33.sigma.step}\sqrt{2}$ and solve for $k$.
\end{ansblock}

The run in this scenario is $\val{p33.drop.steps}$ steps long from end to
end. \textbf{The flat stretch would have to be about three times the whole
run before it meant anything.}
\end{fr}

\transcript{p33-how-flat}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{The loss has been flat for an hour; something is wrong.}}
At this ratio of progress to noise, an hour of flatness is the expected
behaviour of a run that is working, and no amount of staring at the curve
will separate the two.

The mistake is not impatience. It is asking a question of an instrument that
cannot answer it \dash{} and the fix is not a longer look but a different
measurement: fewer, larger evaluations, where Program~\ref{prog:P25}'s
$1/\sqrt{n}$ works for you instead of against you.
\end{trapbox}

That number is large enough to be worth attacking. Give two reasons it might
be too large.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Early training, where the progress per step is larger; and the two readings'
noise, which is not quite independent.
\end{ansblock}

Both are worth spelling out. The first is that late training is the hard case by construction. Early in a
run the loss falls quickly, the progress per step is large against the same
noise, and a plateau of a few hundred steps genuinely is informative. It is
exactly when a run has become expensive that the curve stops being able to
tell you anything about it.

The second is that the arithmetic assumes the noise on the two readings is
independent. It is not quite: consecutive batches overlap in what they are
measuring. That makes the difference's spread a little smaller than
$\val{p33.sigma.step}\sqrt{2}$ and the answer a little smaller than
$\val{p33.plateau.k}$ \dash{} smaller, not different in kind.
\end{fr}

\section{A spike}
\label{sec:P33-spike}
\index{training run!spike}

\begin{fr}
The second shape. One step's loss sits four spreads above the trend, and
everything either side of it is ordinary.

Before deciding it is an event: a run is $\val{p33.steps}$ steps long. How
many steps like that did you expect to see?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{about $\val{p33.spike.expected}$}
A four-spread deviation has a probability of about three in a hundred
thousand, and there are $\val{p33.steps}$ steps. Multiply.

\textbf{Three of them are the expected number in a clean run.} Finding one is
not finding anything.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
You have met this exact arithmetic before, one part earlier and about models
rather than steps.

Where?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Program~\ref{prog:P27}}
That program measured what happens when forty models are compared on one
leaderboard and the winner is chosen after the fact: the largest of many
draws is large by construction, and the threshold has to be corrected for how
many were taken.

A training run is the same experiment with $\val{p33.steps}$ draws instead of
forty. Correcting for that gives the size a spike must reach before it is
surprising:
\[ z = \val{p33.spike.z} \]
and the script that produces it reproduces Program~\ref{prog:P27}'s own
committed figure when forty is put in place of $\val{p33.steps}$, so the two
are one calculation rather than two that resemble each other.

Two spikes, the same size. One is absorbed and the curve carries on where it
was; the other leaves the level higher. Which is worth investigating?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{the second}
\begin{aibox}
A spike \emph{that recovers} is worth almost nothing as a signal and a spike
that does not is worth a great deal. The recovery is the information: a deviation the run absorbs was a draw from the tail, and a
deviation that changes the level afterwards was an event.

The same logic prices the alerting. A threshold set at four spreads on a
$\val{p33.steps}$-step run pages somebody about three times per run for
nothing, and a team that has learned to ignore it has learned to ignore the
fourth.
\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
And there is a mechanism behind the tail that Program~\ref{prog:F06} already
gave you. A spike in the \emph{loss} and a spike in the \emph{gradient} are
different observations, and clipping acts on the second.

Program~\ref{prog:P21} measured what a threshold at four times the typical
gradient size clips: one step in $\val{p33.steps}$ of its own trial, which is
the same arithmetic once more. What clipping at half that size does is not a
safety net \dash{} it is a different algorithm, because it changes the length
of most steps rather than a few.
\end{fr}

\section{A suspiciously smooth descent}
\label{sec:P33-smooth}
\index{training run!smoothing}

\begin{fr}
The third shape, and it is the one nobody suspects, because a smooth curve
looks like good news.

Program~\ref{prog:P21} §5 owns half of what a smoother does: it moves every
event later by exactly the half-life you chose, so \emph{when} something
happened is not a question the smoothed curve can answer. Its trapbox says to
read the raw curve for \emph{when} and the smoothed one for \emph{whether}.

This is the other half. What does smoothing do to \emph{whether}?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
It shrinks the spread, by a factor you chose without meaning to.

An exponential moving average of independent noise has variance
$\frac{1-\beta}{1+\beta}$ times the raw variance \dash{} the sum of a
geometric series, which is Program~\ref{prog:F04}'s own object. So the spread
you see is
\[ \sqrt{\frac{1-\beta}{1+\beta}} \]
of the spread the run has.
\end{ansblock}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Put Program~\ref{prog:P21}'s own $\beta = \val{p33.beta}$ into it and the
picture shows $\val{p33.smooth.pct}$ per cent of the real spread.

Now a dashboard's default. What does $\beta = \val{p33.beta.dash}$ show?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p33.smooth.dash}$ of it}
About a tenth. \textbf{A curve smoothed at $\val{p33.beta.dash}$ understates
the run's step-to-step spread by a factor of
$\val{p33.smooth.dash.times}$}, and the factor is a property of the setting
rather than of the run.

Notice what it does \emph{not} depend on. Not the model, not the batch size,
not how long the run went on for, not the size of the spread being shrunk
\dash{} only $\beta$. So two curves smoothed at the same $\beta$ stay
comparable however different the two runs are, and two smoothed at different
$\beta$ are not comparable however similar the runs are.

So: two runs are plotted side by side and one is visibly calmer. What do you
need to know before saying anything at all?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{how each of them was smoothed}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{This run is much more stable than the last one.}} Two runs
are comparable on smoothness only if they were smoothed identically, and the
smoothing constant is the one number on a training dashboard that nobody
records in a report.

Worse, it usually is not chosen at all: it is whatever the plotting slider
was left at, and a slider is not a measurement.
\end{trapbox}

So is smoothing a mistake?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{no}
None of the above is an argument against smoothing, and it is worth saying
what it is for.

The eye is bad at averaging a noisy sequence and good at following a line, so
a smoothed curve is the right instrument for the question
\emph{is the level moving}. What it is not is an instrument for
\emph{how noisy is this run} or \emph{when did that happen}, and it answers
both of those confidently and wrongly.

Read the setting off the plot before reading the plot.
\end{fr}

  Two things a smoother does, and only one of them is written down anywhere.
  The lag is Program P21's measurement; the shrinkage is this one, and both
  are set by the same number.}{%

\section{The one shape that is diagnostic}
\label{sec:P33-diverge}
\index{training run!divergence}

\begin{fr}
Three shapes so far and none of them has survived the question. The fourth
does.

The loss rises and keeps rising. Which program in this book already says
exactly when that happens, and why it needs no statistics at all?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Program~\ref{prog:P17}}
\begin{ansblock}
On the quadratic model each eigendirection is multiplied by
$1 - \eta\lambda$ every step, so the walk is bounded exactly when
$\lvert 1 - \eta\lambda \rvert < 1$ in every direction at once. Above
$\eta = \val{p33.eta.bound}$ the steepest direction has a factor bigger than
one, and a factor bigger than one applied every step is not noise.
\end{ansblock}

At Program~\ref{prog:P17}'s own demonstration step size of
$\val{p33.eta.over}$ the factor is $\val{p33.diverge.factor}$, and the
distance from the minimum grows by that much per step for ever.

Four shapes, then. How do they divide?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{two and two, and not the way a dashboard suggests}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Shape} & \textbf{What it is} \\
\midrule
plateau & Almost never evidence at the scale a run is watched at. \\
spike & Expected, in the number the run length dictates. \\
smooth & A statement about the smoother as much as about the run. \\
divergence & Evidence, with a threshold that is a property of the surface. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

The three that are read constantly say least, and the one that says most is
the one nobody needs a plot to notice.

If the curve is a poor instrument, what is the remedy?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{a better measurement, not a better plot}
\begin{aibox}
A curve is a poor instrument because it is one noisy number per step, so the
remedy is a better measurement rather than a better plot, and Program~\ref{prog:P27} gives both halves of it
\dash{} evaluate on a held-out set, report an interval rather than a point,
and pair the comparison so the noise cancels.

An evaluation every thousand steps on a fixed set answers questions the
loss curve cannot answer at any resolution.
\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\section{The other curve: a scaling law}
\label{sec:P33-scaling}
\index{scaling law}

\begin{fr}
The second plot in every training write-up is not a loss curve at all. It is
a handful of finished runs on log-log axes with a straight line through them,
and an arrow pointing to the right.

Program~\ref{prog:F03} gave you the machinery: a power law $y = ax^{b}$ is a
straight line in $\ln x$ and $\ln y$, and the gradient of the line is the
exponent. What it deliberately did not do is fit one. This section does, and
then asks what the fit is worth.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Here is the experiment, and everything in it is known because this book chose
it. The truth is
\[ L(N) = \val{p33.fit.linf} + a N^{-\val{p33.fit.alpha}} \]
which is the shape the literature itself uses: an irreducible loss that no
amount of scale removes, plus a part that scales away.

Seven points are taken from it, evenly spaced over
$\val{p33.fit.decades}$ decades from $\val{p33.fit.lo}$ to
$\val{p33.fit.hi}$, and the loss runs from $\val{p33.fit.loss.lo}$ down to
$\val{p33.fit.loss.hi}$. A \emph{pure} power law is then fitted to them by
least squares in $\ln N$ and $\ln L$, which is what everybody fits.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
First question, and it is the one the plot is used to answer.

The true exponent on the reducible part is $\val{p33.fit.alpha}$. What
exponent does the fitted line report?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p33.fit.b}$}
Not close, and not a fault in the fitting. The line is the best straight line
through those seven points, and those seven points are not on a straight
line \dash{} they are on a curve that a floor is bending. The fitted exponent
is a property of the \emph{span}, and it would come out differently over a
different one.

\textbf{So the number read off the plot with a ruler is not the exponent in
the relationship.} It is the average slope over the decades that were
measured.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Second question. Given that, how badly does the fitted line miss the seven
points it was fitted to?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{by at most $\val{p33.fit.resid}$ per cent}
Which is invisible. At the width of a plotted line over
$\val{p33.fit.decades}$ decades, a half of one per cent is not a discrepancy
a reader can see, and no amount of looking at the plot would suggest the
relationship is anything but a power law.

\textbf{A fit that is excellent everywhere it was tested has just returned an
exponent that is wrong by a factor of nearly three.}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Third question, and it is the one the arrow on the plot is making.

Extrapolate the fitted line $\val{p33.fit.decades}$ decades past its last
point, to $N = 10^{\val{p33.out.dec}}$. The truth there is
$\val{p33.out.truth}$. What does the fit predict?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p33.out.fitted}$}
Low by $\val{p33.out.err}$ per cent \dash{} against a residual inside the
span of $\val{p33.fit.resid}$ per cent.

And the error is not a fixed size that could be quoted as a margin. It grows
with every decade, and it grows without bound, because the fitted power law
falls to zero and the truth does not.

The truth has a floor and the fitted line does not. What does that make the
fit predict, far enough out?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{a loss below the floor}
And it is a consequence you can solve for rather than argue about.

The fitted line reaches the irreducible loss $\val{p33.fit.linf}$ at
$N = 10^{\val{p33.absurd.dec}}$, which is
$\val{p33.absurd.past}$ decades past the last measured point. Beyond that it
predicts a loss \emph{below} the floor \dash{} an answer that is not merely
inaccurate but impossible, produced by a fit whose residual is
$\val{p33.fit.resid}$ per cent.
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{p33-inside-outside}{%
  One straight line, fitted over three decades, and the two different claims
  people make with it. Inside the span it is measured; outside it, guessed.}{%
  a fit inside its span and beyond it}

\begin{fr}
Which leaves the last question, and it is the one the brief asks for: the fit
reports an uncertainty of its own. What is that uncertainty about?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
The residuals \dash{} and about nothing else.

A least-squares slope's standard error is built from how far the points miss
the line. These points miss it by almost nothing, so the reported standard
error is $\val{p33.fit.se}$, and two of those carried
$\val{p33.fit.decades}$ decades give a band from $\val{p33.fit.band.lo}$ to
$\val{p33.fit.band.hi}$.
\end{ansblock}

The truth out there is $\val{p33.out.truth}$, which is outside that band.
\textbf{The fit is confident and wrong, and it is confident precisely because
it is wrong}: the data lie on a smooth curve, so the residuals are tiny, so
the reported uncertainty is tiny.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{The scaling law says we get to this loss at that budget.}}
A fitted uncertainty is about noise in the measurements. It says nothing
about whether the functional form is right, and the functional form is what
extrapolation depends on entirely.

The honest form of the claim names its span: \emph{over the range measured,
the loss fell as a power law with this exponent.} Everything to the right of
the last point is an assumption about shape, and the plot contains no
evidence about it \dash{} which is exactly why the plots are drawn with the
line extended and the data marked.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{aibox}
There is a practical form of this that costs nothing. Fit the law twice
\dash{} once on all the points, once with the largest run held out \dash{}
and compare what the second predicts for the run you already have.

That is Program~\ref{prog:P27}'s discipline applied to a fit rather than to a
leaderboard, and it measures the quantity that actually matters: not how
well the line describes the points, but how well the line built from the
small runs predicted the large one.
\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\section{What this program has not checked}
\label{sec:P33-unchecked}
\index{measurement!what is not measured}

\begin{fr}
Everything in sections 3 to 6 rests on one assumption, and it is worth
saying which, because the rest of the arithmetic is exact.

Name it.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
That the step-to-step deviation of a loss is normally distributed with a
spread that does not change.
\end{ansblock}

The first half is Program~\ref{prog:P25}'s central limit theorem doing its
job \dash{} a batch mean of many examples is close to normal in the middle,
and it is the middle that the plateau arithmetic uses. But
Program~\ref{prog:P25} also measured where that stops being true: at three
spreads out the Gaussian already overstates the tail, and it is the
\emph{tail} that the spike arithmetic uses.

So the plateau result is on firmer ground than the spike result, and they are
stated in that order for that reason.

And there is no defence available in the direction of the error, because it
does not have one direction. Program~\ref{prog:P25} measured the ratio of the
Gaussian's tail to the truth at one, three and five spreads:
$\val{p25.tail.ratio1}$, $\val{p25.tail.ratio3}$ and
$\val{p25.tail.ratio5}$. It \emph{understates} close in, crosses, and then
runs away. So \enquote{the approximation is conservative} is not a sentence
anybody can say about it without naming the distance first.

And the thing that would settle it costs nothing, which is why it is worth
naming: a run's own logged losses \emph{are} the sample. Fit the trend, take
the residuals, and ask how often they exceed three and four spreads against
what a normal distribution predicts. Nobody does it, the data is already on
disk, and the answer would turn every number in \S\ref{sec:P33-spike} from a
model into a measurement.

That was the first half of the assumption. Which half is the weaker one?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{the second}
A real run's noise is not stationary: the
spread changes as the model changes, the data ordering is not random in the
way the arithmetic assumes, and a spike is often a property of a particular
batch rather than a draw from a fixed distribution.

\begin{warning}
\textbf{This book has not measured any of that, and it would need a real
training run to.} What the sections above establish is a \emph{floor}: even
under assumptions that are kind to the curve, a plateau is uninformative for
tens of thousands of steps and a long run contains large deviations by
construction. Real noise, being heavier-tailed and drifting, makes both
worse rather than better.
\end{warning}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
And that is Part IX's honest position, arrived at three times now.
Program~\ref{prog:P32} split its own outstanding claim the same way: the
assembly half needed no trained model and the training half did, so the
assembly half was derived and the other stated as outstanding.

Ask that split of anything in this book that looks as though it needs a
model. A surprising amount of it turns out to be a statement about shapes,
about a normal distribution, or about a least-squares line \dash{} and the
part that genuinely needs a run is worth naming rather than hedging the whole
claim.
\end{fr}

\begin{testexercises}
\item A run's loss is falling by $\num{2e-5}$ per step and the observed loss
  has a spread of $\num{0.2}$. Roughly how many steps of no improvement
  would a healthy run produce five times in a hundred?
  \answerto{About $\num{23000}$. The threshold is
  $\val{p33.z.alpha} \times \num{0.2}\sqrt{2}$ divided by $\num{2e-5}$. Note
  that doubling the progress per step halved it, and halving the spread would
  have halved it too \dash{} the answer depends on the ratio and on nothing
  else.}
\item A team raises the batch size from $\val{p33.batch}$ to
  $\num{1024}$ and reports that the loss curve became much cleaner. By what
  factor did the spread fall, and did the run improve?
  \answerto{By $2$, because the spread is $\sigma/\sqrt{B}$ and the batch
  went up fourfold. The run did not necessarily improve at all: a cleaner
  curve at a larger batch is what Program~\ref{prog:P25}'s rate predicts
  whatever the model is doing.}
\item A $\num{200000}$-step run is monitored with an alert at three spreads
  above the trend. How many alerts should be expected in a clean run?
  \answerto{About $\num{270}$. A three-spread deviation has probability
  about $\num{0.00135}$, and $\num{200000}$ steps multiply it. An alert
  firing hundreds of times per run is not an alert.}
\item Two runs are plotted, one smoothed at $\beta = \num{0.9}$ and one at
  $\beta = \num{0.99}$. Their plotted curves have visible spreads in the
  ratio $3$ to $1$. What is the ratio of the underlying runs' spreads?
  \answerto{Compute the two shrinkage factors and divide them out:
  \[ \sqrt{\num{0.1}/\num{1.9}} = \num{0.229},
     \qquad \sqrt{\num{0.01}/\num{1.99}} = \num{0.0709} \]
  a ratio of $\num{3.24}$. So
  the plotted ratio of $3$ is very close to what identical runs would
  produce, and the evidence for any difference between them is
  approximately none.}
\item A power law fitted over two decades has a residual of one per cent.
  What does that bound about its prediction one decade further out?
  \answerto{Nothing at all. A residual bounds the fit inside the span it was
  fitted over. Outside, the error depends on whether the functional form is
  right, and the residual carries no information about that \dash{} which is
  what this program's own fit demonstrates at
  $\val{p33.fit.resid}$ per cent.}
\end{testexercises}

\canyou

\begin{furtherproblems}
\item Show that if the observed loss at each step is the truth plus
  independent noise of spread $\sigma$, the difference between readings $k$
  steps apart has spread $\sigma\sqrt{2}$ regardless of $k$.
  \answerto{The two noise terms are independent, so their variances add:
  $\sigma^{2} + \sigma^{2}$. The $k$ enters the \emph{true} fall and not the
  noise, which is exactly why the plateau threshold is linear in $k$.}
\item A team accumulates four micro-batches holding $1000$, $10$, $500$ and
  $490$ real tokens, each having divided by its own count. Their mean losses
  are $2$, $8$, $3$ and $3$. Is the accumulated gradient the large-batch one?
  \answerto{No. The pooled mean weights by tokens and gives
  $\num{2.475}$; the accumulated one averages the four and gives $4$. The
  error is silent, it is in the gradient rather than in a report, and it is
  Program~\ref{prog:P21}'s trap with one more micro-batch in it.}
\item An exponential moving average is applied twice, at the same $\beta$.
  By what factor is the spread of the doubly-smoothed curve reduced?
  \answerto{Less than by the square of the single factor, because the
  once-smoothed series is no longer independent from step to step. The point
  worth taking is the direction: each pass makes the picture calmer and the
  run no different, and two passes are not twice as honest.}
\item The fit in \S\ref{sec:P33-scaling} reaches the irreducible loss at
  $10^{\val{p33.absurd.dec}}$. Solve for that size from the fitted line.
  \answerto{Set the fitted $\exp(c) N^{-b}$ equal to
  $\val{p33.fit.linf}$ and take logarithms:
  $\ln N = (c - \ln \val{p33.fit.linf})/b$. The arithmetic is
  Program~\ref{prog:F03}'s throughout, which is the point \dash{} the
  absurdity is visible with nothing but the two laws of logarithms.}
\item A colleague proposes detecting plateaus by smoothing the curve heavily
  first, on the grounds that the smoothed curve is less noisy. What is wrong
  with it?
  \answerto{The smoothed curve is less noisy and the estimate it is built
  from is not. Smoothing reduces the \emph{visible} spread by a known factor
  without adding any information, so it makes a plateau easier to see and no
  easier to believe \dash{} and it delays the moment you see one by a
  half-life. The instrument has to change, not the plot.}
\end{furtherproblems}
