\label{prog:P08}
\index{matrix!rank}
\index{least squares}

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{1--8}{say which vectors a matrix can reach and which it sends to
  zero, and read both off its shape before computing anything}
\outcome{9--14}{say what the rank of a matrix is, and why it is one number
  rather than two}
\outcome{15--21}{turn a system with no solution into one with a best answer,
  and say what makes that answer the best one}
\outcome{22--29}{price a low-rank update against the dense one it stands in
  for, and state the assumption it is making}
\outcome{30--34}{say why a narrow layer costs something no later layer can
  recover}
\end{outcomes}

\begin{quiz}
\item $A$ is $\val{p08.wide.rows} \times \val{p08.wide.cols}$. Must some
  non-zero vector be sent to zero? \teachesat{4--5}
  \answerto{Yes, and it is forced. Three columns cannot be independent in a
  space of two dimensions, so some combination of them is zero, and the
  coefficients of that combination are a vector the matrix kills.}
\item The columns of $A$ span one space and its rows span another. Must the two
  have the same dimension? \teachesat{9--10}
  \answerto{Yes, always, and it is a theorem rather than a coincidence. That
  shared number is the \emph{rank}. The two spaces are different sets and
  usually live in rooms of different sizes.}
\item Three points that are not on a line, and you want the line through them.
  What is wrong with the question? \teachesat{15--16}
  \answerto{There is no such line, so the system has no solution at all. The
  question has to be changed before it can be answered: not \emph{which line
  passes through them} but \emph{which line comes closest}.}
\item What makes the least-squares fit the closest one? \teachesat{17--18}
  \answerto{Its residual is perpendicular to every column of $A$. That is the
  defining property, it is Program~\ref{prog:P05}'s projection with a subspace
  in place of a line, and it is what makes the answer a projection rather than
  a compromise.}
\item A $\val{p08.lora.d} \times \val{p08.lora.d}$ update is replaced by two
  factors with $\val{p08.lora.r}$ between them. Roughly what fraction of the
  parameters is that? \teachesat{23--24}
  \answerto{$\val{p08.lora.pct}\%$ \dash{} $\val{p08.lora.lowrank}$ against
  $\val{p08.lora.dense}$, a factor of $\val{p08.lora.factor}$.}
\item A layer in the middle of a stack has rank $\val{p08.chain.r}$. What is
  the most the whole stack can have? \teachesat{30--31}
  \answerto{$\val{p08.chain.r}$. A product cannot have more rank than either
  factor, so nothing placed after that layer can recover what it discarded.}
\end{quiz}

\section{What a matrix reaches, and what it discards}
\label{sec:P08-subspaces}
\index{matrix!column space}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:P07} handed this program a harder question about two indices:
\emph{how much does a matrix actually do?}

Start with the half that is a definition. $A$ takes every vector somewhere, so
run every vector through it and collect the results. What is that set?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Everything the columns of $A$ can build between them \dash{} their span.
\end{ansblock}

$Ax$ is $x_1$ times the first column plus $x_2$ times the second and so on, so
the output is a linear combination of the columns and $x$ supplies the
coefficients. Every output; nothing else is reachable.

That set is the \emph{column space}, and Program~\ref{prog:P04} already gave
you the word for it and the machinery to reason about it. It is a subspace, and
what makes this program worth writing is how much follows from asking how big
it is.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Four subspaces come with a matrix, and it is worth naming all four before using
any.

\begin{itemize}
\item the \textbf{column space}: everything $A$ can reach;
\item the \textbf{row space}: the span of the rows, which lives in the space
  $A$ reads from rather than the one it writes into;
\item the \textbf{null space}: every vector $A$ sends to zero;
\item the \textbf{left null space}: every vector the transpose sends to zero.
\end{itemize}

The first three carry the whole of this program; the fourth is named so that
the set is complete and is not used again.

Two of the four live in the space $A$ reads from rather than the one it writes
into. Which two?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{The row space and the null space.}
Both are made of vectors $A$ can be applied to, so both live on the input side;
the column space and the left null space live on the output side. Sorting them
that way is worth doing once, because the null space is the one people put in
the wrong room.

And it is the one that decides the most, so take a concrete matrix.

$A$ is $\val{p08.wide.rows} \times \val{p08.wide.cols}$: it reads a space of
$\val{p08.wide.cols}$ dimensions and writes into one of
$\val{p08.wide.rows}$.

Must some non-zero vector be sent to zero, or does it depend on the entries?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{It is forced, whatever the entries are.}
Three columns sit in a space of two dimensions, so by
Program~\ref{prog:P04}'s counting bound they cannot be independent: some
combination of them is the zero vector. The coefficients of that combination
\emph{are} a vector $A$ kills, and choosing the entries differently only
changes which one it is.

Measured on the worked matrix: rank $\val{p08.wide.rank}$, null space of
dimension $\val{p08.wide.null}$, and

\[ \val{p08.wide.rank} + \val{p08.wide.null} = \val{p08.wide.cols}. \]

\textbf{That is the rank--nullity theorem}, and the right-hand side is the
number of \emph{columns} \dash{} the size of the space $A$ reads from, not the
one it writes into.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{Rank plus nullity is the size of the matrix} is the version
people carry, and it is wrong on both counts.}

A matrix has two sizes and the theorem names one of them. Every input is
accounted for exactly once: it either survives into the column space or it is
part of what gets sent to zero, and there is nowhere else for it to go. The
output side is not being counted at all.

So a matrix wider than it is tall \textbf{must} discard something, and a matrix
taller than it is wide \textbf{cannot} reach everything. Both are readable off
the shape before a single entry is chosen \dash{} which is
Program~\ref{prog:P06}'s type signature carrying more information than it first
appeared to.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{aibox}
Which is what a bottleneck layer is, stated exactly rather than by analogy.

An autoencoder that squeezes an input through a narrow middle is a wide matrix
followed by a tall one, and the wide one has a null space it cannot avoid
having. The compression is not a side effect of training: it is forced by the
shape, and training only chooses \emph{which} directions get discarded.

The same sentence covers a projection head, an adapter's down-projection and
any layer narrower than the one before it. \textbf{Choosing a width is choosing
how much to throw away}, and the amount is fixed before the weights exist.
\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
One more consequence of the shape, and it is the half people expect less.

A tall matrix \dash{} more rows than columns \dash{} cannot reach everything in
the space it writes into, because its column space has at most as many
dimensions as it has columns. So there are right-hand sides for which $Ax = b$
has \textbf{no solution at all}, and no amount of choosing $x$ will help.

That is not a pathology. It is the ordinary situation whenever there are more
measurements than parameters, which is every fitting problem anybody has, and
section 3 is about what to do instead.
\end{fr}

\section{One number, not two}
\label{sec:P08-rank}
\index{matrix!rank}

\begin{fr}
Now the question that makes rank worth a name.

The column space lives in the space $A$ writes into; the row space lives in the
space $A$ reads from. For a $\val{p08.wide.rows} \times \val{p08.wide.cols}$
matrix those are rooms of different sizes, and the two spaces are not the same
set \dash{} they are not even made of the same kind of object.

Must they have the same dimension?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Yes, always.}
Not usually, not for well-behaved matrices: always, for every matrix there is.
The shared number is the \emph{rank}.

The script checks it on $\val{p08.rank.trials}$ matrices of assorted shapes,
half of them deliberately built to be rank-deficient, over the rationals with
no tolerance anywhere \dash{} and, as in Program~\ref{prog:P04}, what the draws
are really testing is the code. A search that succeeded would have refuted a
proof.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
It is worth being clear about why this is surprising, because a result that
sounds obvious teaches nothing.

Nothing in the definitions connects the two. One is about what combinations of
the columns can produce; the other is about what combinations of the rows can
produce; and for a wide matrix one of the two spaces lives somewhere the other
cannot even be compared to.

One operation ties them, and it is one you already know. Which?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Elimination \dash{} adding a multiple of one row to another.
\end{ansblock}

It changes neither the row space (the new rows build the same combinations) nor
which columns are independent, so the count you read off at the end is the same
count either way round.

Which bounds it, and the bound is the sentence Program~\ref{prog:P07} promised.

The rank cannot exceed the number of rows, because the column space sits in a
space of that many dimensions; and it cannot exceed the number of columns, for
the matching reason. So

\[ \operatorname{rank}(A) \le \min(\text{rows}, \text{columns}) \]

and a matrix reaching that bound is called \emph{full rank}. \textbf{The rank is
a number smaller than the shape, and the gap is how much of the shape is not
doing anything.}
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{p08-one-number}{Two spaces of different kinds, and the count they
share.}{one number, not two}

\begin{fr}
\begin{note}
And the word has to be said back to Program~\ref{prog:P07}, because that
program uses it for something else.

There, the rank of an array is its number of \emph{axes} \dash{} how deep the
indexing goes, and a statement about the container. Here, the rank of a matrix
is how many independent directions it maps onto \dash{} a statement about what
the map \emph{does}, which no amount of looking at the shape will tell you.

A rank-$\val{p08.wide.rows}$ array in that sense could hold a matrix of any
rank in this one. The two words are unrelated and both are standard, so this
book writes \emph{number of axes} for the first wherever there is doubt.
\end{note}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{aibox}
Which makes rank the honest answer to \enquote{how big is this layer}, where
the parameter count is only the answer to \enquote{how much did it cost}.

A $d \times d$ weight matrix has $d^{2}$ parameters however it was trained. If
its rank is $r$, everything it does to every input lands in a space of $r$
dimensions, and the other $d - r$ directions of its output are unreachable no
matter what arrives. Two matrices with identical parameter counts can do
wildly different amounts.

The rest of this program is what follows from taking that seriously: section 4
imposes a rank on purpose, and section 5 is about losing one by accident.
\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\section{Solving what has no solution}
\label{sec:P08-least-squares}
\index{least squares}

\begin{fr}
Section 1 said a tall matrix cannot reach everything. Here is the smallest case
where that stops being abstract.

Three points \dash{} $(1, 1)$, $(2, 3)$ and $(3, 2)$ \dash{} and you want the
straight line through them. Writing $y = mx + c$ for each point gives three
equations in two unknowns, so $A$ is
$\val{p08.ls.points} \times 2$: one column of $x$ values and one column of
$1$s.

Before solving anything: is there a solution?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{No, and not approximately \dash{} none at all.}
The three points are not on a line, so no $(m, c)$ satisfies all three
equations. Said in this program's language, $b$ is not in the column space of
$A$.

\transcript{p08-least-squares}

The first line is the test, and it is exact: $A$ has rank $2$, and adding $b$
as a third column takes the rank to $3$. \textbf{A right-hand side that raises
the rank is a right-hand side the columns cannot build}, and that is what
\enquote{no solution} means without ever attempting one.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
So change the question, which is the only honest move available.

If $b$ cannot be reached, ask for the point of the column space that comes
closest to it. That is a well-posed question, it always has an answer, and it
is a question Program~\ref{prog:P05} already answered \dash{} for a line.

Its closing frame on projection said so in as many words: the derivation used
the inner product's three properties and nothing else, so the same formula
projects onto a line, onto a subspace, and onto the column space of a matrix.

So the characterisation comes free, and it is worth producing rather than
reading. The closest point leaves an error \dash{} the \emph{residual} \dash{}
pointing from it to $b$, and projection onto a line left that residual
perpendicular to the line. What is it perpendicular to here?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
To the whole column space \dash{} equivalently, to every column of $A$.
\end{ansblock}

\textbf{Nothing new was defined to get there.} One object was swapped into a
derivation the reader already had, and the answer came with it.

That is the whole of least squares. If the residual leaned even slightly on one
of the columns you could move along that column and get closer, so the fit
would not have been the closest point.

\textbf{So the test for a best fit is a set of dot products, all zero.} No
optimisation, no iteration and nothing about squares: the squares appear only
because \enquote{closest} was measured with a length, and
Program~\ref{prog:P05} says what a length is.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Worked on the three points, and every number is exact.

The fit is $y = \val{p08.ls.slope}x + \val{p08.ls.intercept}$, so the fitted
values are $\frac{3}{2}$, $2$ and $\frac{5}{2}$ and the residuals are
$-\frac{1}{2}$, $1$ and $-\frac{1}{2}$.

It is worth spending the thirty seconds on this rather than taking it, because
checking those two dot products is not checking the arithmetic. The two
conditions \emph{are} the definition of the answer, so a zero on each is a
proof that no other line in the plane comes closer \dash{} which is a much
stronger thing to have confirmed than that a division came out right.

Check them by hand, and do it before reading on: the residuals against the
column of $1$s, and the residuals against the column of $x$ values.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$-\frac{1}{2} + 1 - \frac{1}{2} = 0$ \qquad
$-\frac{1}{2} + 2 - \frac{3}{2} = 0$
\end{ansblock}

Both exactly zero, and the sum of squared residuals is $\val{p08.ls.sse}$. The
script also perturbs the fit in eight directions and every one of them is
worse, so \enquote{closest} is measured rather than asserted.
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{p08-closest-in-the-space}{Why the best answer to an unsolvable
system is a shadow.}{closest point in the space}

\begin{fr}
\begin{aibox}
That is linear regression, and the closed form everybody quotes is those dot
products written as one equation: $A\T A\,\hat{x} = A\T b$, the \emph{normal
equations}, whose left side is exactly \enquote{make the residual orthogonal to
every column}.

Two things are worth carrying out of it. \textbf{The fit is a projection, so it
can only ever land in the column space} \dash{} if the relationship is not a
combination of the features you supplied, no amount of fitting will find it,
and the residual will tell you so rather than the coefficients.

And a warning this program cannot cash: forming $A\T A$ and solving it is the
worst way to do this on a machine, for a reason that is about conditioning
rather than about algebra. Program~\ref{prog:P11} owns that, and it is why
production code does not follow the textbook here.
\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\section{A rank imposed on purpose}
\label{sec:P08-low-rank}
\index{matrix!low-rank update}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:P06} left this program a specific debt.

It showed that a wide matrix times a very thin one times a wide one is cheap
when you keep the thin factor in the middle, called that the mechanical half,
and said this program is where the thin dimension gets its name and the
assumption behind it gets stated.

The name is $r$, and it is a rank: writing an update as $BA$ with $r$ between
the factors forces the product to have rank at most $r$, because
Program~\ref{prog:P06}'s composition cannot reach more directions than the
narrowest map in it allows.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
So price it, because the price is the whole argument.

A full update to a $\val{p08.lora.d} \times \val{p08.lora.d}$ weight matrix has
$\val{p08.lora.d}^{2}$ parameters. Written as $BA$ with $r =
\val{p08.lora.r}$ it has $2 \times \val{p08.lora.d} \times \val{p08.lora.r}$.

Those two factors are the whole of what is stored and the whole of what is
trained: the original weight matrix is left where it was, and the update is
rebuilt from $B$ and $A$ whenever it is wanted. So the comparison is between
those two counts and nothing else.

Work out roughly what fraction of the parameters that is.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p08.lora.pct}\%$.}
$\val{p08.lora.lowrank}$ against $\val{p08.lora.dense}$, a factor of
$\val{p08.lora.factor}$.

That number is the entire practical case, and it is arithmetic rather than a
result: two thin factors have $2dr$ entries and a square one has $d^{2}$, so
the ratio is $2r/d$ and it does not depend on anything about the model.

\textbf{What is bought is not accuracy, speed or a better optimum. It is the
parameter count, and that is bought outright.}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Which makes a threshold worth having, because \enquote{low-rank} is a claim
about a number.

The two factors cost $2dr$ and the dense update costs $d^{2}$. At what $r$ do
they cost the same?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$r = \val{p08.lora.cross}$, which is half the width.}
$2dr = d^{2}$ exactly when $r = d/2$, so an adapter with an inner dimension
above half the layer's width \textbf{costs more than the dense update it is
standing in for}, while constraining what the update can do.

Nobody sets $r$ that high, which is precisely why the threshold is worth
knowing: it says the saving comes from $r$ being small compared with $d$ and
from nothing else, so a wide layer tolerates a larger $r$ for the same
fraction and a narrow one does not.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
And the part the parameter count hides, which is worth naming before it is
named for you.

Constraining the update to rank $r$ is not a neutral reparametrisation: it buys
the saving by refusing to represent most of the matrices the dense update
could.

The refusal is not incidental to the saving \dash{} it \emph{is} the saving.
Every parameter not stored is a matrix not reachable, and the two are one fact
counted twice. A constraint that cost nothing would buy nothing, which is worth
holding on to whenever a technique is described as free.

Say what is therefore being assumed.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{That a rank-$r$ matrix is close enough to the update you would have made.}
Equivalently: that the change the task needs happens to lie in a few directions
rather than being spread over all $\val{p08.lora.d}$.

That claim can be tested, and this program cannot test it: measuring how much
of a matrix a rank-$r$ approximation captures is a question about singular
values, and it is Program~\ref{prog:P11}'s. \textbf{What this program can say
is what is being assumed}, which is worth more than most of what gets said
about the technique.
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{p08-thin-middle}{What a thin factor buys and what it takes for
granted.}{a thin middle}

\begin{fr}
\begin{aibox}
The same shape turns up in three other places, and recognising it is the point
of having the vocabulary.

A projection head that maps $d$ down to $k$ and back is rank $k$ by
construction. An attention head with head dimension $d_k$ contributes a map of
rank at most $d_k$ whatever the sequence does. And an embedding matrix of
$V$ tokens in $d$ dimensions has rank at most $d$, which is
Program~\ref{prog:P04}'s counting bound restated: at most
$\val{p08.emb.rank.max}$ independent directions, however many tokens there are.

None of those is a design flaw. Each is a rank chosen deliberately, and the
question a review should ask is the one from the last frame: what is being
assumed about the thing that fits into that many directions?
\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\section{A rank lost by accident}
\label{sec:P08-collapse}
\index{matrix!rank}

\begin{fr}
Section 4 imposed a rank and got something for it. This section is the same
mechanism arriving uninvited.

$A$ has rank $\val{p08.chain.r}$ and sits in the middle of a stack. Every
matrix after it is square, $\val{p08.chain.d} \times \val{p08.chain.d}$, and
full rank.

What is the most the whole product can have?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p08.chain.r}$, and no amount of depth changes it.}
A product cannot have more rank than either factor, because
Program~\ref{prog:P06} made it a composition: what the first map cannot reach,
the second has nothing to work with. Measured, a rank-$\val{p08.chain.r}$
bottleneck survives $\val{p08.chain.len}$ full-rank
$\val{p08.chain.d} \times \val{p08.chain.d}$ factors stacked on top with its
rank unchanged.

\textbf{Rank is lost forwards only.} That is a stronger statement than it
sounds: it is not that recovering it is hard, or needs more parameters, or
takes longer to train. There is nothing downstream that can do it.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{A later layer can learn to undo it} is the intuition, and it
is the one place where more capacity provably does not help.}

The intuition is not silly \dash{} a wider or deeper stack can compensate for
most things. But two inputs that the narrow layer sent to the same place arrive
at every later layer as the same vector, and a function cannot return two
answers for one argument. Program~\ref{prog:F05} settled that in its first
section, and it has been true ever since.

Compare it with Program~\ref{prog:P06}'s collapse, which is the mirror image:
there, depth without a non-linearity bought nothing and the fix was to add one.
Here, a narrow layer costs something and there is no fix downstream at all.
\textbf{One is waste and the other is loss.}
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Which is the setting for a phenomenon worth naming carefully, because the
careful version and the folklore version are different claims.

It is often reported that in a deep stack of attention layers the
representations drift towards each other, so that what arrives at the top
carries far fewer independent directions than its width allows \dash{} the
effect usually called \emph{rank collapse}.

\begin{warning}
\textbf{This book has not measured that, and it is not the same statement as
the theorem above.}

The theorem is exact and is about a bottleneck: a narrow map in the chain caps
everything after it. The phenomenon is about a stack with \emph{no} narrow
layer in which the rank falls anyway, which is a claim about what training and
the softmax do rather than about what shapes force. It depends on the
architecture, the depth, the residual connections and the normalisation, and
the reported strength of it varies accordingly.

What would settle it is the singular-value spectrum of the representations at
each depth, which needs Program~\ref{prog:P11}'s machinery and a real model.
Until then this program states the mechanism it can prove and labels the rest
as what it is.
\end{warning}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The last frame, and it is about what one number bought.

Five sections ago a matrix had a shape, and the shape was two numbers that had
to agree at a join. It has a rank as well, which is a third number the shape
does not determine, and almost everything in this program was a consequence of
that gap: what a matrix must discard, what it cannot reach, what makes an
unsolvable system answerable, what a thin factor costs and what it assumes, and
why a narrow layer is permanent.

Program~\ref{prog:P09} asks the question this one kept deferring. Rank says
\emph{how many} directions survive; the determinant says \emph{how much} of the
space does, in one number that is zero exactly when something was destroyed
\dash{} which is this program's null space seen from the other side.
\end{fr}

\begin{summarybox}
\sumitem{1--3}{\result{\textbf{The column space is everything $A$ can reach} \dash{}
  the span of its columns, because $Ax$ is a combination of them with $x$ as
  the coefficients. Four subspaces come with a matrix: column space, row space,
  null space and left null space, two about what comes out and two about what
  goes in}.}
\sumitem{4--6}{\result{\textbf{Rank plus nullity is the number of COLUMNS}, not the
  size of the matrix: every input either survives into the column space or is
  sent to zero. So a wide matrix must discard something and a tall one cannot
  reach everything, both readable off the shape before an entry is chosen}.}
\sumitem{7--8}{\result{A bottleneck layer is that fact rather than a training effect:
  \textbf{choosing a width is choosing how much to throw away}, and training
  only chooses which directions go. And a tall $A$ makes $Ax = b$ unsolvable
  for most $b$, which is every fitting problem there is}.}
\sumitem{9--12}{\result{\textbf{The column space and the row space always have the same
  dimension}, though they are different sets in rooms of different sizes. That
  shared number is the \emph{rank}, checked on $\val{p08.rank.trials}$
  matrices exactly \dash{} where, as in Program~\ref{prog:P04}, the draws are
  testing the code, because a search that succeeded would have refuted a
  proof}.}
\sumitem{12--14}{\result{$\operatorname{rank}(A) \le \min(\text{rows}, \text{columns})$,
  so \textbf{the rank is a number smaller than the shape and the gap is how
  much of the shape does nothing}. Program~\ref{prog:P07}'s \emph{rank} is the
  number of axes and is unrelated; this book writes \emph{number of axes} for
  that one}.}
\sumitem{15--17}{\result{A right-hand side that \textbf{raises the rank when added as a
  column} is one the columns cannot build, which is what \enquote{no solution}
  means without attempting one. The move is then to change the question:
  the closest point of the column space, which Program~\ref{prog:P05} already
  derived for a line and said would generalise}.}
\sumitem{18--20}{\result{\textbf{The residual of the best fit is perpendicular to every
  column of $A$}, and that is the whole of least squares \dash{} if it leaned
  on a column you could move along that column and get closer. Worked exactly:
  $y = \val{p08.ls.slope}x + \val{p08.ls.intercept}$, residuals
  $-\frac{1}{2}$, $1$, $-\frac{1}{2}$, both dot products zero, sum of squares
  $\val{p08.ls.sse}$}.}
\sumitem{21}{\result{The normal equations $A\T A\,\hat{x} = A\T b$ are those dot
  products written once, which is linear regression's closed form.
  \textbf{The fit can only land in the column space}, so a relationship outside
  it will show in the residual rather than the coefficients \dash{} and
  Program~\ref{prog:P11} owns why forming $A\T A$ is the wrong way to solve it
  on a machine}.}
\sumitem{22--24}{\result{Writing an update as $BA$ with $r$ between the factors caps
  its rank at $r$. At $\val{p08.lora.d}$ wide and $r = \val{p08.lora.r}$ that
  is $\val{p08.lora.lowrank}$ parameters against $\val{p08.lora.dense}$, a
  factor of $\val{p08.lora.factor}$ and $\val{p08.lora.pct}\%$ \dash{} and the
  ratio is $2r/d$, which depends on nothing about the model}.}
\sumitem{25--29}{\result{\textbf{The saving stops at $r = \val{p08.lora.cross}$}, half
  the width, above which the adapter costs more than the dense update while
  still constraining it. And the constraint is an \textbf{assumption} \dash{}
  that the needed change lies in a few directions \dash{} which
  Program~\ref{prog:P11} can test and this program cannot}.}
\sumitem{30--32}{\result{\textbf{Rank is lost forwards only.} A product cannot exceed
  either factor's rank, so a rank-$\val{p08.chain.r}$ bottleneck survives
  $\val{p08.chain.len}$ full-rank factors stacked on top. Two inputs sent to
  one place arrive everywhere later as one vector, and a function cannot return
  two answers for one argument}.}
\sumitem{32--34}{\result{Program~\ref{prog:P06}'s collapse and this one are mirror
  images: there depth without a non-linearity was \textbf{waste} and had a fix;
  here a narrow layer is \textbf{loss} and has none.} Rank collapse in deep
  attention stacks is a different and unmeasured claim, and this book says so.}
\end{summarybox}

\begin{testexercises}
\item Say what the column space of a matrix is, in terms of its columns.
  \answerto{Their span \dash{} every linear combination of them. $Ax$ is the
  combination whose coefficients are the entries of $x$, so the column space is
  exactly the set of outputs the matrix can produce.}
\item $A$ is $3 \times 7$. Give the largest its rank can be and the smallest
  its null space can be.
  \answerto{Rank at most $3$, because the column space sits in a space of three
  dimensions. Nullity is $7$ minus the rank, so at least $4$: a matrix that
  wide must discard at least four dimensions' worth of input.}
\item $A$ is $9 \times 4$ with rank $4$. Say whether $Ax = b$ has a solution
  for every $b$, and why.
  \answerto{No. The column space has four dimensions inside a space of nine, so
  almost every $b$ lies outside it. Full rank here means nothing is discarded
  on the way in, not that everything is reachable on the way out.}
\item State the rank--nullity theorem and say which of the matrix's two sizes
  appears in it.
  \answerto{Rank plus nullity equals the number of columns. It is the input
  side: every input vector either survives into the column space or is sent to
  zero, and the number of rows does not enter.}
\item Say why the row space and the column space must have the same dimension,
  in terms of elimination.
  \answerto{Row operations change neither the row space nor which columns are
  independent, so the count read off the eliminated matrix is the same count
  either way. The two spaces are different sets; only the count is shared.}
\item Given $A$ and $b$, give a test for whether $Ax = b$ has a solution that
  does not involve trying to solve it.
  \answerto{Compare the rank of $A$ with the rank of $A$ with $b$ added as an
  extra column. If adding $b$ raises the rank, $b$ is outside the column space
  and there is no solution.}
\item State the property that makes a least-squares fit the closest one.
  \answerto{Its residual is orthogonal to every column of $A$. If it were not,
  moving along the offending column would reduce the distance, so the fit would
  not have been closest.}
\item An adapter replaces a $d \times d$ update with two factors of inner
  dimension $r$. Give the ratio of parameters and the $r$ at which the saving
  disappears.
  \answerto{$2r/d$, which depends on nothing else about the model. The saving
  disappears at $r = d/2$; above that the adapter costs more than the dense
  update while still constraining it.}
\item Say what a rank-$r$ update assumes, and name what would test the
  assumption.
  \answerto{That the change the task needs lies in at most $r$ directions
  rather than being spread over all $d$. Testing it means asking how much of
  the full update a rank-$r$ matrix captures, which is a question about
  singular values and belongs to Program~\ref{prog:P11}.}
\item A stack contains one layer of rank $2$ and twenty full-rank square layers
  after it. Give the rank of the whole stack and say why more layers do not
  help.
  \answerto{At most $2$. A product cannot exceed either factor's rank, and two
  inputs the narrow layer sent to one place are one vector from then on \dash{}
  no later function can return two answers for one argument.}
\end{testexercises}

\begin{furtherproblems}
\item Show that $Ax$ is a linear combination of the columns of $A$.
  \answerto{Entry $i$ of $Ax$ is $\sum_j A_{ij} x_j$, and reading that down the
  $i$ index it is $x_1$ times column $1$ plus $x_2$ times column $2$ and so on.
  Program~\ref{prog:P07}'s index notation makes it one line.}
\item Give a $2 \times 2$ matrix of rank $1$ and describe its column space, its
  null space and what it does to the plane.
  \answerto{Any matrix with two proportional columns, for instance
  $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$. Its column space is the line
  through $(1, 2)$, its null space is the line through $(2, -1)$, and it
  flattens the whole plane onto one line.}
\item Show that $\operatorname{rank}(AB) \le \operatorname{rank}(A)$.
  \answerto{Every column of $AB$ is $A$ applied to a column of $B$, so every
  column of $AB$ lies in the column space of $A$. A span of vectors inside a
  space cannot have more dimensions than that space.}
\item Show that $\operatorname{rank}(AB) \le \operatorname{rank}(B)$ as well,
  and say why the two together give the bound the program uses.
  \answerto{Apply the previous argument to the transpose: $(AB)\T = B\T A\T$,
  so the rank of $AB$ is at most that of $B\T$, which equals that of $B$.
  Together they give $\operatorname{rank}(AB) \le \min$ of the two, which is
  the statement that a bottleneck caps everything after it.}
\item Derive the normal equations from the orthogonality condition.
  \answerto{The residual is $b - A\hat{x}$, and orthogonality to every column
  says $A\T(b - A\hat{x}) = 0$, since the rows of $A\T$ are the columns of $A$.
  Rearranged that is $A\T A\,\hat{x} = A\T b$.}
\item The least-squares fit through $(1,1)$, $(2,3)$, $(3,2)$ has residuals
  $-\frac{1}{2}$, $1$, $-\frac{1}{2}$. Verify both orthogonality conditions and
  say what each column is testing.
  \answerto{Against the column of $1$s: $-\frac{1}{2} + 1 - \frac{1}{2} = 0$,
  which says the residuals average to zero. Against the column of $x$ values:
  $-\frac{1}{2} + 2 - \frac{3}{2} = 0$, which says the residuals carry no
  remaining trend in $x$.}
\item Say what it would mean for a least-squares residual to be large but still
  orthogonal to every column.
  \answerto{That the fit is the best one available and the relationship is not
  a combination of the features supplied. Orthogonality is a statement about
  the fit being optimal, not about it being good; the size of the residual is a
  separate question and is the one worth reporting.}
\item An embedding matrix holds $V$ tokens in $d$ dimensions with $V$ far
  larger than $d$. Give its largest possible rank and say what that does and
  does not imply.
  \answerto{At most $d$, which is Program~\ref{prog:P04}'s counting bound. It
  implies that most tokens are combinations of others; it does not imply the
  model cannot distinguish that many tokens, because distinguishing them needs
  only that they differ, not that they be independent.}
\end{furtherproblems}
