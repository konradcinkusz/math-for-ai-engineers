\program[Constrained optimisation]{Constrained optimisation and Lagrange
multipliers}
\label{prog:P22}
\index{optimisation!constrained}
\index{Lagrange multiplier}

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{1--6}{say what a constraint does to a minimisation problem, and why
  the unconstrained answer stops being available}
\outcome{7--11}{state the condition that holds at a constrained optimum, and
  say why it is a statement about gradients}
\outcome{12--17}{read a multiplier as a \textbf{price}, and say what its units
  are}
\outcome{18--21}{name the other way to enforce a constraint, and say what
  projecting does and does not buy}
\outcome{22--28}{read the coefficient of a penalty as an exchange rate, and
  say why a hard constraint and a penalty are the same problem}
\outcome{29--35}{recognise the conditions for an inequality constraint, and
  say what a multiplier of zero means}
\end{outcomes}

\begin{quiz}
\item What is true of the gradients of the objective and the constraint at a
  constrained optimum? \teachesat{8--9}
  \answerto{They are parallel. If they were not, there would be a direction
  along the constraint that still improves the objective, so the point would
  not be optimal.}
\item A constraint is relaxed by one unit and the best achievable value
  improves. What is the multiplier? \teachesat{13--14}
  \answerto{The rate of that improvement \dash{} how much objective you buy
  per unit of constraint relaxed. It is a price, and it has the units of
  objective divided by the units of the constraint.}
\item Projecting each step back onto the constraint set keeps every iterate
  feasible. Does it find the constrained optimum? \teachesat{20--21}
  \answerto{Not on its own. Projection answers \emph{which feasible point is
  nearest}, which is a different question from \emph{which feasible point is
  best}. It is a way to stay feasible, and it has to be combined with a
  method that improves the objective.}
\item An objective is written as a reward minus $\beta$ times a penalty. What
  kind of quantity is $\beta$? \teachesat{22--23}
  \answerto{An exchange rate, with units: how much reward you are willing to
  pay per unit of penalty. That is why a $\beta$ tuned against one reward
  model means something different against another.}
\item An inequality constraint is satisfied strictly at the optimum. What is
  its multiplier? \teachesat{30--31}
  \answerto{Zero. A constraint that is not binding is not costing anything, so
  its price is zero \dash{} which is what complementary slackness says.}
\end{quiz}

\section{A best point that is not available}
\label{sec:P22-constraint}
\index{optimisation!feasible set}

\begin{fr}
Every optimisation in this book so far has been free: pick any point, and the
only question was how to get downhill. Program~\ref{prog:P15} found the
direction, Program~\ref{prog:P17} bounded the step and Program~\ref{prog:P20}
built the methods.

Real problems are rarely free. A budget is fixed, a probability has to sum to
one, an update may not move too far from where it started. Take the smallest
possible example:
\[ \text{minimise } f(x,y) = x^{2} + 2y^{2}
   \quad\text{subject to}\quad x + y = \val{p22.c} \]
What is the unconstrained minimum of $f$, and why can we not have it?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
The origin, where $f = 0$ \dash{} and $0 + 0$ is not $\val{p22.c}$.
\end{ansblock}

Which is the whole of what a constraint does: it removes the answer you
wanted. The points that remain are the \textbf{feasible set}, here a straight
line, and the problem is to find the best point \emph{on that line}.

\begin{note}
If the constraint did not exclude the free optimum it would not be doing
anything, and everything in this program would be about a multiplier of zero.
That case is real and it turns up in section 6; for now the constraint bites.
\end{note}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
There is an obvious first move and it is worth taking, because seeing what it
costs is the argument for everything after it.

The constraint gives $y = \val{p22.c} - x$. Substitute, differentiate, solve.
What do you get?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$x = \val{p22.x}$, $y = \val{p22.y}$, and $f = \val{p22.fstar}$.
\end{ansblock}

Substitution works, it is exact, and on this problem it is the right thing to
do. So why does anybody need another method?

Because substitution needs the constraint to be solvable for one variable in
terms of the others, and most are not: a probability vector summing to one in
fifty thousand dimensions, a norm bounded by a constant, a divergence between
two distributions held below a level. \textbf{The method that follows never
solves the constraint}, which is the only reason it is worth learning.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
So look at the answer geometrically instead, because the geometry is what
generalises.

Program~\ref{prog:P15} established something about the gradient and a contour
that this program needs, and it derived it rather than drawing it. What was
it?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
The gradient is \textbf{perpendicular to the contour} through its point.
\end{ansblock}

That came out of $D_u f = \nabla f \cdot u$ read at $\cos\theta = 0$: the
directions along which $f$ does not change are exactly those perpendicular to
its gradient, and those directions are the contour.

Now the same sentence, said about \emph{two} functions at once, is the whole
of this program.

\mermaidfig{p22-two-gradients}{Two curves, and what has to be true where the
best point is.}{two curves, one point}
\end{fr}

\section{Where the two curves touch}
\label{sec:P22-stationarity}
\index{Lagrange multiplier!stationarity condition}

\begin{fr}
Picture a contour of $f$ crossing the constraint line at an angle. You are
standing on the line, at that crossing.

Can you improve?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Yes. Slide along the line, and because the contour crosses rather than touches,
one of the two directions takes you to a lower contour.
\end{ansblock}

So a crossing is never optimal, and the optimum must be where a contour
\textbf{touches} the constraint \dash{} where the two curves have the same
tangent direction.

Say what that makes true of the two gradients.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
They are \textbf{parallel}. Each is perpendicular to its own curve, and at a
touching point the two curves share a tangent, so the two perpendiculars share
a direction.
\end{ansblock}

Parallel means one is a multiple of the other, and that multiple is the whole
of the method:
\[ \nabla f = \lambda \,\nabla g \]
The number $\lambda$ is the \textbf{Lagrange multiplier}. It has arrived as
\emph{whatever ratio makes two parallel vectors equal}, which is not yet an
interpretation \dash{} section 3 is where it becomes one.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Two vector equations plus the constraint is three equations in three unknowns,
so the problem is now algebra. On ours, $\nabla f = (2x, 4y)$ and
$\nabla g = (1, 1)$:
\[ 2x = \lambda, \qquad 4y = \lambda, \qquad x + y = \val{p22.c} \]

Solve them.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$\lambda = \val{p22.lam}$, so $x = \val{p22.x}$ and $y = \val{p22.y}$
\dash{} the same point substitution gave.
\end{ansblock}

The script does this over fractions and asserts that the two gradients really
are parallel at the answer, so the word \emph{exactly} in the last frame is
earned rather than assumed. What it also does is show what has been gained:
\textbf{the constraint was never solved}. It was used, as one more equation,
which is why the method survives a constraint nobody can rearrange.

\begin{note}
The sign and the side of $\lambda$ are conventions and books differ: you will
see $\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g$ and $\nabla f + \lambda\nabla g = 0$, and they
give multipliers of opposite sign for the same problem. Program~\ref{prog:P20}
made the same point about momentum. Read the line, not the letter.
\end{note}
\end{fr}

\section{The multiplier is a price}
\label{sec:P22-price}
\index{Lagrange multiplier!as a price}

\begin{fr}
So far $\lambda$ is a ratio that fell out of some algebra, and if that were all
it were, this program would be a technique. It is not all it is.

Solve the same problem at several levels of the constraint. Here are the
optimal values:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
$x + y = \val{p22.c.lo}$ & best $f$ is $\val{p22.fstar.lo}$, $\lambda = \val{p22.lam.lo}$ \\
$x + y = \val{p22.c}$ & best $f$ is $\val{p22.fstar}$ \\
$x + y = \val{p22.c.hi}$ & best $f$ is $\val{p22.fstar.hi}$, $\lambda = \val{p22.lam.hi}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The best achievable value is a function of where the constraint is set. What
would you want to know about that function?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Its \emph{rate of change} \dash{} how much the best value moves when the
constraint moves.
\end{ansblock}

Which is a derivative, $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}f^{*}}{\mathrm{d}c}$, and it is the
number a person actually wants: it says what relaxing the budget is worth.

Compute it at $c = \val{p22.c.lo}$, $c = \num{2}$ and $c = \val{p22.c}$, and
compare with the multipliers at those levels.

\transcript{p22-lambda-is-slope}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{The two lists are identical, term for term, as exact fractions.}
\[ \lambda \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{d}f^{*}}{\mathrm{d}c} \]
That is not an approximation and not an analogy. The script checks it at
$\val{p22.price.levels}$ levels over fractions, with a step of
$\frac{1}{1000}$, and asserts \emph{equality} \dash{} there is no tolerance
anywhere in it, because a central difference is exact for a quadratic.

\textbf{So a Lagrange multiplier is a price.} It is how much objective you buy
per unit of constraint relaxed, and it has units: objective divided by
constraint. A multiplier is not a bookkeeping variable to be eliminated; it is
usually the most interesting number in the answer.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Read as a price it answers questions the optimum itself does not.

At $c = \val{p22.relax.from}$ the multiplier is $\val{p22.relax.lam}$. So if
somebody offers to raise the budget to $\val{p22.relax.to}$, roughly what
should you expect the best value to change by \dash{} and will the true change
be larger or smaller than that?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
About $\val{p22.relax.lam}$, and the true change is \emph{larger}:
$\val{p22.relax.gain}$.
\end{ansblock}

\textbf{The multiplier prices the first unit, not every unit.} It is a
derivative, so it describes an infinitesimal relaxation; over a whole unit the
curvature of $f^{*}$ adds the rest. Here $f^{*}$ curves upward, so a further
unit costs more than the price of the last one, which is the ordinary shape of
a budget.

\mermaidfig{p22-price}{A level, a best value, and the rate between them.}{a
level and its price}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{note}
This is the same object economics calls a \emph{shadow price} and the same one
that appears in a solver's output beside every constraint. If you have ever
looked at a linear-programming report and wondered what the column of numbers
next to the constraints was for, it was this, and it was telling you which
constraints to spend money on.
\end{note}
\end{fr}

\section{The other way to enforce a constraint}
\label{sec:P22-projection}
\index{projection!as constraint enforcement}

\begin{fr}
Pricing a constraint into the objective is one way to enforce it, and the
answer it gives sits on the constraint because the algebra put it there. There
is a second way, and it is the one that appears in code.

Take any step you like, feasible or not, and then move back to the constraint
set. What is the natural way to move back, and which program has already built
it?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Go to the \textbf{nearest} point of the set. That is
Program~\ref{prog:P05}'s projection.
\end{ansblock}

P05 derived it for a vector onto a line through the origin and proved the
property that matters \dash{} the answer is the closest point rather than
merely a point. The same derivation with the constraint's normal in place of a
vector projects onto our line.

From $(\val{p22.proj.from.x}, \val{p22.proj.from.y})$, which is not feasible,
the projection onto $x + y = \val{p22.c}$ is
$(\val{p22.proj.to.x}, \val{p22.proj.to.y})$, and the script checks it against
$\val{p22.proj.tried}$ other points of the same line: every one is further
away.

\mermaidfig{p22-two-enforcements}{Pricing, and projecting.}{two ways to
enforce}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Now the distinction the section exists for, and it is one people run together.

That projected point is feasible. Is it the constrained optimum?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
No. Its objective value is $\val{p22.proj.f}$ against the optimum's
$\val{p22.fstar}$.
\end{ansblock}

\textbf{Projection answers \emph{which feasible point is nearest}. The
multiplier answers \emph{which feasible point is best}.} They are different
questions and they have different answers, and the only reason to say so
plainly is that projected gradient methods invite the confusion: they step
downhill, project, step downhill, project, and it is the \emph{stepping} that
finds the optimum while the projection only keeps the iterate legal.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{I project onto the constraint set, so my answer satisfies the
constraint and is therefore right.}} It satisfies the constraint, which is half
of what \emph{right} means. Every point of the set satisfies the constraint,
including all the bad ones, and a projection with no descent behind it will
happily sit on the nearest feasible point for ever.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\section{A price you have already been setting}
\label{sec:P22-penalty}
\index{Lagrange multiplier!and penalised objectives}

\begin{fr}
Now the reason this program is in a book for AI engineers, because the object
above is one you have set by hand, probably without a unit in mind.

Objectives of this shape are everywhere:
\[ \text{maximise } \;\mathbb{E}_{p}[r] \;-\; \beta \, D(p \,\Vert\, q) \]
a reward, minus a coefficient times a measure of how far the new distribution
has moved from the old one. Say what $\beta$ looks like, in the vocabulary of
this program.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
A multiplier \dash{} which is to say a \textbf{price}.
\end{ansblock}

And a price for what, in what units? $\beta$ multiplies the divergence and is
subtracted from the reward, so it is \textbf{reward per unit of divergence}:
how much reward you are willing to give up to move one unit further from where
you started.

\begin{rigourbox}
The $D$ above is the \textbf{Kullback--Leibler divergence}, and this program
does not define it \dash{} Program~\ref{prog:P30} does. The one fact used here
is that it is a \textbf{non-negative measure of how far one distribution sits
from another, zero only when they agree}, which is enough to read it as a
quantity you spend. That is a declared forward reference, exactly as
Program~\ref{prog:P18} declares cross-entropy, and it is recorded as such.
\end{rigourbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
That reading is checkable, so it is checked. The solution of the penalised
problem is the tilted distribution $p \propto q\,e^{r/\beta}$
\dash{} stated here and derived in Program~\ref{prog:P26} \dash{} so the
family of solutions can be swept over $\beta$ and the exchange rate measured
directly.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
$\beta$ & divergence spent & reward bought \\
\midrule
$\val{p22.kl.beta.hi}$ & $\val{p22.kl.hi}$ & $\val{p22.kl.r.hi}$ \\
$\val{p22.kl.beta.lo}$ & $\val{p22.kl.lo}$ & $\val{p22.kl.r.lo}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Predict the slope of reward against divergence along that family.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\beta$}
Measured to better than $\val{p22.kl.slope.bound}$ at every $\beta$ tried, over
$\val{p22.kl.betas}$ of them. \textbf{$\beta$ is the exchange rate}, in exactly
the sense section 3 established, and it is the same object as $\lambda$ wearing
a Greek letter people are less suspicious of.

Which turns a tuning knob into a quantity with units, and that has a
consequence: a $\beta$ tuned against one reward model does not transfer to
another, because the two rewards are in different units. It is
Program~\ref{prog:P21}'s point about hyperparameters, one part later and one
level up.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
And the last piece, which the table has already shown you.

Each $\beta$ produced exactly one divergence: $\val{p22.kl.beta.hi}$ gave
$\val{p22.kl.hi}$ and $\val{p22.kl.beta.lo}$ gave $\val{p22.kl.lo}$, and the
script asserts that a smaller $\beta$ always buys more. So say what solving the
penalised problem at a given $\beta$ amounts to.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Solving the \emph{constrained} problem at that divergence level.
\end{ansblock}

\textbf{A hard constraint and a penalty are the same problem, parameterised
differently.} Choose a level and the multiplier comes out; choose a multiplier
and the level comes out. Neither is more fundamental, and which one an
implementation exposes is a matter of what is convenient to control.

That is why a method that clips or constrains an update and a method that
penalises the same quantity are arguing about parameterisation rather than
about objectives, and it is worth being able to say so when the two are
presented as rival ideas.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{$\beta$ is a hyperparameter, so I will tune it.}} You will,
and it is still worth knowing what you are tuning. A $\beta$ is a price in
reward per nat, so \dash{} unlike a learning rate \dash{} it can be reasoned
about before it is searched: what would you pay, in reward, to move one nat
further from the reference?

Measured on the sweep above, dropping $\beta$ from $\val{p22.kl.beta.hi}$ to
$\val{p22.kl.beta.lo}$ spends $\val{p22.kl.spend}$ times the divergence to buy
$\val{p22.kl.buy}$ per cent more reward. Whether that is a good trade is a
judgement about the reward model, and the numbers let you make it rather than
guess.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\section{When the constraint is an inequality}
\label{sec:P22-kkt}
\index{optimisation!KKT conditions}

\begin{fr}
Everything so far had an equality. Most real constraints are inequalities
\dash{} a budget you may not exceed, a divergence you must stay under \dash{}
and the change is smaller than it looks.

An inequality constraint at the optimum is in one of two states. Name them.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Either it is \textbf{tight} \dash{} the optimum sits on the boundary \dash{} or
it is \textbf{slack}, and the optimum is strictly inside.
\end{ansblock}

If it is tight, nothing changes: the boundary is an equality and the previous
five sections apply unaltered.

If it is slack, the constraint is not stopping you doing anything. What is its
price?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Zero}
Because relaxing a constraint that is not binding buys you nothing, and the
price of a thing that buys nothing is nothing.

Those two cases together are one line:
\[ \lambda \, g(x) = 0 \]
which is called \textbf{complementary slackness} and says exactly what you
just said: either the constraint is tight ($g = 0$) or its price is zero, and
never both non-zero at once.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
That line, together with the ones already in hand, is the whole recognition
set. For minimising $f$ subject to $g \le 0$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
$\nabla f + \lambda \nabla g = 0$ & stationarity \dash{} section 2 \\
$g(x) \le 0$ & feasibility \\
$\lambda \ge 0$ & the price of a bound is not negative \\
$\lambda\, g(x) = 0$ & complementary slackness \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

These are the \textbf{KKT conditions}, and they are here to be recognised
rather than developed: this program does not prove that they are necessary,
does not treat the several-constraint case, and does not say when they are
sufficient.

\begin{rigourbox}
What is missing is the constraint qualification \dash{} a condition on the
constraint set without which the conditions can fail to hold at a genuine
optimum \dash{} and the sufficiency argument, which needs
Program~\ref{prog:P19}'s convexity. Both belong to a first course in
optimisation. What survives without them is the reading: each $\lambda$ is a
price, and a zero price means a constraint that is not doing anything.
\end{rigourbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
One consequence is worth having, because it is the third row of the table and
it is easy to skip.

Why must $\lambda$ be non-negative for a constraint written $g \le 0$?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Because relaxing a bound can only help. If the price were negative, loosening
the constraint would make the optimum \emph{worse}, and a larger feasible set
cannot contain a worse best point.
\end{ansblock}

Which is a sanity check you can apply to a solver's output without knowing
anything about the problem: \textbf{a negative multiplier on a $\le$
constraint means the sign convention is not the one you assumed}, and the
note in section 2 said the conventions differ.

That is the program's last practical use of the reading, and it is typical of
it: the interpretation catches a mistake that the algebra alone would not.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Which closes Part VI, and it is worth saying what the four programs have in
common.

Program~\ref{prog:P19} said what convexity promises and what is lost without
it. Program~\ref{prog:P20} took an optimiser apart into one line and a sequence
of repairs. Program~\ref{prog:P21} showed that every complaint about training
noise is a statement about a variance. This program says that every constraint
carries a price, whether or not anybody wrote it down.

None of the four introduced a new object. All four took something already in
the book \dash{} a chord, an average, a spread, a gradient \dash{} and read it
somewhere it had not been read before, which is what an optimisation part is
for.
\end{fr}

\sumitem{1--2}{A constraint removes the answer you wanted. What remains is the

  generalise, because most constraints cannot be rearranged. The method that
  follows \textbf{never solves the constraint}.}
\sumitem{5--6}{Program~\ref{prog:P15}'s result \dash{} the gradient is
  perpendicular to the contour \dash{} said about two functions at once is the
  whole of this program.}
\sumitem{7--8}{A contour \emph{crossing} the constraint is never optimal,
  because you can slide along and improve. The optimum is where they
  \textbf{touch}.}
\sumitem{9}{Touching means the tangents agree, so the two gradients are
  \textbf{parallel}: $\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g$. That is where $\lambda$
  comes from.}
\sumitem{10--11}{Three equations, three unknowns, solved over fractions:
  $\lambda = \val{p22.lam}$ at $(\val{p22.x}, \val{p22.y})$. Sign conventions
  differ between books; read the line, not the letter.}
\sumitem{12--14}{$\lambda = \mathrm{d}f^{*}/\mathrm{d}c$, checked as an
  \textbf{equality} over fractions at $\val{p22.price.levels}$ levels.
  \textbf{A multiplier is a price}, with units of objective per unit of
  constraint.}
\sumitem{15--16}{It prices the \emph{first} unit: relaxing from
  $\val{p22.relax.from}$ to $\val{p22.relax.to}$ gains
  $\val{p22.relax.gain}$ against a multiplier of $\val{p22.relax.lam}$, and
  the difference is the curvature of $f^{*}$.}
\sumitem{17}{It is the same object as a solver's shadow price, and it says
  which constraints are worth money.}
\sumitem{18--19}{The other way to enforce a constraint is to

\sumitem{20--21}{Projection answers \emph{which feasible point is nearest} and
  the multiplier answers \emph{which is best}: $\val{p22.proj.f}$ against
  $\val{p22.fstar}$ here. Projecting keeps an iterate legal; it does not make

\sumitem{22--23}{An objective written as reward minus $\beta$ times a
  divergence has a multiplier in it already, and $\beta$ is a \textbf{price in
  reward per unit of divergence}.}
\sumitem{24--25}{Measured along the family of solutions, the slope of reward
  against divergence is $\beta$ itself, to better than
  $\val{p22.kl.slope.bound}$. So a $\beta$ tuned against one reward model does
  not transfer to another.}
\sumitem{26--28}{Each $\beta$ names one divergence level, so \textbf{a hard
  constraint and a penalty are the same problem} parameterised differently,
  and neither is more fundamental.}
\sumitem{29--31}{An inequality is tight or slack. Tight is an equality; slack
  means the constraint buys nothing, so its price is \textbf{zero}. Together:
  $\lambda g(x) = 0$, complementary slackness.}
\sumitem{32}{The KKT conditions are stationarity, feasibility,
  $\lambda \ge 0$ and complementary slackness \dash{} stated for recognition,
  with the constraint qualification and the sufficiency argument left to a
  first course.}
\sumitem{33--35}{A negative multiplier on a $\le$ constraint means the sign
  convention is not the one you assumed, because a larger feasible set cannot
  have a worse best point.}
\end{summarybox}

\begin{testexercises}
\item Minimise $x^{2} + y^{2}$ subject to $x + 2y = 5$ using the multiplier
  condition. What are $x$, $y$ and $\lambda$?
  \answerto{$\nabla f = (2x, 2y)$ and $\nabla g = (1, 2)$, so $2x = \lambda$
  and $2y = 2\lambda$, giving $x = \lambda/2$ and $y = \lambda$. The constraint
  gives $\lambda/2 + 2\lambda = 5$, so $\lambda = 2$, $x = 1$ and $y = 2$. The
  best value is $5$.}
\item In that problem, the constraint is changed from $5$ to $\num{5.1}$.
  Estimate the new best value, and say whether your estimate is high or low.
  \answerto{The multiplier is $2$, so the best value changes by about
  $2 \times \num{0.1} = \num{0.2}$, giving about $\num{5.2}$. The estimate is
  \emph{low}: $f^{*}$ curves upward, so the true value is a little more. (It
  is $\num{5.202}$.)}
\item A solver reports a multiplier of $0$ on one of your constraints. What
  does that tell you, and what should you do about that constraint?
  \answerto{It is not binding at the optimum \dash{} the answer sits strictly
  inside it \dash{} so relaxing or tightening it slightly changes nothing.
  There is no value in spending anything to loosen it, and if it was added to
  encode a real requirement it is worth checking that it is doing what you
  meant.}
\item An objective is $\mathbb{E}_p[r] - \beta D(p \Vert q)$ with $r$ measured
  in a reward model's arbitrary units. Your colleague ports $\beta$ from a
  project whose reward model was scaled ten times larger. What has happened?
  \answerto{The effective price has changed by a factor of ten. $\beta$ is
  reward per unit of divergence, so scaling the reward by ten while keeping
  $\beta$ makes the penalty ten times weaker relative to the objective \dash{}
  the model will move ten times further from the reference than intended.}
\item Why can a constrained optimum never be better than the unconstrained
  one, and what does that imply about the sign of the multiplier for a
  constraint written $g \le 0$?
  \answerto{The feasible set is a subset of everything, so the best point in
  it cannot beat the best point overall. Relaxing the constraint enlarges the
  set, which can only improve or leave unchanged the best value \dash{} so the
  rate of that improvement, the multiplier, cannot be negative.}
\end{testexercises}

\begin{furtherproblems}
\item Show that for $f = Ax^{2} + By^{2}$ subject to $x + y = c$ the multiplier
  is $\lambda = 2ABc/(A+B)$, and check directly that
  $\mathrm{d}f^{*}/\mathrm{d}c = \lambda$.
  \answerto{Stationarity gives $2Ax = \lambda$ and $2By = \lambda$, so
  $x = \lambda/2A$ and $y = \lambda/2B$; the constraint gives
  $\lambda(1/2A + 1/2B) = c$, i.e. $\lambda = 2ABc/(A+B)$. Then
  $f^{*} = Ax^{2} + By^{2} = \lambda^{2}(1/4A + 1/4B) = \lambda c/2$, and
  substituting $\lambda$ gives $f^{*} = ABc^{2}/(A+B)$, whose derivative is
  $2ABc/(A+B) = \lambda$.}
\item A constraint is written $g(x) = 0$ in one paper and $-g(x) = 0$ in
  another. What happens to the multiplier, and what does that say about
  comparing multipliers between two write-ups?
  \answerto{It changes sign, because $\nabla(-g) = -\nabla g$ and the
  stationarity condition must still hold. So the \emph{magnitude} of a
  multiplier is a property of the problem and its sign is a property of how
  somebody wrote the constraint. Two papers reporting multipliers of opposite
  sign may be reporting the same price.}
\item The penalised objective's solution is $p \propto q e^{r/\beta}$. Without
  deriving it, say what happens to $p$ as $\beta$ grows without bound and as it
  falls towards zero, and relate both to the constraint reading.
  \answerto{As $\beta \to \infty$ the exponent vanishes and $p \to q$: an
  infinite price on divergence means you cannot afford to move at all, which
  is the constrained problem at a divergence level of zero. As
  $\beta \to 0$ the exponential concentrates on whichever outcome has the
  largest $r$, so $p$ approaches a point mass: a price of zero means the
  constraint has been removed, which is the unconstrained problem.}
\item Give an example of an equality constraint for which substitution is
  impossible in practice, and say what the multiplier method does instead.
  \answerto{Requiring a distribution over a fifty-thousand-token vocabulary to
  sum to one: solving for one probability in terms of the other $49\,999$ is
  formally possible and useless, since it breaks the symmetry of the problem
  and makes every derivative carry a term from the eliminated variable. The
  multiplier method adds one unknown and one equation and leaves every
  probability on the same footing \dash{} which is exactly how the softmax's
  normalisation arises when it is derived rather than asserted.}
\item A colleague says the KKT conditions \enquote{prove} their point is
  optimal. What is missing from that claim?
  \answerto{Two things. The conditions are necessary at an optimum only when a
  constraint qualification holds, so a point failing them may still be
  optimal in a badly behaved feasible set; and they are sufficient only under
  convexity, which Program~\ref{prog:P19} says is the property most problems
  in this field do not have. Satisfying KKT makes a point a candidate, and on
  a non-convex problem it makes it one candidate among many.}
\end{furtherproblems}
