\program{The derivative: rate of change}
\label{prog:F11}
\index{derivative!from first principles}
\index{derivative!sign and size}

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{1--6}{compute the average rate of change of a function between two
  points, and say what it is the slope of}
\outcome{7--15}{shrink that gap and say what the slopes settle on, and write
  the definition the settling is}
\outcome{16--20}{say what a real machine does with the definition as the gap
  gets small, and choose a gap accordingly}
\outcome{21--26}{read the sign and the size of a derivative, and say what
  each one is a statement about}
\outcome{27--35}{walk downhill using nothing but the derivative, and say what
  decides whether the walk arrives}
\end{outcomes}

\begin{quiz}
\item A quantity goes from $10$ to $22$ while another goes from $3$ to $5$.
  What is the average rate of change? \teachesat{1--2}
  \answerto{$6$, from $\frac{22 - 10}{5 - 3}$. A change divided by a change.}
\item What line's slope is that? \teachesat{2--3}
  \answerto{The straight line through the two points on the curve, which is
  called a chord.}
\item For $y = x^{2}$, what is the slope of the chord from $x = 3$ to
  $x = 3 + h$? \teachesatone{10}
  \answerto{$6 + h$ exactly, whatever $h$ is.}
\item So what is the derivative of $x^{2}$ at $x = 3$? \teachesat{9--10}
  \answerto{$\val{f11.deriv.at3}$, which is what $6 + h$ settles on as $h$
  shrinks \dash{} and no chord ever equals it.}
\item Is the derivative the slope of a chord with a very small gap?
  \teachesat{11--12}
  \answerto{No. It is the number the chord slopes settle on, which is a
  different kind of statement and is why the definition needs a limit.}
\item A program computes the derivative of $f(x) = x^{2}$ at $x = 3$ as
  $\frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}$, and $h$ is made smaller and smaller. What happens
  to the error? \teachesat{16--17}
  \answerto{It falls, reaches about $\val{f11.fd.best.err}$ at
  $h = \val{f11.fd.best.h}$, and then climbs again.}
\item What does $\frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}$ give at
  $h = \val{f11.fd.vanishes}$? \teachesat{17--18}
  \answerto{Exactly zero here, because $3 + h$ is $3$ in double precision, so
  the numerator is a subtraction of a number from itself. At a smaller $x$ the
  quotient would not have collapsed yet, because the gap between one stored
  number and the next grows with the size of the number.}
\item A derivative is negative. Which way does the curve go as $x$ increases?
  \teachesat{21--22}
  \answerto{Downwards. The sign is the direction.}
\item A derivative is zero. Is that a minimum? \teachesat{23--24}
  \answerto{Not necessarily. It is a place where the curve is momentarily
  level, which a maximum also is.}
\item Point $A$ has a steeper derivative than point $B$. Which is further from
  the minimum? \teachesat{25--26}
  \answerto{You cannot say. The program works a case where the steeper point
  is the nearer one.}
\item You are at $x$ and the derivative there is positive. Which way is
  downhill? \teachesat{27--28}
  \answerto{Towards smaller $x$ \dash{} against the sign of the derivative.}
\item Two walks on $f(x) = (x-3)^{2} + 1$ from $x = \val{f11.gd.start}$ use
  step sizes $\val{f11.gd.small.eta}$ and $\val{f11.gd.big.eta}$. Which gets
  closer in eight steps? \teachesat{30--31}
  \answerto{Neither: they land on exactly the same number,
  $\val{f11.gd.small.end}$, and the program says why.}
\item A step size of $\val{f11.gd.toobig.eta}$ is used instead. What happens?
  \teachesat{32--33}
  \answerto{The walk moves away from the minimum, reaching
  $\val{f11.gd.toobig.end}$ after $\val{f11.gd.steps}$ steps.}
\end{quiz}

\section{A change divided by a change}
\label{sec:F11-average}
\index{derivative!average rate of change}

\begin{fr}
Everything in this program comes from one quantity, and you have computed it
many times without a name for it.

A model's loss is $\num{2.40}$ at one point in training and $\num{2.28}$ a
hundred steps later. How fast is it falling, per step?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$\num{0.0012}$ per step, from $\frac{\num{2.40} - \num{2.28}}{100}$.
\end{ansblock}

A change in one quantity divided by the change in another. That is the
\textbf{average rate of change}, and it is the only idea in the first half of
this program.

Try it on a curve instead of on a training run. Take $y = x^{2}$. What is the
average rate of change between $x = 3$ and $x = 5$?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$8$}
From $\frac{25 - 9}{5 - 3} = \frac{16}{2}$.

Now look at what that number is, geometrically. Mark the two points $(3, 9)$
and $(5, 25)$ on the curve and join them with a straight line. The line goes
$16$ up while it goes $2$ across, so its gradient is $8$ \dash{} which is
Program~\ref{prog:F06}'s $m$ in $y = mx + c$, computed from two points on it.

That straight line has a name. It is a \textbf{chord}: the line through two
points of a curve.

So an average rate of change is the gradient of a chord, and the two phrases
mean the same thing.

Which raises the question this program exists for, and it is worth seeing why
the question is forced. The number $8$ was not a property of the curve at
$x = 3$; it was a property of the curve between $3$ and $5$. Take the second
point at $x = 6$ instead and the chord's slope is $\frac{36 - 9}{3} = 9$. Same
starting point, different answer, and neither answer is wrong \dash{} they are
answers to different questions.

So what would you have to do to get the slope of the curve \emph{at} $x = 3$,
rather than between $3$ and something?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Bring the second point closer to the first.
\end{ansblock}

Which is the whole idea, and the rest of the program is what happens when you
take it seriously.

Notice first why the question is not silly. At $x = 3$ alone there is only one
point, and a slope needs two \dash{} a change divided by a change, and a
single point has no change to divide. So \enquote{the slope at a point} is not
obviously a thing that exists, and the mathematics has to be careful about
what it means before it can compute it.

\mermaidfig{f11-chord-to-tangent}{Two points make a chord; bringing them
together is the whole of the definition.}{from a chord to a tangent}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
One more piece of vocabulary before the shrinking starts.

The gap between the two $x$ values will be written $h$, so the second point is
at $x + h$, and the chord's slope is
\[ \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} \]
which is the change in $y$ over the change in $x$ with the names filled in.
Check it against the last calculation: at $x = 3$ with $h = 2$ it gives
$\frac{f(5) - f(3)}{2} = \frac{16}{2} = 8$, which is what we got.

\begin{note}
This expression is called a \emph{difference quotient}, and both words are
literal: a difference on the top, a quotient overall. It is not a new object
\dash{} it is the average rate of change with the letters arranged so that
one of them can be made small.
\end{note}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Two remarks on $h$, both of which matter later.

$h$ is allowed to be \textbf{negative}, which puts the second point to the
left. The chord is then drawn from the other side and its slope comes out the
same as $h$ shrinks, which is a fact worth checking once and not worth
labouring.

$h$ is \textbf{never zero}. Setting $h = 0$ gives $\frac{0}{0}$, which is not
a number and not a very small number either \dash{} it is an expression with
no value. Every difficulty in the next section comes from wanting to reach a
place the expression is not defined at.
\end{fr}

\section{Shrinking the chord}
\label{sec:F11-shrinking}
\index{derivative!from first principles}

\begin{fr}
Take $y = x^{2}$ at $x = 3$ and shrink $h$. Work out the chord's slope at
$h = 1$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{f11.chord.1}$}
From $\frac{16 - 9}{1} = \frac{7}{1}$.

Now $h = \num{0.1}$, then $h = \num{0.01}$, then $h = \num{0.001}$. You do not
have to compute all three; predict the pattern from the first two.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule
$h$ & chord slope \\
\midrule
$1$ & $\val{f11.chord.1}$ \\
$\num{0.1}$ & $\val{f11.chord.01}$ \\
$\num{0.01}$ & $\val{f11.chord.001}$ \\
$\num{0.001}$ & $\val{f11.chord.0001}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{ansblock}

The slopes are heading somewhere obvious. Where?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{f11.deriv.at3}$}
And the algebra says so exactly, which is better than four rows suggesting it.
Expand the difference quotient with $f(x) = x^{2}$ and $x = 3$:
\[ \frac{(3+h)^{2} - 3^{2}}{h} = \frac{9 + 6h + h^{2} - 9}{h}
   = \frac{6h + h^{2}}{h} = 6 + h \]
using Program~\ref{prog:F02}'s expansion of $(a+b)^{2}$, and the cancellation
of $h$ is allowed \emph{because $h$ is not zero}, which is why the previous
section insisted on it.

\code{code/f11\_derivative.py} checks that the chord's slope is $6 + h$ for
every $h$, at four hundred values. As $h$ gets small the slope gets close to
$\val{f11.deriv.at3}$ and never reaches it, because reaching it would need
$h = 0$ and there is no chord then.

\textbf{$\val{f11.deriv.at3}$ is the number the slopes settle on}, and that
number is the \textbf{derivative} of $x^{2}$ at $x = 3$.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The phrase \enquote{settles on} is doing real work and it is worth one frame.

It does not mean \enquote{the value at $h = 0$}, because there is no value
there. It does not mean \enquote{the value at a very small $h$}, because every
such value is $6 + h$ and none of them is $6$. What it means is: \textbf{you can get the chord slope as close to
$\val{f11.deriv.at3}$ as anybody demands, by choosing $h$ small enough.} Demand within a thousandth and $h = \num{0.0005}$
does it; demand within a millionth and a smaller $h$ does.

That is a \textbf{limit}, and the notation is
\[ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} \]
where $f'$ is read \enquote{$f$ prime}. The arrow means \emph{towards} and
never \emph{at}.

Is the derivative the slope of a chord with a very small gap?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{No}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{The derivative is not defined as a chord slope.} Every chord slope
for $x^{2}$ at $3$ is $6 + h$, and none of those is $6$. The derivative is the
number they approach, which is a statement about the whole family of chords
rather than about any one of them. On a straight line the two happen to agree,
as the next two frames show; the definition never says they must.

That distinction sounds like pedantry until section 3, where a machine is
asked to shrink $h$ and the answer gets \emph{worse}. If the derivative were
merely \enquote{the chord with a tiny gap}, that could not happen.

The habit worth taking: when you see $\lim_{h \to 0}$, read it as
\emph{as close as you like, by choosing $h$}, not as \emph{at $h = 0$}. The
first is a promise you can check; the second is an expression with no value.
\end{trapbox}

Do the same for $f(x) = x^{2}$ at a general $x$. Expand and cancel.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
\[ \frac{(x+h)^{2} - x^{2}}{h} = \frac{2xh + h^{2}}{h} = 2x + h
   \quad\longrightarrow\quad f'(x) = 2x \]
\end{ansblock}

Check it against the last section: at $x = 3$ that is
$\val{f11.deriv.at3}$, as before.

Two more, both quick, and both worth doing rather than reading. What is the
derivative of $f(x) = c$, a constant?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$0$}
Because the difference quotient is $\frac{c - c}{h} = 0$ for every $h$, so the
slopes do not merely settle on zero \dash{} they are zero all along. A flat
line has no slope anywhere, which is what the answer says.

And $f(x) = mx + c$?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$m$}
From $\frac{m(x+h) + c - mx - c}{h} = \frac{mh}{h} = m$, again for every $h$.
A straight line's slope is the same everywhere and equal to the $m$
Program~\ref{prog:F06} named, which is the one case where the derivative tells
you nothing you did not already have.

Three results so far:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
$f(x)$ & $f'(x)$ \\
\midrule
$c$ & $0$ \\
$mx + c$ & $m$ \\
$x^{2}$ & $2x$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

There is a pattern in there and you may well have spotted it. Do not go
looking for the general rule yet: Program~\ref{prog:F12} states it, along with
three more, and adds the one rule for differentiating a function of a
function. What matters here is that all three
came out of \emph{one} definition and a page of algebra, so the rules are
consequences rather than facts to be issued.
\end{fr}

\section{What a machine does when you shrink $h$}
\label{sec:F11-machine}
\index{derivative!and the finite difference}

\begin{fr}
The definition says: make $h$ small and the quotient gets close. So write it
out and make $h$ small. What could go wrong?

Here is $\frac{f(3+h) - f(3)}{h}$ for $f(x) = x^{2}$, computed at seventeen
sizes of $h$. The true answer is $\val{f11.deriv.at3}$.

Before you look: at which $h$ do you expect the smallest error?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Not the smallest one.
\end{ansblock}

\transcript{f11-shrinking-h}

The error falls exactly as the mathematics says it should \dash{} it is $h$
itself, because the quotient is $6 + h$ \dash{} down to about
$\val{f11.fd.err.8}$ at $h = \val{f11.fd.best.h}$. And then it \textbf{climbs
back}, through $\val{f11.fd.err.12}$ at $h = 10^{-12}$, to
$\val{f11.fd.err.16}$ at $h = \val{f11.fd.vanishes}$ \dash{} which is the
whole answer, wrong.

The last row gives the value at $h = \val{f11.fd.vanishes}$. Why is it
exactly that?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Because at that size \textbf{$3 + h$ is $3$}.
\end{ansblock}

The machine has no room to record the difference, so the numerator is a number
subtracted from itself, the quotient is $\frac{0}{h}$, and the last row of the
table reads $\val{f11.fd.approx.16}$.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{Smaller $h$ is more accurate} is true in the mathematics and
false on the machine}, and the two part company at about
$h = \val{f11.fd.best.h}$.

Two errors are being traded and they pull opposite ways. Make $h$ big and the
chord is not yet the tangent, which is the error the definition is about. Make
$h$ small and the two values of $f$ become so close that subtracting them
throws away most of the digits that distinguished them \dash{} and then you
divide the wreckage by something tiny, which magnifies it.

Program~\ref{prog:P01} is where the right-hand branch gets its explanation,
because it is a fact about how a machine stores a number rather than about
calculus. What belongs here is the shape: \textbf{the error curve is a U, and
the bottom of it is not at the smallest $h$ you can type.}
\end{trapbox}

\mermaidfig{f11-two-errors}{Two errors pulling opposite ways, and the U they
make between them.}{why shrinking the step stops helping}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The practical consequence is worth stating plainly, because a version of this
appears in every framework's test suite.

Checking a derivative by finite differences is a real and useful technique
\dash{} it is how an autodiff implementation is tested \dash{} and it works
when $h$ is chosen near the bottom of the U rather than as small as possible.
The best row above is $h = \val{f11.fd.best.h}$, with an error of
$\val{f11.fd.best.err}$, and that is about as well as this method does for a
quantity of this size.

\begin{aibox}
That is why a gradient check reports agreement to a handful of digits rather
than to all of them, and why one that demands more fails on correct code. The
comparison is between an exact derivative and an approximation whose best
possible error is around the square root of the machine's precision, so the
tolerance has to be set to what the method can deliver.

An implementation that passes a gradient check to three or four digits has
almost certainly got the derivative right. One that agrees to fifteen is
suspicious, because the check itself cannot be that good.
\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
One caution about reading the table, because it is a claim about one
computation rather than about arithmetic in general.

The turning point sits where it does because of the size of the numbers
involved: $f(3) = 9$, and the difference being taken is against a value of
about $9$. Do the same sweep at $x = \val{f11.fd.big.x}$, where $f$ is nine
million, and the best $h$ moves from $\val{f11.fd.best.h}$ to
$\val{f11.fd.big.h}$ \dash{} a thousandfold \dash{} because the digits
available to record a difference are the digits left over after representing a
much larger number.

\textbf{So the shape of the curve is the transferable part and the position of
its bottom is not.} That is the same distinction Program~\ref{prog:F09} drew
between an identity and a claim about arrangement, and it is worth making
every time a measurement is quoted.
\end{fr}

\section{Reading a derivative}
\label{sec:F11-reading}
\index{derivative!sign and size}

\begin{fr}
A derivative is a number, and there are exactly two things to read off it: its
sign and its size. This section does both, and section 5 uses them.

Take $f(x) = x^{2}$, whose derivative is $2x$. At $x = -4$ the derivative is
$-8$. As $x$ increases from $-4$, is the curve going up or down?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Down}
Because the slope is negative, and a negative slope is a line going down as
you move right \dash{} which is Program~\ref{prog:F06}'s reading of $m$,
applied to the tangent instead of to the whole line.

So the sign is the direction:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
$f'(x) > 0$ & the curve rises as $x$ increases \\
$f'(x) < 0$ & the curve falls as $x$ increases \\
$f'(x) = 0$ & the curve is momentarily level \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

At $x = 4$ the derivative of $x^{2}$ is $8$ and at $x = 1$ it is $2$. What
does the difference between $8$ and $2$ tell you?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
The curve is four times as steep at $x = 4$ as at $x = 1$.
\end{ansblock}

And that is all it tells you: how steeply the curve rises \emph{just there}. A
derivative is a local quantity. It is computed from what the function does in
an arbitrarily small neighbourhood of one point, so it cannot carry
information about anywhere else \dash{} not about where the minimum is, not
about how far away it is, not about whether there is another one.

That will matter enormously in a moment. First, the third row of the table.
The derivative of $x^{2}$ is zero at $x = 0$. Is that a minimum?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Here, yes}
But not because the derivative is zero.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{A zero derivative says the curve is level, and a maximum is level
too.} So is the flat spot in the middle of a curve that goes up, levels off,
and carries on up.

Take $f(x) = -x^{2}$. Every chord slope on it is the negative of $x^{2}$'s,
so its derivative is $-2x$, which is zero at $x = 0$, and
$x = 0$ is its \emph{maximum}. The derivative cannot tell the two apart,
because it is computed from an arbitrarily small neighbourhood and both look
level there.

The word for a place with a zero derivative is a \textbf{stationary point},
and it is deliberately non-committal. Which kind it is takes more information
than the first derivative has: it takes the derivative of the derivative, which
Program~\ref{prog:F12} gives you the rules to compute and
Program~\ref{prog:P17} turns into a statement about curvature. And whether a
minimum you have found is the only one is a further question again, which is
Program~\ref{prog:P19}'s.

Until then: \textbf{a zero gradient means the walk has stopped, and stopping
is not the same as arriving.}
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Now the size, and the misconception that costs the most.

Here is $f(x) = x^{4} - 4x^{2}$, which has a minimum at
$x = \val{f11.min.x}$. Consider two points on it: $x = \val{f11.steep.x}$,
where the derivative is $\val{f11.steep.slope}$, and $x =
\val{f11.shallow.x}$, where it is $\val{f11.shallow.slope}$. Take those two
slopes on trust for the moment \dash{} expanding the quotient for a fourth
power is a page of algebra, and Program~\ref{prog:F12} is where the rules that
make it a line arrive. What the frame turns on is the two numbers, not where
they came from.

And the signs say what this section has already made them say: negative at
$x = \val{f11.shallow.x}$ means the curve is still falling as $x$ increases, so
that point sits before the minimum, and positive at $x = \val{f11.steep.x}$
means it is rising again, so that one sits after it. The sign has settled which
side of the minimum each point is on. The size is being asked for something
else.

One of those points is much steeper than the other. Which is further from the
minimum?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
The \emph{shallower} one, by twice as much.
\end{ansblock}

$x = \val{f11.steep.x}$ is $\val{f11.steep.dist}$ from the minimum and
$x = \val{f11.shallow.x}$ is $\val{f11.shallow.dist}$ from it.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{A big derivative does not mean a long way to go.} It means the ground
is steep here, and steep ground can sit a step from the bottom of a narrow
pit, or at the top of a long run that flattens out for miles.

This is the previous frame's point with a number attached: the derivative is
computed from an arbitrarily small neighbourhood, so it \emph{cannot} know how
far away anything is. Reading distance out of it is asking one number to carry
information it was never given.

The consequence people meet: \enquote{the gradient is still large, so we are
far from convergence} is not an inference. Neither is its opposite \dash{} a
small gradient can mean the bottom, and it can equally mean a long flat
stretch with the bottom a long way off, which is exactly what a saturated unit
produces and what Program~\ref{prog:F07} measured.
\end{trapbox}

\mermaidfig{f11-sign-and-size}{The sign is a direction and the size is a
steepness, and neither is a distance.}{what a derivative does and does not
say}
\end{fr}

\section{Walking downhill}
\label{sec:F11-downhill}
\index{derivative!and walking downhill}

\begin{fr}
You are standing on a curve at some $x$, you cannot see the curve, and the
only thing you are allowed to ask for is the derivative at your feet.

The derivative comes back positive. Which way is downhill?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Towards smaller $x$.
\end{ansblock}

Because a positive derivative means the curve rises as $x$ increases, so it
falls as $x$ decreases. Downhill is \textbf{against the sign of the
derivative}, always, and that single sentence is the entire steering mechanism
of everything this book will later call training.

Write it as an instruction. You are at $x$ and you want to move downhill by a
step of some size $\eta$. Where do you go?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
\[ x \;\longleftarrow\; x - \eta\, f'(x) \]
\end{ansblock}

The minus sign is \enquote{against the derivative}. The $\eta$ \dash{} the
Greek letter eta \dash{} is how far you go, and it is a choice rather than
something the derivative told you, because by the last section the derivative
does not know how far anything is.

\textbf{That is gradient descent, and there is nothing else in it.} Every
optimiser this book discusses later is that line with a better answer to
\emph{how far}, and Program~\ref{prog:P20} is where they get compared.

Try it. $f(x) = (x-3)^{2} + 1$, and expanding
$\frac{((x-3)+h)^{2} - (x-3)^{2}}{h}$ exactly as in frame 13 gives
$2(x-3) + h$, so $f'(x) = 2(x-3)$. Start at
$x = \val{f11.gd.start}$ with $\eta = \val{f11.gd.small.eta}$. Where does one
step take you?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\num{8.6}$}
From $10 - \num{0.1} \times 14$. The derivative at $10$ is $14$, positive, so
the step goes left, which is downhill.

Do it $\val{f11.gd.steps}$ times and you arrive at
$\val{f11.gd.small.end}$, closing most of the gap to $3$.

Now the same walk with a much bigger step, $\eta =
\val{f11.gd.big.eta}$. Predict where it ends up after the same
$\val{f11.gd.steps}$ steps: nearer than $\val{f11.gd.small.end}$, or further?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Exactly the same: $\val{f11.gd.big.end}$}
Which is not a coincidence and is the best thing in this program.

Substitute the update into this particular $f$. The step is
$\eta \times 2(x-3)$, so
\[ x - 3 \;\longleftarrow\; (x - 3) - 2\eta(x - 3) = (1 - 2\eta)(x - 3) \]

\textbf{The distance to the minimum is multiplied by $1 - 2\eta$ at every
step.} At $\eta = \val{f11.gd.small.eta}$ that factor is
$\val{f11.gd.factor.small}$; at $\eta = \val{f11.gd.big.eta}$ it is
$-\val{f11.gd.factor.big}$. Same size, opposite sign.

So the small step crawls down one side, and the big step jumps over the
minimum every single time and lands the same fraction closer on the far side.
After an even number of steps they are in the same place, to the digit.
Asserted in the script one step at a time and compounded over sixty, at three
hundred values of $\eta$.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
That expression answers the question the previous frames kept deferring.

The walk arrives when $\lvert 1 - 2\eta \rvert < 1$, because a distance
multiplied repeatedly by something smaller than one goes to zero. It runs away
when $\lvert 1 - 2\eta \rvert > 1$. And at $\eta = \val{f11.gd.perfect}$ the
factor is exactly zero, so the walk lands on the minimum in \emph{one step}
\dash{} which is a warning as much as a result, because that value depends
entirely on this $f$ and you will not be handed it in general.

Notice what that range does and does not say. It is a statement about this
$f$ and no other: $2$ is the number that turns up in $1 - 2\eta$ because
$f'(x) = 2(x-3)$, and a different curve puts a different number there. What
transfers is the shape of the condition \dash{} that there is an upper limit
on the step, that it is a property of the function rather than of the
gradient, and that exceeding it does not merely slow the walk down.

Try $\eta = \val{f11.gd.toobig.eta}$ from the same start. Predict the sign of
the outcome before computing it: does the walk get closer or further away?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Further, and fast}
The factor is $-\val{f11.gd.factor.toobig}$, whose size exceeds one, so every
step multiplies the distance by $\val{f11.gd.factor.toobig}$ and flips its
sign. After $\val{f11.gd.steps}$ steps the walk is at
$\val{f11.gd.toobig.end}$, having started at $\val{f11.gd.start}$ and been
aiming at $3$.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Too large a step does not overshoot once and recover; it overshoots
further every time.} The walk oscillates and the oscillation grows, which is
what \enquote{the loss exploded} looks like from the inside.

Notice what the derivative did wrong here: \emph{nothing}. It pointed downhill
correctly at every single step. The direction was right every time and the
distance was too far, and no amount of asking the derivative more carefully
would have helped, because it does not know how far anything is.

What bounds the step size is the \emph{curvature} rather than the gradient,
and Program~\ref{prog:P17} derives the bound. What you have here is the
mechanism and the failure, which is enough to recognise the failure when you
meet it.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
One more property of the walk, and it is the one that makes gradient descent
worth using rather than merely definable.

Look at what the walk needed. It needed the derivative at the point it was
standing on, and nothing else: not the shape of the curve, not where the
minimum is, not the values of $f$ anywhere but here. It never even evaluated
$f$.

That is why the method survives into problems where $f$ is a loss over
billions of parameters and nobody could draw it or search it. \textbf{A local
quantity is enough to steer by}, and the price of using only local
information is everything section 4 said: no sense of distance, no way to tell
a minimum from a maximum, and a step size that has to come from somewhere
else.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
A closing observation, which is what this program has been building.

The derivative was defined by shrinking a chord, which is a piece of geometry
about a curve on a page. Nothing about that definition mentioned optimisation,
loss, or training. And yet the last section's instruction \dash{} step against
the derivative \dash{} came out of the definition and one reading of a sign.

\textbf{Gradient descent is not an algorithm somebody invented on top of
calculus. It is what the definition of a derivative says to do}, once you want
to go downhill and can only see your feet.

What Program~\ref{prog:F12} adds is the ability to compute $f'$ for functions
built out of other functions, which is what turns this from a demonstration on
$(x-3)^{2}$ into something that works on a network.
\end{fr}

\begin{summarybox}
\sumitem{1--3}{\result{\textbf{An average rate of change is a change divided by a
  change}, and geometrically it is the gradient of a \textbf{chord} \dash{}
  the straight line through two points of a curve. On $y = x^{2}$ between
  $x = 3$ and $x = 5$ it is $8$}.}
\sumitem{4--6}{\result{Written with a gap $h$ the chord's slope is
  $\frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}$. $h$ may be negative and is \textbf{never zero}:
  at $h = 0$ the expression is $\frac{0}{0}$, which is not a number and not a
  small one either, and every difficulty in section 3 comes from wanting to
  reach a place the expression is not defined at}.}
\sumitem{7--10}{\result{For $x^{2}$ at $x = 3$ the chord slope is \textbf{$6 + h$
  exactly}, so the slopes run $\val{f11.chord.1}$, $\val{f11.chord.01}$,
  $\val{f11.chord.001}$, $\val{f11.chord.0001}$ and settle on
  $\val{f11.deriv.at3}$ \dash{} which is the \textbf{derivative} of $x^{2}$
  there. The cancellation of $h$ is allowed precisely because $h$ is not
  zero}.}
\sumitem{11--12}{\result{\textbf{The derivative is not defined as a chord
  slope.} Every chord slope here is $6 + h$ and none of them is $6$; the
  derivative is the number they approach, and $\lim_{h \to 0}$ means \emph{as
  close as you like, by choosing $h$}, never \emph{at $h = 0$}}.}
\sumitem{13--15}{\result{From the definition alone: $x^{2}$ gives $2x$, a constant
  gives $0$, and $mx + c$ gives $m$. There is a pattern and
  Program~\ref{prog:F12} states it \dash{} what matters here is that all three
  came out of one definition and a page of algebra, so the rules are
  consequences rather than facts to be issued}.}
\sumitem{16--18}{\result{\textbf{Smaller $h$ is more accurate in the mathematics and
  false on the machine.} The error falls to $\val{f11.fd.best.err}$ at
  $h = \val{f11.fd.best.h}$ and then climbs back to $\val{f11.fd.err.16}$ at
  $h = \val{f11.fd.vanishes}$, where $3 + h$ \emph{is} $3$ and the quotient is
  exactly $\val{f11.fd.approx.16}$. Two errors pull opposite ways and the
  curve between them is a U. Program~\ref{prog:P01} owns the right-hand
  branch}.}
\sumitem{19--20}{\result{So a gradient check agrees to a handful of digits and not to
  all of them, and one demanding more fails on correct code. \textbf{The shape
  of the curve transfers and the position of its bottom does not} \dash{} it
  moves with the size of the numbers being subtracted}.}
\sumitem{21--23}{\result{\textbf{The sign is a direction}: positive rises, negative
  falls, zero is momentarily level. \textbf{The size is a steepness, just
  here.} A derivative is computed from an arbitrarily small neighbourhood, so
  it cannot carry information about anywhere else}.}
\sumitem{24}{\result{\textbf{A zero derivative is a \emph{stationary point}, not a
  minimum}: a maximum is level too, and so is a flat spot on the way up. Which
  kind it is takes more than the first derivative \dash{}
  Program~\ref{prog:F12} gives the rules for the second derivative and
  Program~\ref{prog:P17} turns it into curvature. Stopping is not the same as
  arriving}.}
\sumitem{25--26}{\result{\textbf{A big derivative does not mean a long way to go.} On
  $x^{4} - 4x^{2}$ the point $x = \val{f11.steep.x}$ has slope
  $\val{f11.steep.slope}$ and is $\val{f11.steep.dist}$ from the minimum,
  while $x = \val{f11.shallow.x}$ has slope $\val{f11.shallow.slope}$ and is
  $\val{f11.shallow.dist}$ away. The steeper point is the nearer one}.}
\sumitem{27--29}{\result{Downhill is \textbf{against the sign of the derivative}, and
  the instruction is $x \leftarrow x - \eta f'(x)$. \textbf{That is gradient
  descent and there is nothing else in it}; $\eta$ is a choice, because by the
  previous section the derivative does not know how far anything is}.}
\sumitem{30--32}{\result{On $(x-3)^{2} + 1$ the update is exactly
  $x - 3 \leftarrow (1 - 2\eta)(x-3)$, so \textbf{the distance is multiplied
  by $1 - 2\eta$ every step}. At $\eta = \val{f11.gd.small.eta}$ and
  $\eta = \val{f11.gd.big.eta}$ that factor is
  $\pm\val{f11.gd.factor.small}$, so one walk crawls down and the other jumps
  the minimum every step and both reach $\val{f11.gd.small.end}$ to the digit.
  The walk arrives when $\lvert 1 - 2\eta \rvert < 1$}.}
\sumitem{33--35}{\result{\textbf{Too large a step overshoots further every time}: at
  $\eta = \val{f11.gd.toobig.eta}$ the factor is
  $-\val{f11.gd.factor.toobig}$ and eight steps take the walk from
  $\val{f11.gd.start}$ to $\val{f11.gd.toobig.end}$. The derivative did
  nothing wrong \dash{} it pointed downhill every time, and it does not know
  how far. What bounds the step is curvature, and that is
  Program~\ref{prog:P17}'s. The walk needed only the derivative at its feet
  and never evaluated $f$, which is why a local quantity is enough to steer
  by \dash{} and \textbf{gradient descent is not an algorithm on top of
  calculus, it is what the definition says to do} once you want to go downhill
  and can only see your feet}.}
\end{summarybox}

\begin{testexercises}
\item A quantity goes from $8$ to $20$ as another goes from $1$ to $4$. Give
  the average rate of change.
  \answerto{$4$, from $\frac{20-8}{4-1}$.}
\item For $f(x) = x^{2}$, give the chord slope between $x = 2$ and $x = 2 + h$
  in terms of $h$.
  \answerto{$4 + h$, by the same expansion: $\frac{4 + 4h + h^{2} - 4}{h}$.}
\item Hence give $f'(2)$.
  \answerto{$4$, which is $2x$ at $x = 2$.}
\item Why is $h = 0$ not allowed in the difference quotient?
  \answerto{Because it gives $\frac{0}{0}$, which has no value \dash{} and
  because the cancellation of $h$ that produces the answer requires dividing
  by it.}
\item Give the derivative of $f(x) = 7$ and of $f(x) = 5x - 2$.
  \answerto{$0$ and $5$. A constant has no slope anywhere; a straight line has
  the same slope everywhere.}
\item A finite difference is computed with $h = 10^{-15}$ and the answer is
  badly wrong. Give the reason in one sentence, and give a better $h$.
  \answerto{The two values of $f$ are too close for the subtraction to keep
  the digits that distinguished them. Something near
  $\val{f11.fd.best.h}$ is about the best this method does.}
\item Somebody proposes checking a gradient to twelve decimal places by finite
  differences. What is wrong with the proposal?
  \answerto{The method's own best error here is about
  $\val{f11.fd.best.err}$, so it cannot deliver twelve places and the check
  would fail on correct code.}
\item $f'(x) = -3$ at some point. Is the curve rising or falling there, and
  how steeply compared with a point where $f'(x) = 1$?
  \answerto{Falling, and three times as steep.}
\item $f(x) = -x^{2}$ has $f'(0) = 0$. Is $x = 0$ a minimum?
  \answerto{No, it is the maximum. A zero derivative says level, and a maximum
  is level.}
\item Two points on a curve have derivatives $\num{0.1}$ and $\num{40}$. Which
  is nearer its minimum?
  \answerto{Unanswerable from those two numbers. A derivative is local and
  carries no information about distance.}
\item You are at $x = -2$ and $f'(-2) = -6$. Give the next point with
  $\eta = \num{0.25}$.
  \answerto{$-\num{0.5}$, from $-2 - \num{0.25} \times (-6)$. The negative
  derivative sends the step to the right.}
\item On $f(x) = (x-3)^{2} + 1$, give the factor by which the distance to the
  minimum is multiplied each step at $\eta = \num{0.25}$.
  \answerto{$\num{0.5}$, from $1 - 2 \times \num{0.25}$.}
\item At which $\eta$ does that walk reach the minimum in one step, and why is
  that not a useful general fact?
  \answerto{$\val{f11.gd.perfect}$, where the factor is zero. It depends
  entirely on this $f$, and in general nobody hands you the right value.}
\item Give the range of $\eta$ for which that walk converges at all.
  \answerto{$0 < \eta < 1$, which is where $\lvert 1 - 2\eta \rvert < 1$.}
\end{testexercises}

\begin{furtherproblems}
\item Derive $f'(x)$ for $f(x) = x^{3}$ from the definition.
  \answerto{$\frac{(x+h)^{3} - x^{3}}{h} = 3x^{2} + 3xh + h^{2}$, which
  settles on $3x^{2}$.}
\item Show that the chord slope of $x^{2}$ at $x$ comes out the same for $h$
  and for $-h$ as they shrink.
  \answerto{The quotient is $2x + h$ either way, so both approach $2x$. The
  chord drawn from the left and the one from the right settle on one number,
  which is what makes the derivative well defined.}
\item Explain why the error of the finite difference on $x^{2}$ is exactly $h$
  in exact arithmetic.
  \answerto{The quotient is $2x + h$ and the derivative is $2x$, so the
  difference is $h$ itself. That is the left-hand branch of the U and it is
  why the table's first few rows fall by a factor of ten each time.}
\item Would using $\frac{f(x+h) - f(x-h)}{2h}$ instead move the bottom of the
  U? Say what you would measure to find out.
  \answerto{On $x^{2}$ it removes the left-hand branch altogether: the $h$
  terms cancel exactly, so the quotient is $2x$ for every $h$ and the only
  error left is the machine's. On a curve whose quotient keeps an $h^{2}$
  \dash{} $x^{3}$, where it is $3x^{2} + h^{2}$ \dash{} that branch falls like
  $h^{2}$ rather than like $h$, so the bottom moves to a larger $h$ and a
  smaller error. Measure it the same way: sweep $h$ over many decades against a
  known derivative and plot the error.}
\item A function's derivative is zero at three different points. What can you
  say about it, and what can you not?
  \answerto{It is level at three places. Which of them are minima, which are
  maxima and which are neither takes more than the first derivative, and so
  does whether any of them is the lowest.}
\item Give a function and two points on it where the derivative is larger at
  the point nearer the minimum. Say how you built it.
  \answerto{Any curve with a narrow basin and a wide shallow one \dash{}
  $x^{4} - 4x^{2}$ is the program's own. Put one point on the steep wall of
  the narrow basin and the other on the shallow slope far out.}
\item The update is $x \leftarrow x - \eta f'(x)$. What would $x \leftarrow x
  + \eta f'(x)$ do, and when might somebody want it?
  \answerto{Walk uphill. It is what you want when the quantity is to be
  maximised rather than minimised, which is why the same machinery is
  described as ascent in some settings and descent in others.}
\item On $(x-3)^{2}+1$ with $\eta = 1$, describe the walk from $x = 10$.
  \answerto{The factor is $\lvert 1 - 2 \rvert = 1$, so the distance never
  changes: the walk bounces between $10$ and $-4$ for ever. It neither
  converges nor diverges, which is the boundary case.}
\item Why can no choice of $\eta$ rescue a walk on a curve whose derivative is
  zero at the starting point but which is not at a minimum?
  \answerto{Because the step is $\eta$ times zero whatever $\eta$ is, so the
  walk does not move. The mechanism has no way to leave a stationary point,
  which is a real limitation and not a tuning problem.}
\item This program never evaluated $f$ during the walk. Say what that buys and
  what it costs.
  \answerto{It buys applicability: the method works where $f$ is a loss over
  billions of parameters and nobody could search it. It costs every kind of
  global knowledge \dash{} no sense of distance, no way to tell a minimum from
  a maximum, and a step size that has to come from somewhere else.}
\end{furtherproblems}
