\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{1--7}{say what a minibatch gradient estimates, and what
  \emph{unbiased} does and does not promise}
\outcome{8--12}{say how the noise depends on the batch size, and why the
  dependence is a square root}
\outcome{13--17}{say why an occasional enormous step happens at all, and what
  a clipping threshold is a decision about}
\outcome{18--22}{state what the linear and the square-root scaling rules each
  hold fixed, and say which is folklore}
\outcome{23--25}{say what a smoothed loss curve hides and by how much}
\outcome{26--30}{say when accumulating micro-batches is not the same as one
  large batch, and why it is silent}
\outcome{31--39}{name the two ways to get a gradient through a sampling step,
  and say what decides between them}
\end{outcomes}

\begin{quiz}
\item A minibatch gradient is called \emph{unbiased}. What does that promise?
  \teachesat{3--4}
  \answerto{That averaging over every batch that could have been drawn gives
  the full gradient exactly. It promises nothing whatever about the batch you
  actually drew.}
\item You increase the batch size sixteenfold. By what factor does the noise
  in the gradient fall? \teachesat{10--11}
  \answerto{Four. The variance falls like $1/B$, so the spread \dash{} which
  is what you see \dash{} falls like $1/\sqrt{B}$. That square root is where
  the diminishing return comes from, and it is exact rather than a heuristic.}
\item You halve the batch size. What should happen to the learning rate?
  \teachesat{18--19}
  \answerto{It depends on what you want to hold fixed, and the two common
  rules hold different things. Halving the step size with the batch keeps the
  \emph{ratio} the rules are usually stated in; dividing it by $\sqrt{2}$ is
  what keeps the noise in the update where it was. Neither is a law.}
\item Four micro-batches are accumulated. Is that the same as one batch four
  times as large? \teachesat{26--27}
  \answerto{Only if every micro-batch holds the same number of real tokens.
  Otherwise the accumulated loss is a mean of means and the large batch's is
  a pooled mean, and the two are different objectives.}
\item Two estimators of a gradient through a sampling step are both unbiased.
  What else is there to choose between them? \teachesat{33--34}
  \answerto{Their variance, and it can differ by orders of magnitude. Being
  right on average is not the same as being usable, and at a hundred
  dimensions one of the two is still visibly wrong after forty thousand
  samples.}
\end{quiz}

\section{A gradient you did not ask for}
\label{sec:P21-estimator}
\index{gradient!as an estimator}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:P20} took an optimiser apart and never once asked where the
gradient came from. Every walk in it used the gradient of the whole loss, and
no training run has ever done that.

What a run computes is the gradient of the loss over a \emph{batch}. Say what
that makes the number you step along.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
An \textbf{estimate} of the gradient you wanted, built from the examples that
happened to be drawn.
\end{ansblock}

That is the whole subject of this program, and it is worth saying plainly
before anything is derived: \textbf{the noise is not a defect in the method,
it is the method}. Nobody could afford the full gradient, and nobody wants it \dash{}
so what training does is take a noisy step, very many times, and the
properties of that noise decide almost everything people complain about.

\mermaidfig{p21-one-draw}{One draw, and the ensemble it came from.}{one draw,
one ensemble}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Start with what is promised. Take a population of $\val{p21.pop.n}$ numbers
standing for per-example gradients, and every possible batch of
$\val{p21.pop.b}$ of them \dash{} there are $\val{p21.pop.subsets}$.

Average the $\val{p21.pop.subsets}$ batch means. What do you get?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p21.pop.mean}$}
The population's own mean, and \textbf{exactly}: the script does the whole
thing over fractions, so there is no tolerance anywhere in it. That is what
\emph{unbiased} means, and it is the property every minibatch method rests on.

\begin{note}
Every batch was drawn, so this is a \textbf{proof} for that population rather
than evidence about it, which is Program~\ref{prog:P14}'s distinction. On a
real training set the same statement is a theorem about sampling rather than
an experiment, and the arithmetic is identical.
\end{note}

Now the half that matters more. Those $\val{p21.pop.subsets}$ means average to
$\val{p21.pop.mean}$ exactly. Guess how far the worst of them is from it.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
They run from $\val{p21.pop.lo}$ to $\val{p21.pop.hi}$.
\end{ansblock}

\transcript{p21-every-batch}

A span of $\val{p21.pop.span}$ around a quantity of $\val{p21.pop.mean}$, and
one of them has the wrong sign.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{The gradient is unbiased, so I can trust it.}} Unbiased is a
statement about the \emph{ensemble}: average over every batch that could have
been drawn and the answer is exact. It says nothing at all about the batch in
front of you, and here the batches straddle the answer with one of them
pointing the opposite way.

The two words that go together are \emph{unbiased} and \emph{variance}, and a
method that has the first without the second under control is a method whose
individual steps are noise.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{rigourbox}
\textbf{This program uses variance before the book has defined it}, and that
is deliberate rather than an oversight. What a random variable is, what a
variance is and how averages concentrate are Programs \ref{prog:P24} and
\ref{prog:P25}, two parts later; the curriculum notes record why the order is
this way round and why reordering the parts would break six other
dependencies.

What you need here is one sentence, and it is the one you would guess: the
\textbf{variance} of a quantity is the average of its squared distance from
its own mean, and its square root \dash{} the \textbf{spread} \dash{} is in
the same units as the quantity, which is why it is the one you can picture.
Program~\ref{prog:P25} returns to minibatch noise as a worked example once the
machinery exists.
\end{rigourbox}
\end{fr}

\section{How noisy, exactly}
\label{sec:P21-variance}
\index{gradient!noise scale}

\begin{fr}
The population here has $\val{p21.noise.pop}$ examples and a spread of
$\val{p21.noise.sd}$. Batches of $B$ are drawn from it,
$\val{p21.noise.trials}$ times at each size, and the spread of the batch means
is measured.

Before the numbers: a batch of $B$ averages $B$ independent draws. What should
happen to the spread as $B$ grows, and how fast?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
It should fall, and the \emph{variance} should fall like $1/B$.
\end{ansblock}

Which is what happens. Over $B$ from $\val{p21.noise.b.lo}$ to
$\val{p21.noise.b.hi}$ the measured variance tracks $\sigma^{2}/B$ to within
$\val{p21.noise.tol}$ per cent, and the script asserts that rather than any
single figure \dash{} a variance measured from four thousand trials is itself
a random quantity and would move with the seed.

The spread you actually see is the square root of that, so it falls from
$\val{p21.noise.sd1}$ at $B = \val{p21.noise.b.lo}$ to
$\val{p21.noise.sd256}$ at $B = \val{p21.noise.b.hi}$ \dash{} a factor of
$\val{p21.noise.drop}$ for $\val{p21.noise.b.hi}$ times the work.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
That factor is the one people misread, so take it slowly. Going from $B = 1$
to $B = \val{p21.noise.b16}$ is sixteen times the work per step.

By what factor does the \emph{spread} fall?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p21.noise.spread16}$}
Four, not sixteen, because the spread is the square root of the variance.

\textbf{That square root is where \enquote{diminishing returns on batch size}
comes from}, and it is exact rather than a rule of thumb. Doubling the batch
buys a factor of $\sqrt{2}$ in noise for a factor of $2$ in compute, at every
batch size, for ever. Nothing about the model enters the statement.

\mermaidfig{p21-noise-and-batch}{Two numbers describing one quantity.}{batch
size and the spread}

One more thing to fix before the next section, because it turns on it. That is
the spread of the \emph{estimate}. What is the spread of the \emph{step} you
take?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$\eta$ times as large, because the step is $\eta$ times the estimate.
\end{ansblock}

Two knobs, one quantity \dash{} and neither of them is meaningful without the
other, which is exactly the shape Program~\ref{prog:P20} found for the
momentum conventions.
\end{fr}

\section{Why the occasional enormous step}
\label{sec:P21-clipping}
\index{gradient!clipping}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:F06} took gradient clipping apart completely: clipping by
value truncates each component and turns the vector, clipping by norm rescales
it and does not, and the two are one word apart in every API. It then said the
question of \emph{why the enormous step happens at all} belonged here, because
it needs the noise model.

You have the noise model now. Say why.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Because a batch mean has a spread, so a batch will occasionally be far from
the population's gradient \dash{} and \enquote{occasionally} is exactly what a
spread describes.
\end{ansblock}

There is nothing wrong with the model, the data or the optimiser when it
happens. It is the tail of a distribution the method has by construction, and
its frequency is set by the spread and the batch size and by nothing else.

Which turns the threshold into a decision about a distribution rather than a
constant. Measured on the same batches: their typical size is
$\val{p21.clip.typical}$, and a threshold set there clips
$\val{p21.clip.one}$ per cent of steps \dash{} which is what \emph{typical}
means and is a check that the arithmetic is right.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Now the two ends, and the gap between them is the section's point.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
threshold at half the typical size & clips $\val{p21.clip.half}$ per cent of steps \\
threshold at four times it & clips $\val{p21.clip.four.n}$ of $\val{p21.clip.trials}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

$\val{p21.clip.half}$ per cent against $\val{p21.clip.four.n}$ in
$\val{p21.clip.trials}$. What is the algorithm doing in the first row?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Following the \emph{direction} of the gradient with a step size fixed by hand,
and discarding its magnitude entirely.
\end{ansblock}

Which is a different algorithm from the one anybody thinks they are running,
and nothing reports the difference. Program~\ref{prog:F06} says this in its own
box; what this program adds is that \textbf{the threshold is only meaningful
against a measured distribution of gradient sizes}, and that distribution is a
property of the batch size as well as of the data.

So the question to ask of a clipping threshold is not \enquote{is it a
reasonable number} but \enquote{what fraction of steps does it clip}. One is a
guess and the other is a measurement, and the second is two lines of logging.

Which of the two thresholds above is the safer one to be running?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
The one that almost never fires.
\end{ansblock}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{Clipping is a safety net, so a tighter one is safer.}} A
threshold below the typical gradient size is not a net, it is a redesign: it
converts the optimiser into one that uses the gradient's direction and ignores
its length, and it does so on nearly every step rather than occasionally.

The safe reading is the opposite of the intuitive one. A threshold that fires
rarely is doing the job it was added for; one that fires constantly has
quietly replaced the method.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\section{Two rules that hold different things fixed}
\label{sec:P21-scaling}
\index{optimiser!linear scaling rule}

\begin{fr}
Everybody who has changed a batch size has met the question, and it is usually
answered with a rule rather than with an argument. Take the argument first.

The update is $\eta$ times the estimate. Section 2 gives the estimate's
variance as $\sigma^{2}/B$. Write down the variance of the \emph{update}.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
\[ \Var(\eta\,\hat g) \;=\; \frac{\eta^{2}\sigma^{2}}{B} \]
\end{ansblock}

One line, and it settles the question mechanically. Multiply the batch by $k$
and ask what to do with $\eta$ so that this quantity does not move:
$\eta^{2}/B$ must be unchanged, so $\eta$ goes up by $\sqrt{k}$.

Checked at $k = \val{p21.scale.k}$: the square-root rule leaves the update's
variance at exactly $\val{p21.scale.sqrt}$ times what it was, and \textbf{the
linear rule multiplies it by $\val{p21.scale.linear}$}.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Which is awkward, because the rule that is actually recommended is the linear
one.

Before reading on \dash{} that is not a contradiction, and the reason is one
word. What does the linear rule hold fixed, if not the noise in the update?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
The distance travelled per \emph{example}, rather than per step. With $k$
times the batch you take $k$ times fewer steps, and $k$ times the step size
covers the same ground.
\end{ansblock}

So the two rules are answers to two different questions, and both are exactly
right about their own. The square-root rule holds the noise in each update
fixed; the linear rule holds the progress per epoch fixed and lets the noise
grow.

\begin{warning}
\textbf{The empirical claim is not settled and this book does not settle it.}
Which invariant matters more depends on the model, the stage of training and
the optimiser, the reported evidence for the linear rule comes with a warm-up
and a ceiling above which it stops working, and the disagreement is live.

What is stated above is arithmetic and holds regardless: those are the two
quantities, and a rule holds one of them fixed. Anyone who tells you which to
use is making an empirical claim, and it is fair to ask what it rests on.
\end{warning}

So there is a third choice, and it is the one people make without noticing.
Halve the batch and leave $\eta$ alone: which of the two quantities does that
hold fixed?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Neither.
\end{ansblock}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{I halved the batch size, so I will keep the learning rate.}}
That is a third rule, and it is the only one of the three nobody argues for:
it lets the noise in each update grow by $\sqrt{2}$ \emph{and} halves the
ground covered per example.

The useful habit is not to memorise a rule. It is to notice that $\eta$ and
$B$ appear together in one expression, so changing one without the other is a
change to a quantity you have not looked at.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\section{What a smoothed curve hides}
\label{sec:P21-smoothing}
\index{moving average!and the loss curve}

\begin{fr}
A raw loss curve made of noisy estimates is hard to read, so every training
dashboard smooths it, and the smoother is Program~\ref{prog:F04}'s exponential
moving average with a loss in it rather than a gradient.

At $\beta = \val{p21.ema.beta}$ its half-life is $\val{p21.ema.halflife}$
steps, which is F04's own committed figure and is gated against it. Now
suppose the loss genuinely drops, in one step, and stays down. What does the
smoothed curve show?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Half the drop after about a half-life, and the rest over the following few.
\end{ansblock}

Measured: the smoothed curve crosses the midpoint $\val{p21.ema.lag}$ steps
after the change, against a half-life of $\val{p21.ema.halflife}$. The lag
\textbf{is} the half-life \dash{} it is not a property of the change, and it
does not depend on how large the drop was.

So smoothing does not merely make a curve prettier. It moves every event later
by a known amount, and the amount is the number you chose when you set the
smoothing.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{The loss stopped falling around step 4000.}} With smoothing
at a half-life of $h$, it stopped falling around step $4000 - h$, and if you
are comparing two runs smoothed differently you are comparing two different
delays.

The same goes the other way: an intervention appears to take effect late, so
it gets credited to whatever came next. Read the raw curve when the question is
\emph{when}, and the smoothed one when the question is \emph{whether}.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\section{One denominator, two answers}
\label{sec:P21-accumulation}
\index{gradient!accumulation}

\begin{fr}
Gradient accumulation runs several micro-batches, adds their gradients, and
steps once \dash{} which is how a batch larger than the memory gets trained.
The claim it rests on is that this is identical to one large batch.

Program~\ref{prog:F04} has already made you meet the shape of the objection.
Three micro-batches hold $1000$, $10$ and $500$ real tokens and come back with
mean losses of $2$, $8$ and $3$. What is the mean loss over the
$\val{p21.acc.tokens}$ tokens?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p21.acc.pooled}$}
And what accumulation computes, if each micro-batch has already divided by its
own token count, is the mean of $2$, $8$ and $3$ \dash{}
$\val{p21.acc.accumulated}$, which is $\val{p21.acc.ratio}$ times larger.

\textbf{The difference is not in a report. It is in the gradient}, because the
loss that was averaged wrongly is the loss that was differentiated. The run
optimises an objective in which a ten-token micro-batch counts as much as a
thousand-token one.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Which raises the question that makes this worth a section rather than a
warning: why does anybody ship it?

Compute the same two numbers for three micro-batches holding $500$ tokens
each.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
They are equal \dash{} exactly, over fractions.
\end{ansblock}

\textbf{That is why it hides.} With equal token counts the two expressions are the
same expression, so a test written on padded fixtures passes, a small run with
uniform batches passes, and the defect appears only when the micro-batches are
ragged \dash{} which is to say, on real text, at scale, in the run that
matters.

It is Program~\ref{prog:P02}'s \enquote{safe for inputs you have not tried} for
the third time in this book, and Program~\ref{prog:P07}'s broadcasting identity
for the second: a defect that is invisible on the fixture and decisive on the
corpus.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{Accumulation over four micro-batches is a four-times batch.}}
Only when the four hold the same number of real tokens. Otherwise it is a mean
of means where a pooled mean was wanted, and it is silent: no error, no
warning, and a loss curve that looks entirely normal because it is the curve
of a different objective.

The fix is the one Program~\ref{prog:F04} states \dash{} carry the numerator
and the denominator separately and divide once, at the end \dash{} and the
test is the one this section suggests: run a fixture with deliberately ragged
micro-batches and check the accumulated gradient against the large-batch one.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\section{Two ways through a coin flip}
\label{sec:P21-estimators}
\index{gradient!score-function estimator}
\index{gradient!reparameterisation}

\begin{fr}
Everything so far has been about noise you did not want. This section is about
randomness that is part of the model: a sampling step \emph{inside} the thing
being differentiated.

That is not exotic. A policy samples an action, a variational model samples a
latent, a decoder samples a token. In each case the quantity to be maximised
is an average over what might be drawn, and its gradient is wanted.

Say what makes that hard.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
The sampling step has no derivative. You cannot differentiate \enquote{a
draw}.
\end{ansblock}

There are two ways round it and they are completely different in character.
This section derives neither \dash{} both are a line of algebra that
Programs \ref{prog:P24} and \ref{prog:P26} are better placed to give \dash{}
and instead does the thing this book can do: measures what separates them.

\mermaidfig{p21-two-estimators}{Two routes, and what the choice costs.}{two
routes through a draw}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\textbf{The score-function estimator}, which is what a policy gradient is:
multiply the value you got by the derivative of the log-probability of getting
it. It needs the \emph{value} of the function and nothing else \dash{} the
function may be a black box, a simulator, a human rating.

\textbf{The reparameterisation trick}, which is what a variational
auto-encoder uses: write the draw as a fixed random number pushed through a
differentiable function of the parameters, and differentiate straight through
it. It needs a differentiable path from the parameter to the value.

Both are unbiased. Say what is left to choose between them.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Whether the path exists at all \dash{} and, when it does, the
\textbf{variance}.
\end{ansblock}

Measured on one problem where both apply, at $\val{p21.grad.samples}$ samples
and a true gradient of $\val{p21.grad.true}$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
dimension & score function & reparameterised \\
\midrule
$\val{p21.grad.d.lo}$ & $\val{p21.grad.score.lo}$ & $\val{p21.grad.repar}$ \\
$\val{p21.grad.d.hi}$ & $\val{p21.grad.score.hi}$ & $\val{p21.grad.repar}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Read the right-hand column first. What has it done?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Nothing. The reparameterised estimator's variance is the same at
$\val{p21.grad.d.hi}$ dimensions as at $\val{p21.grad.d.lo}$.
\end{ansblock}

Exactly the same, and not by luck: the estimator for one component is a
function of that component alone, so the other $99$ dimensions do not enter
it. The script asserts it at every dimension.

The score-function estimator's variance meanwhile goes from
$\val{p21.grad.score.lo}$ to $\val{p21.grad.score.hi}$, a factor of
$\val{p21.grad.ratio.hi}$ at $\val{p21.grad.d.hi}$ dimensions \dash{} because
it multiplies by the value of the \emph{whole} function, and the whole
function grows with the dimension.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Now the consequence, which is the sentence the section exists for. Both
estimators are unbiased. After $\val{p21.grad.samples}$ samples at
$\val{p21.grad.d.hi}$ dimensions, the reparameterised estimate is
$\val{p21.grad.off.repar}$ per cent from the true value.

How far off is the score-function estimate?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p21.grad.off.score}$ per cent}
$\val{p21.grad.score.est}$ against a true $\val{p21.grad.true}$, after forty
thousand samples, from an estimator that is exactly right on average.

\textbf{Unbiased is not the same as usable.} That is the whole of the fork:
a variational model has a differentiable path and takes it, and a
policy-gradient method does not have one and pays for it \dash{} which is why
the literature on policy gradients is largely a literature about variance
reduction, and the literature on variational auto-encoders is not.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{Both estimators are unbiased, so they are interchangeable.}}
They estimate the same quantity and they are not interchangeable, because an
estimator is used a finite number of times. The relevant comparison is not
whether the average is right but how far a realistic number of samples leaves
you from it, and that is set by the variance.

This is the same distinction as section 1's, at the other end of the program:
there, unbiased said nothing about one batch; here, it says nothing about one
run.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Which closes the program, and it is worth saying what has held throughout.

Every complaint this program addressed \dash{} a noisy curve, an enormous
step, a learning rate that did not transfer, a smoothed curve that lied about
when, an accumulated batch that was not one, an estimator that would not
converge \dash{} was a statement about a \emph{variance}, and none of them was
a statement about the model.

That is worth carrying, because the instinct in each case is to look at the
model. The habit this program is for is to ask first what quantity is being
estimated, from how many samples, and with what spread \dash{} and the answer
is usually two lines of arithmetic that nobody had written down.
\end{fr}

\begin{summarybox}
\sumitem{1--2}{\result{A minibatch gradient is an \textbf{estimate} of the gradient
  you wanted. The noise is the method rather than a defect in it}.}
\sumitem{3--4}{\result{\emph{Unbiased} means the average over every batch that could
  have been drawn is exact \dash{} proved here over all
  $\val{p21.pop.subsets}$ of them, over fractions, which is a proof for this
  population rather than evidence about it}.}
\sumitem{5--6}{\result{It says nothing about one draw: the batch means run from
  $\val{p21.pop.lo}$ to $\val{p21.pop.hi}$ around $\val{p21.pop.mean}$, and
  one of them has the wrong sign}.}
\sumitem{7}{The forward prerequisite is declared: variance itself is
  Program~\ref{prog:P24}'s and concentration is Program~\ref{prog:P25}'s, two
  parts later, and P25 returns to minibatch noise once the machinery exists.}
\sumitem{8--9}{\result{The variance of a batch mean falls like $1/B$, measured to
  within $\val{p21.noise.tol}$ per cent over $B$ from
  $\val{p21.noise.b.lo}$ to $\val{p21.noise.b.hi}$}.}
\sumitem{10--11}{\result{\textbf{The spread falls like $1/\sqrt{B}$}, so
  $\val{p21.noise.b16}$ times the batch buys $\val{p21.noise.spread16}$ times
  less noise. That square root is the whole of \enquote{diminishing returns}
  and it is exact rather than a rule of thumb}.}
\sumitem{12}{\result{The step's spread is $\eta$ times the estimate's, so $\eta$ and
  $B$ are two numbers describing one quantity}.}
\sumitem{13--14}{\result{An enormous step happens because a batch mean has a spread.
  Nothing is wrong with the model, the data or the optimiser when it does}.}
\sumitem{15--17}{\result{A clipping threshold is a decision about a distribution: at
  half the typical size it clips $\val{p21.clip.half}$ per cent of steps and
  at four times it, $\val{p21.clip.four.n}$ of $\val{p21.clip.trials}$.
  \textbf{The first is a different algorithm}, following the direction and
  discarding the magnitude, and the safe threshold is the one that almost
  never fires}.}
\sumitem{18--19}{\result{The update's variance is $\eta^{2}\sigma^{2}/B$, which
  settles the scaling question mechanically}.}
\sumitem{20--21}{\result{Scaling $\eta$ by $\sqrt{k}$ holds that variance
  \textbf{exactly} fixed; scaling it by $k$ multiplies it by
  $\val{p21.scale.linear}$ and holds the ground covered per example fixed
  instead. Two rules, two invariants, and the empirical question is not
  settled here}.}
\sumitem{22}{\result{Keeping $\eta$ while changing $B$ holds neither fixed, which is
  why it is the one choice nobody argues for}.}
\sumitem{23--24}{\result{A smoothed loss curve lags by its half-life:
  $\val{p21.ema.lag}$ steps measured against $\val{p21.ema.halflife}$
  committed by Program~\ref{prog:F04}. The lag does not depend on the size of
  the change}.}
\sumitem{25}{\result{Read the raw curve when the question is \emph{when} and the
  smoothed one when it is \emph{whether}}.}
\sumitem{26--27}{\result{Accumulating micro-batches with different token counts gives
  $\val{p21.acc.accumulated}$ where the large batch gives
  $\val{p21.acc.pooled}$ \dash{} and it is the \textbf{gradient} rather than a
  report, because the loss that was averaged wrongly is the loss that was
  differentiated}.}
\sumitem{28--30}{\result{With equal token counts the two are identical over fractions,
  which is exactly why it ships: it is invisible on a padded fixture and
  decisive on real text}.}
\sumitem{31--33}{\result{A sampling step has no derivative, and there are two ways
  round it: the score function, which needs only the value, and
  reparameterisation, which needs a differentiable path}.}
\sumitem{34--35}{\result{Both are unbiased. The reparameterised estimator's variance
  is $\val{p21.grad.repar}$ at \emph{every} dimension; the score function's
  goes to $\val{p21.grad.score.hi}$, a factor of $\val{p21.grad.ratio.hi}$}.}
\sumitem{36--38}{\result{After $\val{p21.grad.samples}$ samples the score-function
  estimate is still $\val{p21.grad.off.score}$ per cent out.
  \textbf{Unbiased is not the same as usable}, and that is the fork between a
  variational model and a policy gradient}.}
\sumitem{39}{Every complaint in this program was a statement about a
  \textbf{variance} and none was a statement about the model.}

\begin{testexercises}
\item A batch of $32$ gives gradient estimates with a spread of $\num{0.40}$.
  What batch size would halve that spread, and what does it cost?
  \answerto{$128$. The spread falls like $1/\sqrt{B}$, so halving it needs
  four times the batch \dash{} and four times the compute per step.}
\item A run at batch $256$ uses $\eta = \num{3e-4}$. You move to batch $1024$.
  What is $\eta$ under the linear rule, and under the square-root rule?
  \answerto{$k = 4$, so linear gives $\num{1.2e-3}$ and square-root gives
  $\num{6e-4}$. The first holds the ground covered per example fixed and
  multiplies the noise in each update by $4$; the second holds that noise
  exactly fixed.}
\item A dashboard smooths the loss with $\beta = \num{0.98}$. A change is made
  at step $1000$. Roughly when does the smoothed curve show half of its
  effect?
  \answerto{About step $1034$. The half-life is
  $\ln(\num{0.5})/\ln(\num{0.98}) \approx 34$ steps, and the lag is the
  half-life whatever the size of the change.}
\item Two micro-batches hold $600$ and $200$ real tokens with mean losses of
  $\num{1.5}$ and $\num{3.5}$. What does accumulation report, and what should
  it be?
  \answerto{Accumulation reports the mean of means, $\num{2.5}$. The pooled
  mean is $(600 \times \num{1.5} + 200 \times \num{3.5})/800 = \num{2.0}$. The
  gradient is wrong by the same ratio, not just the number on the screen.}
\item You are training a model whose output is fed to a non-differentiable
  scorer. Which of the two estimators is available, and what should you expect?
  \answerto{Only the score-function estimator, because there is no
  differentiable path through the scorer. Expect a high-variance gradient, and
  expect to spend effort on variance reduction \dash{} which is what baselines
  and advantage estimates are for.}
\end{testexercises}

\begin{furtherproblems}
\item Show that if $\hat g$ is the mean of $B$ independent draws with variance
  $\sigma^{2}$, then $\Var(\hat g) = \sigma^{2}/B$, and say which word in the
  statement is doing the work.
  \answerto{The variance of a sum of independent quantities is the sum of
  their variances, so the sum of $B$ draws has variance $B\sigma^{2}$;
  dividing by $B$ divides the variance by $B^{2}$, leaving $\sigma^{2}/B$.
  \emph{Independent} is the word doing the work: draws that are correlated do
  not obey it, which is why sampling a batch from a sorted or clustered
  dataset breaks the noise model rather than merely being untidy.}
\item Write down what each of the two scaling rules holds fixed, as an
  expression, and say what the third common choice \dash{} keeping $\eta$
  \dash{} holds fixed.
  \answerto{Square-root scaling holds $\eta^{2}/B$ fixed, which is the
  variance of one update. Linear scaling holds the ground covered per
  example fixed, since $k$ times the batch means $k$ times fewer steps. Keeping $\eta$ holds neither fixed: the update's variance
  changes by $1/k$ and the ground covered per example by $1/k$ as well, which
  is why it is a choice nobody argues for rather than a third rule.}
\item A colleague reports that clipping \enquote{does nothing} because
  removing it changes the loss curve very little. What measurement would
  settle whether that is true, and what would each answer mean?
  \answerto{Log the fraction of steps that clip. If it is near zero then
  clipping genuinely does nothing on this run and the threshold is doing its
  job as a backstop. If it is large then clipping is active on most steps, the
  optimiser is following the gradient's direction with a hand-fixed length,
  and \enquote{removing it changes little} means the magnitudes were carrying
  little information \dash{} which is a much more interesting finding and a
  different one.}
\item The reparameterised estimator's variance did not change between one and
  a hundred dimensions. Explain why, and say what would break it.
  \answerto{The estimator for one component of the parameter is a function of
  that component's own draw and nothing else, so the other dimensions never
  enter. It would break if the function being averaged coupled the components
  in a way that made the derivative with respect to one depend on all of them
  \dash{} which is common, and is why the flatness here is a property of this
  example rather than a general law. What is general is the contrast: the
  score-function estimator multiplies by the value of the whole function, so
  it picks up the dimension whatever the function is.}
\item Both estimators are unbiased, and one of them is unusable at a hundred
  dimensions. Reconcile those two statements in one sentence, and name the
  quantity that does the reconciling.
  \answerto{Unbiasedness is a statement about the limit of infinitely many
  samples and an estimator is used finitely many times, so what decides
  usability is the \emph{variance} \dash{} which sets how far a realistic
  sample leaves you from the value the limit would give.}
\end{furtherproblems}
