\program{Exponential, logistic and hyperbolic functions}
\label{prog:F07}
\index{exponential!where e comes from}
\index{function!logistic}

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{1--6}{say what number $e$ is and where it comes from, and say what
  \enquote{exponential growth} claims about a quantity and what it does not}
\outcome{7--12}{read a growth or decay rate off $e^{kt}$, work out a half-life
  from it, and convert between a rate and a per-step multiplier}
\outcome{13--18}{derive the logistic curve from the odds, and read its limits,
  its symmetry and the width of its steep part off the curve itself}
\outcome{19--22}{write $\tanh$ in terms of the logistic, and say which of the
  two is the better behaved and in what respect}
\outcome{23--26}{say what a unit in saturation does to a change in its input,
  and give the one thing $\relu$ changes about that}
\outcome{27--31}{write $\softmax$ as exponentials over their own sum, say what
  it becomes on two scores, and say why a constant may be subtracted from
  every score first}
\end{outcomes}

\begin{quiz}
\item What is $e$, to three decimal places, and where does it come from?
  \teachesat{2--3}
  \answerto{$\num{2.718}$. It is what $\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^{n}$
  settles at as $n$ grows \dash{} interest compounded ever more often on a
  balance that never runs away.}
\item Which is larger at $x = 5$: $e^{x}$ or $x^{5}$? \teachesat{4--5}
  \answerto{$x^{5}$, and by a long way: $3125$ against $\val{f07.poly.at5.exp}$.
  The exponential does not overtake until $x = \val{f07.poly.crossover}$.}
\item A quantity decays as $e^{-kt}$ with $k = \num{0.05}$. How long until it
  halves? \teachesat{7--8}
  \answerto{$\val{f07.decay.halflife}$, from $\frac{\ln 2}{k}$.}
\item And how many half-lives until it reaches a tenth of where it started?
  \teachesat{9--10}
  \answerto{$\val{f07.decay.tenth.halflives}$ of them, not ten and not five.}
\item Show that $\frac{e^{x}}{1 + e^{x}}$ and $\frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}$ are the
  same function. \teachesat{13--14}
  \answerto{Divide the numerator and the denominator of the first by $e^{x}$.}
\item What does $\sigma(x)$ approach as $x$ grows, and as $x$ falls?
  \teachesat{14--15}
  \answerto{$1$ and $0$, and it reaches neither, because $e^{-x}$ is never
  zero and never infinite.}
\item Write $\tanh$ in terms of $e^{x}$ and $e^{-x}$. \teachesatone{19}
  \answerto{$\tanh x = \frac{e^{x} - e^{-x}}{e^{x} + e^{-x}}$.}
\item Evaluate $\tanh(1)$ and $2\sigma(2) - 1$. What do you notice?
  \teachesat{19--20}
  \answerto{Both are $\val{f07.tanh.1}$. $\tanh$ is the logistic squashed
  horizontally by two, doubled in height and slid down by one \dash{} all
  three moves, and the squash is the one it is easy to drop.}
\item Why was $\tanh$ preferred to the logistic in hidden layers?
  \teachesat{20--21}
  \answerto{Because it is centred on zero and the logistic is not. That is a
  claim about where its outputs sit, not about how gently it bends.}
\item Which saturates harder, the logistic or $\tanh$? \teachesat{21--22}
  \answerto{$\tanh$. Its steepness falls from $\val{f07.tanh.slope0}$ to
  $\val{f07.tanh.slope2}$ over two units; the logistic's falls from
  $\val{f07.slope.0}$ to $\val{f07.slope.2}$.}
\item Roughly how much less steep is the logistic six units from its centre
  than at it? \teachesat{17--18}
  \answerto{About $\val{f07.slope.ratio6}$ times.}
\item What does $\relu$ change about saturation? \teachesat{24--25}
  \answerto{It removes it on one side: above zero the graph is a straight line
  of constant steepness and never flattens.}
\item Write $\softmax$ over $n$ scores. \teachesatone{27}
  \answerto{$\softmax(z)_j = \frac{e^{z_j}}{\sum_k e^{z_k}}$ \dash{}
  exponentials divided by their own sum.}
\item Apply $\softmax$ to just two scores, $a$ and $b$. What do you get?
  \teachesat{28--29}
  \answerto{$\sigma(a - b)$ for the first and $\sigma(b - a)$ for the second.
  On two outcomes softmax is the logistic.}
\item Why may you subtract the largest score from every score before
  exponentiating? \teachesat{29--30}
  \answerto{Because a constant added to every score cancels between the
  numerator and the denominator, so the result does not change \dash{} and the
  exponentials become small enough to compute.}
\end{quiz}

\section{Where the number \texorpdfstring{$e$}{e} comes from}
\label{sec:F07-e}
\index{exponential!compound interest}

\begin{fr}
Put one pound in an account that pays one hundred per cent interest over a
year. Paid once, at the end, you finish with $2$.

Now let the interest be paid twice, half of it each time. After six months you
have $\num{1.5}$, and the second payment is half of \emph{that}, so you finish
with $\num{1.5} \times \num{1.5} = \num{2.25}$. Paying it more often is worth
more, because the interest starts earning interest.

The pattern is
\[ \left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^{n} \]
for interest paid $n$ times.

What do you get if it is paid monthly \dash{} that is, at $n = 12$? An
approximate answer is enough; you are being asked whether it is nearer $2$,
nearer $3$, or past $3$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{f07.compound.12}$}
Nearer three than two, and not past three. Keep going:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rc}
\toprule
$n$ & $\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^{n}$ \\
\midrule
$1$ & \val{f07.compound.1} \\
$2$ & \val{f07.compound.2} \\
$12$ & \val{f07.compound.12} \\
$365$ & \val{f07.compound.365} \\
$1\,000\,000$ & \val{f07.compound.1000000} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Paying the interest a million times a year is worth almost exactly what paying
it every day is worth. The column is climbing and it is slowing down.

Does it climb forever?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{No}
It settles. Every row is larger than the one above it and every row is below
one particular number, and a sequence that climbs but is held under a ceiling
has nowhere to go but towards it. That number is
\[ e = \val{f07.e}\ldots \]
and it is a number in exactly the way $\pi$ is a number: not a process, not an
approximation of something else, and not rational.

So $e^{x}$ means what $2^{x}$ and $10^{x}$ mean, with a less convenient base.
Program~\ref{prog:F01}'s index laws all hold unchanged, and in particular
$e^{0} = 1$, which is the empty product Program~\ref{prog:F04} argued for.
The function is also written $\exp(x)$, and that spelling is what you will
meet in code.

Which of $2^{x}$, $e^{x}$ and $3^{x}$ grows fastest for large $x$?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$3^{x}$}
The larger the base, the faster the growth, and $e$ sits between $2$ and $3$.
So $e$ is not the base that grows fastest, and nothing about the size of $e$
is what makes it special.

\begin{rigourbox}
What makes $e$ special is a statement about its \emph{rate of change}: the
steepness of $e^{x}$ at any point equals the height of $e^{x}$ at that point,
and $e$ is the only base for which that is true.

This program states that and does not prove it, because the proof needs a
definition of steepness that Program~\ref{prog:F11} supplies and
Program~\ref{prog:F12} uses. What you can do here is take the consequence: a
quantity growing exponentially is one whose growth at every moment is
proportional to how much of it there already is.
\end{rigourbox}

Now one to answer fast. No working, and no checking.

\textbf{At $x = 5$, which is larger: $e^{x}$ or $x^{5}$?}
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$x^{5}$, and it is not close}
$5^{5} = 3125$ against $e^{5} = \val{f07.poly.at5.exp}$. The polynomial is
$\val{f07.poly.at5.ratio}$ times larger.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{Exponential} is a claim about the mechanism, not about the
speed.} It says the growth at each moment is proportional to the current size.
It does not say the quantity is large, or that it is larger than something
else, or that it is growing quickly now.

$e^{x}$ does not overtake $x^{5}$ until $x = \val{f07.poly.crossover}$, and up
to that point the polynomial leads by as much as a factor of twenty. Somebody
describing a quantity as growing exponentially has told you the shape of the
curve and nothing at all about where on it they are standing.

What is true, and what makes the word worth using, is the other end: once the
exponential passes, it never comes back. No polynomial of any degree stays
ahead of $e^{x}$ forever, and the crossover merely moves further out as the
degree rises.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The mechanism is worth one more frame, because it is what the word actually
means and it is checkable without any calculus.

Take $x^{5}$ at $x = 2$ and at $x = 4$: it goes from $32$ to $1024$, a factor
of $32$. Take it from $4$ to $8$: $1024$ to $32768$, a factor of $32$ again.
\emph{Doubling the input} multiplies a power by a fixed amount.

Now $e^{x}$. From $x = 2$ to $x = 3$ it multiplies by $e$; from $3$ to $4$, by
$e$ again. \emph{Adding a fixed amount to the input} multiplies an exponential
by a fixed amount.

That is the whole difference, and it is why the two curves must eventually
cross whatever the power: the exponential gains a constant factor per step
along the axis, and the polynomial's factor per step shrinks as you go out.
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{f07-e-arrives}{Interest paid more and more often on the same
balance. The total climbs, slows, and stops at a number.}{where e comes from}

\section{Growth, decay and the half-life}
\label{sec:F07-decay}
\index{exponential!decay}

\begin{fr}
Put a constant in the exponent and you can set the pace:
\[ y = y_0\,e^{kt} \]
$y_0$ is where the quantity starts, because $e^{0} = 1$. A positive $k$ grows
and a negative $k$ decays, and the size of $k$ says how fast.

You have met the decaying case without necessarily naming it. A learning rate
that is stepped down smoothly through training is
\[ \eta(t) = \eta_0\,e^{-kt} \]
and with $k = \num{0.05}$ that is a rate falling by about five per cent per
step at the start.

After how many steps is the learning rate half of what it began at?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{f07.decay.halflife}$ steps}
Set $e^{-kt} = \frac{1}{2}$, take logarithms of both sides as
Program~\ref{prog:F03} showed, and get $t = \frac{\ln 2}{k}$. It is
Program~\ref{prog:F06}'s undoing with a logarithm in place of a division.

Notice what is \emph{not} in that formula: $\eta_0$. The half-life does not
depend on where the quantity started, so a rate falling from $\num{0.1}$ and a
rate falling from $\num{0.001}$ take exactly the same number of steps to halve.
\code{code/f07\_exponential.py} checks it from three different starting values.

How many steps until the rate is a tenth of where it began?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{f07.decay.tenth}$ steps}
From $t = \frac{\ln 10}{k}$, by the same route.

Now put the two together. Fill in the gap: reaching a tenth takes
\blank\ half-lives.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$\val{f07.decay.tenth.halflives}$.
\end{ansblock}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Not ten, and not five.} Both wrong answers come from carrying a linear
intuition into a ratio: ten times smaller feels like it should be ten halvings,
or perhaps five because halving twice is quartering.

What it actually is, is $\frac{\ln 10}{\ln 2} = \logb{2} 10$, and the reason is
Program~\ref{prog:F03}'s: each half-life divides by $2$, so $m$ of them divide
by $2^{m}$, and you want $2^{m} = 10$.

This is Program~\ref{prog:F01}'s $2^{10} \approx 10^{3}$ read from the other
end \dash{} ten halvings take you to about a thousandth, not a tenth \dash{}
and it is worth having both directions of that fact, because one of them is
always the one you need.
\end{trapbox}

At $t = 100$ steps, what fraction of the original rate is left?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{f07.decay.at100}$}
About two-thirds of one per cent, from $e^{-5}$.

One conversion is worth having, because the same schedule is written both
ways and the two look nothing alike. A rate multiplied by $\gamma$ every step
is
\[ \eta_t = \eta_0\,\gamma^{t} = \eta_0\,e^{t\ln\gamma} \]
using Program~\ref{prog:F03}'s $\gamma = e^{\ln\gamma}$. So a per-step
multiplier $\gamma$ is the same thing as a rate $k = -\ln\gamma$.

At $\gamma = \num{0.99}$, what is $k$?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{About $\num{0.01}$}
Because $-\ln(\num{0.99}) = \num{0.01005}$, and for a $\gamma$ near $1$ the
rate is very nearly $1 - \gamma$. That approximation is good enough to reason
with and it is the reason the two forms are so easy to confuse: at
$\gamma = \num{0.99}$ they agree to three decimal places.

\begin{aibox}
Two schedules described as \enquote{multiply by $\num{0.99}$ each step} and
\enquote{decay at a rate of $\num{0.01}$} are the same schedule, and the
conversion is one logarithm. Two described as \enquote{multiply by
$\num{0.5}$ each step} and \enquote{decay at a rate of $\num{0.5}$} are
\emph{not}: multiplying by $\num{0.5}$ is a rate of
$-\ln(\num{0.5}) = \num{0.693}$, so the \emph{first} decays about
$\num{1.4}$ times as fast as the second. Halving takes one step under the
first and $\num{1.39}$ under the second.

The approximation $k \approx 1 - \gamma$ that makes the first pair agree is
only good near $\gamma = 1$, which is where schedules usually live and which
is exactly why the disagreement goes unnoticed until somebody sets an
aggressive one.
\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\section{Squashing it into a probability}
\label{sec:F07-logistic}
\index{function!logistic}
\index{odds!and the logistic}

\begin{fr}
Programs~\ref{prog:F05} and~\ref{prog:F06} both used the logistic function and
neither said where its shape comes from. It comes from odds.

Suppose the odds in favour of something are $e^{x}$ to one \dash{} which is
a way of letting $x$ run over every real number while the odds stay
positive, since $e^{x}$ is never zero and never negative. Odds of $a$ to $1$
mean a probability of $\frac{a}{a+1}$, so
\[ p = \frac{e^{x}}{1 + e^{x}} \]

That is the logistic, and it is not obviously the form you have been using.
Show that it is the same as $\frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Divide the numerator and the denominator by $e^{x}$:
$\frac{e^{x}}{1 + e^{x}} = \frac{1}{e^{-x} + 1}$.
\end{ansblock}

Both forms are in use and it is worth recognising each, because a paper that
writes one and code that writes the other are doing the same thing.

The derivation also explains the shape without any curve-sketching. As $x$
runs from very negative to very positive, the odds run from nearly zero to
enormous, and a probability built from odds is squeezed against $0$ at one end
and against $1$ at the other. The flatness is not a feature somebody added; it
is what a bounded quantity has to do.

What does $\sigma(x)$ approach as $x$ grows without bound, and as $x$ falls
without bound?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$1$ and $0$, and it reaches neither.
\end{ansblock}

Because $e^{-x}$ falls towards zero but is never zero, so $\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}$
climbs towards $1$ but is never $1$; and $e^{-x}$ grows without bound but is
never infinite, so the fraction falls towards $0$ without arriving.

\begin{note}
\emph{Approaches but never reaches} is not pedantry here, it is a fact you
will meet as a number. A logistic output is never exactly $1$, so
$\ln(1 - p)$ is always defined in exact arithmetic \dash{} and in floating
point it is not, because $\sigma$ rounds to exactly $\num{1.0}$ from
$x = \val{f07.sig.saturates}$ upwards. Program~\ref{prog:P02} owns the consequence; what belongs
here is that the mathematics and the arithmetic disagree, and that the
disagreement has a location.
\end{note}

Program~\ref{prog:F05} noticed from a table that $\sigma(-2)$ and $\sigma(2)$
add to $1$. Using the odds form, say why.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Because odds of $e^{x}$ to $1$ \emph{for} are odds of $1$ to $e^{x}$
\emph{against}, which is $e^{-x}$ to $1$ the other way round.
\end{ansblock}

Negating $x$ swaps which outcome you are quoting the odds on, and the two
probabilities of a two-outcome question add to $1$. So $\sigma(-x) = 1 -
\sigma(x)$ is not an algebraic coincidence, it is that swap written down.

The inverse follows from the same reading, and Program~\ref{prog:F06} already
derived it: undoing $p = \frac{e^{x}}{1+e^{x}}$ gives
\[ x = \ln\!\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right) \]
\dash{} the logarithm of the odds, which is why it is called the log-odds as
often as the logit.

So the three objects are one object seen three ways: $x$ is a log-odds,
$e^{x}$ is odds, and $\sigma(x)$ is a probability.

Now the shape. Is the curve flat everywhere outside, say, $x = \pm 2$?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{No}
It is flat\emph{ter}, and how much flatter is worth a number rather than an
adjective. Measure the steepness \dash{} how much the output rises for a small
rise in the input \dash{} at a few places:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rc}
\toprule
$x$ & steepness of $\sigma$ \\
\midrule
$0$ & \val{f07.slope.0} \\
$1$ & \val{f07.slope.1} \\
$2$ & \val{f07.slope.2} \\
$4$ & \val{f07.slope.4} \\
$6$ & \val{f07.slope.6} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

At $x = 2$ the curve is still doing $\val{f07.slope.2}$, which is a little
under half of its steepest. It is at $4$ and beyond that it stops meaningfully
moving.

The largest value in that column is $\val{f07.slope.0}$, at the centre, and it
is exactly a quarter. What is the steepness at $x = 6$ as a fraction of the
steepness at $0$?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
About one part in $\val{f07.slope.ratio6}$.
\end{ansblock}

Six units out from the middle, the same change in the input buys about a
hundredth as much change in the output. That is the number section 5 is built
on, and it is the whole of what the word \emph{saturation} names.

Two things about that quarter are worth keeping. It is the largest the
logistic ever gets, so a change of one in the input never changes the output
by more than about a quarter \dash{} the curve is gentle even where it is
steepest. And it sits at $x = 0$, which is where $\sigma$ reads a half, so
\textbf{a unit is most responsive exactly when it is least certain.}
\end{fr}

\section{\texorpdfstring{$\tanh$}{tanh} is the same curve}
\label{sec:F07-tanh}
\index{function!hyperbolic tangent}

\begin{fr}
The hyperbolic tangent is
\[ \tanh x = \frac{e^{x} - e^{-x}}{e^{x} + e^{-x}} \]
and it is the other activation function of the era before $\relu$.

Its values, for comparison with the logistic's:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rc}
\toprule
$x$ & $\tanh x$ \\
\midrule
$-2$ & \val{f07.tanh.m2} \\
$-1$ & \val{f07.tanh.m1} \\
$0$ & \val{f07.tanh.0} \\
$1$ & \val{f07.tanh.1} \\
$2$ & \val{f07.tanh.2} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

It is zero at zero, it is odd \dash{} $\tanh(-x) = -\tanh(x)$, which
Program~\ref{prog:F05} named \dash{} and it runs between $-1$ and $1$.

Now compare $\tanh(1) = \val{f07.tanh.1}$ with the number you get from
$2\sigma(2) - 1$, using $\sigma(2) = \val{f05.sig.2}$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$2 \times \val{f05.sig.2} - 1 = \val{f07.tanh.1}$. They are the same number.
\end{ansblock}

And not by coincidence:
\[ \tanh x = 2\,\sigma(2x) - 1 \]
which \code{code/f07\_exponential.py} checks over four hundred points and
cross-checks against a second implementation over four thousand, with a worst
disagreement of $\val{f07.tanh.identity.err}$.

Read the right-hand side as Program~\ref{prog:F05} taught. The $2x$ inside the
bracket squashes the curve horizontally by two; the $2$ outside stretches it
to twice its height; the $-1$ slides it down by one. Three of the four moves,
and out comes a curve running from $-1$ to $1$ instead of from $0$ to $1$.

\textbf{So $\tanh$ is not a second function to learn.} It is the logistic
rescaled, and every property of one is a property of the other with the
numbers moved.

Given that, why do both exist?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Because of where their outputs sit: $\tanh$ is centred on zero and the
logistic is centred on a half.
\end{ansblock}

A layer whose outputs are all positive sends a systematically one-signed
signal into the next layer, and a layer whose outputs straddle zero does not.
That was the argument for $\tanh$ over the logistic in hidden layers, and it is
an argument about the \emph{location} of the outputs and nothing else.

Answer this one quickly, without working anything out:
\textbf{does $\tanh$ also saturate more gently than the logistic?}
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{No. It saturates harder}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
& at $x = 0$ & at $x = 2$ & ratio \\
\midrule
logistic & \val{f07.slope.0} & \val{f07.slope.2}
  & about $\val{f07.slope.ratio2}$ \\
$\tanh$ & \val{f07.tanh.slope0} & \val{f07.tanh.slope2}
  & about $\val{f07.tanh.ratio2}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{$\tanh$ is steeper in the middle and flatter at the ends, which is
what \enquote{saturates harder} means.} Its steepness starts at $1$ rather
than at a quarter, and two units out it has lost a factor of about
$\val{f07.tanh.ratio2}$ where the logistic has lost about
$\val{f07.slope.ratio2}$.

That follows from the identity rather than being a separate fact. Squashing
the curve horizontally by two makes it twice as steep; stretching it
vertically by two makes it twice as steep again; so $\tanh$ is four times the
logistic's steepness at the centre \dash{} four quarters, which is $1$.

But only the \emph{squash} moves anything along the axis: a vertical stretch
scales every steepness and shifts no point. So the flat region comes in by two
rather than by four, and the evidence is two numbers that agree \dash{}
$\tanh$ loses a factor of about $\val{f07.tanh.ratio2}$ over two units, and
the logistic loses about $\val{f07.slope.ratio4}$ over \emph{four}. Those are
different curves measured over different distances, and they come to the same
figure because the squash is by two.

The case for $\tanh$ was always about being centred on zero. Nobody claimed it
bends more gently, and it does not.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\section{Saturation, and what it costs}
\label{sec:F07-saturation}
\index{saturation!of an activation}

\begin{fr}
A unit is \textbf{saturated} when its input has taken it out to the flat part
of its curve. The word describes a position, not a fault: there is nothing
wrong with a unit reading $\num{0.999}$, and if the right answer is close to
one then that is where it should be.

What is true of a saturated unit is narrower and it is the number of the
previous section. Six units out, the logistic answers a change in its input
with about one part in $\val{f07.slope.ratio6}$ of the change in its output it
would give at the centre. Four units out it is about one part in
$\val{f07.slope.ratio4}$.

So the unit is not broken and it is not stuck. It is \emph{quiet}: it reports
almost the same thing whatever you do to it, within reason.

What would that do to a process that learns by watching how the output changes
when the input is nudged?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
It would have almost nothing to watch.
\end{ansblock}

The nudge produces a hundredth of the response it would produce in the middle,
so whatever signal drives the change arrives a hundred times smaller.

\begin{rigourbox}
That is as far as this program goes, and the restraint is deliberate.

\textbf{This is not yet the vanishing-gradient problem.} A factor of a
hundredth in one layer is survivable; what is not survivable is the same
factor arriving once per layer and multiplying, and \emph{multiplying} is the
part that needs the chain rule. Program~\ref{prog:F12} supplies it, and only
there does the sentence \enquote{the gradient vanishes} carry the meaning
people give it.

What you can take from here is the shape and the number: this curve is about a
hundred times less responsive at its ends than in its middle, and a network
made of such curves has a property that compounds with depth. The compounding
is F12's.
\end{rigourbox}

Program~\ref{prog:F05} drew $\relu$ as half a V. What does its right-hand half
do about all of this?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Nothing at all, and that is the point}
Above zero, $\relu(x) = x$ is a straight line of constant steepness. Nudge the
input by a little and the output moves by exactly that little, at $x = 1$ and
at $x = 100$ alike. There is no flat part on that side, so there is no
saturation on that side, and nothing to compound.

Below zero it is entirely flat, which is a harder version of the same problem
rather than an escape from it \dash{} a unit sitting there answers nothing at
all rather than answering weakly. The trade the function makes is to be
perfectly responsive on one side and perfectly unresponsive on the other,
instead of gently unresponsive at both ends.

\begin{aibox}
This is the main reason $\relu$ displaced $\tanh$ in deep networks, and the
argument is one line long once you have the numbers above: $\tanh$ loses a
factor of about $\val{f07.tanh.ratio2}$ over two units either side of zero,
and $\relu$ loses nothing over its entire positive half.

Note what the argument is not. It is not that $\relu$ is smoother, simpler or
better behaved \dash{} it has a corner where $\tanh$ is smooth, and a dead
half where $\tanh$ is merely quiet. It is that a network is a composition, and
a factor that multiplies once per layer is the one property depth makes
expensive.
\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
One caution before leaving, because it is the mirror image of the trap in
section 4 and it is as easy to acquire.

\textbf{Saturation is not a reason to avoid the logistic at an output.} A
classifier's final layer is supposed to produce a probability, the flatness is
what keeps that probability inside $\intcc{0}{1}$, and the confident answers
the flat parts represent are the answers you wanted.

The complaint is about the logistic in \emph{hidden} layers, stacked, where
the flatness compounds and buys nothing. Those are two different arguments
about the same function in two different places, and the second is not
evidence for the first.
\end{fr}

\section{Softmax, previewed}
\label{sec:F07-softmax}
\index{softmax!as exponentials over their sum}

\begin{fr}
Everything so far has turned one number into one number. A classifier with
more than two answers needs to turn a \emph{list} of scores into a list of
probabilities, and the standard way is
\[ \softmax(z)_j = \frac{e^{z_j}}{\sum_k e^{z_k}} \]

Read it as three steps you already have. Exponentiate every score, which makes
them all positive and is the odds reading of section 3. Add them up, which is
Program~\ref{prog:F04}'s sigma. Divide each by that total, which forces them to
add to $1$ and so makes them a probability distribution.

Take the scores $z = (\num{2.0}, \num{0.5})$. Work out $\softmax(z)$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$(\val{f07.softmax2.a},\ \val{f07.softmax2.b})$.
\end{ansblock}

From $\frac{e^{2}}{e^{2} + e^{\num{0.5}}}$ and its partner, and they add to $1$ as
they must.

Now look at that first number again. It is $\sigma(\num{1.5})$ \dash{} the
logistic evaluated at the \emph{difference} of the two scores. The logistic is
the function Program~\ref{prog:F06} had you running backwards; here it is
running forwards, and what it is being fed is a difference.

Show that this is general: apply $\softmax$ to two scores $a$ and $b$, and
simplify the first component.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
\[ \frac{e^{a}}{e^{a} + e^{b}}
   = \frac{1}{1 + e^{b-a}} = \sigma(a - b) \]
\end{ansblock}

Divide top and bottom by $e^{a}$, exactly as in section 3.

\textbf{So softmax on two outcomes is the logistic, and softmax is not a new
function \dash{} it is the one you already have, generalised past two.}
\code{code/f07\_exponential.py} checks it over every pair on a grid, with a
worst disagreement of $\val{f07.softmax2.err}$.

That also settles a question people ask about binary classifiers: whether to
use one output with a logistic or two with a softmax. Mathematically it is the
same model, because the two-score softmax depends only on $a - b$ and a single
logistic output is that difference given a name.

There is one property left, and it is the one that makes softmax usable.
$e^{20}$ is about half a billion and $e^{800}$ is not a number a machine
holds. What could you do to the scores first to keep the exponentials small?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Subtract the largest of them from all of them.
\end{ansblock}

Then the largest score becomes $0$, its exponential becomes $1$, and every
other exponential is somewhere between $0$ and $1$. Nothing can overflow.

And it changes no answer, which is the part that has to be shown rather than
hoped:
\[ \frac{e^{z_j - c}}{\sum_k e^{z_k - c}}
   = \frac{e^{-c}\,e^{z_j}}{e^{-c}\sum_k e^{z_k}}
   = \frac{e^{z_j}}{\sum_k e^{z_k}} \]
The $e^{-c}$ is a constant factor on every term, so it cancels between the
numerator and the denominator. Program~\ref{prog:F05} made the same
observation about dividing a list by one positive number, and this is that
observation used deliberately.

\begin{warning}
\textbf{Subtracting the maximum is not an approximation and it is not a
trick.} It is an identity, and the version of softmax that does not do it is
the one that is wrong \dash{} wrong for large scores, in the sense of
producing \code{inf} and then \code{nan} rather than producing a slightly
different number.

Program~\ref{prog:P02} computes the exact score at which a naive softmax
overflows, and shows why the same reasoning applied to the logarithm gives
\code{logsumexp}.
\end{warning}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Two things softmax does \emph{not} do, both of which follow from what you have
already established.

It does not change which score is largest. Every step in it \dash{}
exponentiating, and dividing by one positive total \dash{} is strictly
increasing or a positive scaling, so Program~\ref{prog:F05}'s result applies
unchanged: $\argmax \softmax(z) = \argmax z$.

And it does not turn scores into probabilities in any sense stronger than
arithmetic. The output adds to $1$ and each entry lies in $\intoo{0}{1}$,
which is what a probability distribution is required to look like; whether the
numbers deserve to be believed is a question about the model and not about the
function, and Program~\ref{prog:P26} is where it gets asked.

\begin{aibox}
The temperature of Program~\ref{prog:F05} enters here as a divisor on the
scores, $\softmax(z/T)$, and now that you have the odds reading you can say
what it does in one sentence: it scales every log-odds by $\frac{1}{T}$, so it
raises or lowers the odds ratio between any two tokens by a power.

At $T = 2$ every odds ratio is square-rooted; at $T = \frac{1}{2}$ every one is
squared. A token that was ten times more likely than another is
$\num{3.16}$ times more likely at $T = 2$ and a hundred times more likely at
$T = \frac{1}{2}$ \dash{} which is a sharper statement of the same thing
Program~\ref{prog:F05} measured, and it explains why the argmax cannot move: a
power with a positive exponent preserves order.
\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\sumitem{1--3}{\result{\textbf{$e$ is a number, and it is where compounding stops.}
  $\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^{n}$ climbs from $2$ towards
  $\val{f07.e}\ldots$ and never passes it. $e^{x}$ obeys every index law of

\sumitem{4}{\result{$e$ is not the base that grows fastest \dash{} $3^{x}$ beats it.
  What is special is that \textbf{the steepness of $e^{x}$ equals its height},
  which this program states and Programs~\ref{prog:F11} and~\ref{prog:F12}
  prove}.}
\sumitem{4--6}{\result{\textbf{\enquote{Exponential} is a claim about the mechanism,
  not the speed.} At $x = 5$, $x^{5}$ is $\val{f07.poly.at5.ratio}$ times
  larger than $e^{x}$, and the exponential does not overtake until
  $x = \val{f07.poly.crossover}$. Adding a fixed amount to the input
  multiplies an exponential by a fixed amount; that is the whole of it}.}
\sumitem{7--8}{\result{$y_0 e^{kt}$ grows for $k > 0$ and decays for $k < 0$.
  Written as a decay, $y_0 e^{-kt}$ with $k > 0$, the half-life is
  $\frac{\ln 2}{k}$ \dash{} $\val{f07.decay.halflife}$ steps at
  $k = \num{0.05}$ \dash{} and \textbf{it does not depend on where the quantity
  started}}.}
\sumitem{9--10}{\result{\textbf{Reaching a tenth takes
  $\val{f07.decay.tenth.halflives}$ half-lives}, not ten and not five, because
  $m$ halvings divide by $2^{m}$ and you want $2^{m} = 10$. It is
  Program~\ref{prog:F01}'s $2^{10} \approx 10^{3}$ read the other way}.}
\sumitem{11--12}{\result{A per-step multiplier $\gamma$ is a rate $k = -\ln\gamma$.
  Near $\gamma = 1$ the two nearly agree, which is why they are confused; at
  $\gamma = \num{0.5}$ they do not, since $-\ln(\num{0.5}) = \num{0.693}$}.}
\sumitem{13--14}{\result{\textbf{The logistic comes from odds.} If the odds are
  $e^{x}$ to one then the probability is $\frac{e^{x}}{1+e^{x}}$, which is
  $\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}$ after dividing through by $e^{x}$. Both forms are in
  use}.}
\sumitem{15--16}{\result{It approaches $1$ and $0$ and reaches neither, because
  $e^{-x}$ is never zero and never infinite. $\sigma(-x) = 1 - \sigma(x)$
  because negating $x$ swaps which outcome the odds are quoted on. $x$ is a
  log-odds, $e^{x}$ is odds, $\sigma(x)$ is a probability: one object, three
  readings}.}
\sumitem{17--18}{\result{The steepness is largest at the centre, where it is exactly
  $\val{f07.slope.0}$, and falls to $\val{f07.slope.6}$ six units out \dash{}
  about one part in $\val{f07.slope.ratio6}$. \textbf{A unit is most
  responsive exactly when it is least certain.}}}
\sumitem{19--20}{\result{\textbf{$\tanh x = 2\sigma(2x) - 1$}, checked to
  $\val{f07.tanh.identity.err}$. It is the logistic squashed horizontally,
  stretched vertically and slid down \dash{} three of
  Program~\ref{prog:F05}'s moves \dash{} so it is not a second function to
  learn}.}
\sumitem{21--22}{\result{\textbf{$\tanh$ saturates harder, not more gently.} Its
  steepness runs $\val{f07.tanh.slope0}$ to $\val{f07.tanh.slope2}$ over two
  units against the logistic's $\val{f07.slope.0}$ to $\val{f07.slope.2}$, and
  that follows from the identity: two squashes make it four times as steep at
  the centre. The case for it was always that it is centred on zero}.}
\sumitem{23--24}{\result{A saturated unit is quiet rather than broken: it answers a
  change in its input with about one part in $\val{f07.slope.ratio6}$ of the
  response it would give at the centre. \textbf{This is not yet the
  vanishing-gradient problem} \dash{} one such factor is survivable and the
  product of forty is not, and the multiplying is
  Program~\ref{prog:F12}'s}.}
\sumitem{25--26}{\result{$\relu$ has no flat part on its positive side at all, so
  there is nothing there to compound; below zero it is flat entirely, which is
  a harder version of the problem rather than an escape. And saturation is no
  argument against a logistic at an \emph{output}, where the flatness is what
  keeps a probability inside its range}.}
\sumitem{27--28}{\result{$\softmax(z)_j = \frac{e^{z_j}}{\sum_k e^{z_k}}$:
  exponentiate, add up, divide by the total. On
  $z = (\num{2.0}, \num{0.5})$ it gives
  $(\val{f07.softmax2.a},\ \val{f07.softmax2.b})$}.}
\sumitem{29}{\result{\textbf{On two scores softmax is the logistic of their
  difference}, $\frac{e^{a}}{e^{a}+e^{b}} = \sigma(a-b)$, checked to
  $\val{f07.softmax2.err}$. So one logistic output and a two-way softmax are
  the same model}.}
\sumitem{30}{\result{\textbf{Subtracting the largest score from every score changes
  nothing}, because the common factor cancels above and below \dash{} and it
  is what stops the exponentials overflowing. It is an identity, not an
  approximation}.}
\sumitem{31}{\result{Softmax cannot move the $\argmax$, by
  Program~\ref{prog:F05}'s result. Dividing the scores by a temperature scales
  every log-odds by $\frac{1}{T}$, so it raises every odds ratio to a power
  \dash{} which is why the ordering survives and the gaps do not}.}
\end{summarybox}

  \answerto{The product $p(1-p)$ over $p$ in $\intcc{0}{1}$ is largest at

  \answerto{Where $p(1-p) = \frac{1}{8}$, which is $p \approx \num{0.146}$ or

  its responsiveness.}

  \answerto{$2\sigma(2x) - 1$ squashes horizontally by $2$, which doubles the

  \answerto{About $\num{0.0003}$, about $-1$, and exactly $0$. All three are

  \answerto{Because there is only one of it. The complaint is that the factor

  \answerto{Shrinks it dramatically, because the inputs to the second

  \answerto{$\ln 2 \approx \num{0.693}$, because the odds ratio is

  \answerto{It becomes $\frac{a-b}{T}$, so the odds ratio becomes
