\program{Floating point: what the machine actually computes}
\label{prog:P01}
\index{floating point!what a float is}
\index{numbers!machine representation}

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{1--6}{read a float as a sign, an exponent and a significand, and say
  what each of the three carries}
\outcome{7--8}{say what the distance between neighbouring representable
  numbers has to do with their size}
\outcome{9--12}{say what machine epsilon is, and use it to decide whether two
  numbers can be told apart at all}
\outcome{13--19}{say what the order of a sum has to do with its result, and
  what makes a contribution too small to add}
\outcome{20--26}{compute a format's ceiling from its exponent budget, and
  compare two formats of the same width}
\outcome{27--33}{say what a subnormal is, and what a format's two floors are
  each for}
\end{outcomes}

\begin{quiz}
\item A float is written as a sign, an exponent and a significand. Which of
  the three carries the precision? \teachesat{2--3}
  \answerto{The significand. The exponent carries the size and says nothing
  about how finely the number is resolved.}
\item The distance from $1$ to the next representable double is
  $\val{p01.gap.one}$. What is the distance from a billion to the next
  representable double? \teachesat{7--8}
  \answerto{$\val{p01.gap.billion}$. The gap scales with the number, so it is
  larger by roughly the factor that a billion is larger than $1$.}
\item What does \code{0.1 + 0.2} evaluate to in Python? \teachesat{10--11}
  \answerto{$\val{p01.sum.shown}$. It is not $\num{0.3}$, and the difference
  is $\val{p01.sum.gap}$.}
\item What does \code{1.0 + 1e-17 == 1.0} evaluate to? \teachesat{16--17}
  \answerto{\code{True}. $10^{-17}$ is below half the gap at $1$, so under
  round-to-nearest the addition moves nothing at all.}
\item \code{fp16} and \code{bf16} are both sixteen bits wide. What differs
  between them? \teachesat{20--21}
  \answerto{How the sixteen bits are split. \code{fp16} spends
  $\val{p01.fp16.ebits}$ on the exponent and $\val{p01.fp16.mbits}$ on the
  significand; \code{bf16} spends $\val{p01.bf16.ebits}$ and
  $\val{p01.bf16.mbits}$.}
\item A format has $\val{p01.fp16.ebits}$ exponent bits. Roughly how large a
  number can it hold? \teachesat{22--23}
  \answerto{$\val{p01.fp16.max}$. Five bits give thirty-two exponent values,
  two of which are reserved, and the top one puts the number at about
  $2^{16}$.}
\item Two accelerators sum the same gradients and report different totals in
  the last few bits. Which one is wrong? \teachesat{14--15}
  \answerto{Neither. Addition in floating point is not associative, so a
  different order of summation is a different \emph{correct} answer.}
\item What is directly below the smallest normal number of a format?
  \teachesat{27--28}
  \answerto{Subnormal numbers, which continue down to a second and lower
  floor \dash{} $\val{p01.fp64.minsub}$ for a double, against a smallest
  normal of $\val{p01.fp64.minnorm}$.}
\item Program F03 computed a sequence probability of $\val{p01.f03.prob}$.
  What happens when you ask a double to hold it? \teachesat{30--31}
  \answerto{It cannot. The value is far below the smallest subnormal, so it
  reads back as $\val{p01.f03.readback}$ \dash{} exactly zero, with no
  warning.}
\end{quiz}

\section{Scientific notation, in base two}
\label{sec:P01-form}
\index{floating point!as scientific notation}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:F01} wrote every number as
\[ m \times 10^{e}, \qquad 1 \le m < 10, \quad e \text{ an integer} \]
and said what each part is for: $m$ is the mantissa and carries the precision,
$e$ is the exponent and carries the size.

Your machine does exactly that, with one digit changed and one restriction
added. The digit is the base: it works in twos, not tens. The restriction is
the interesting one, and it is what this whole program follows from.

Before it is named: a double occupies $\val{p01.fp64.width}$ bits. At most how
many different numbers can it possibly represent?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$2^{64}$ of them, and not one more.}
That is a large number and it is a \emph{finite} one, which is the restriction.
There are infinitely many real numbers between $1$ and $2$; your machine has
room for finitely many of them, so it holds a selection and rounds everything
else to the nearest member of it.

So the question stops being \enquote{how accurate is the arithmetic} and
becomes \textbf{which numbers exist, and how far apart are they}. Every
surprise in this program is an answer to that one question.

The form is
\[ \pm\, m \times 2^{e}, \qquad 1 \le m < 2 \]
with $m$ written in binary on a fixed number of bits. Two of those three
symbols are stored in separate fields. Which of them carries the size of the
number, and which carries how finely it is resolved?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$e$ carries the size. $m$ carries the precision.
\end{ansblock}

Same division of labour as base ten, which is why Program~\ref{prog:F01} is
enough preparation for this. What is new is that $m$ has a fixed budget of
bits, so the precision is fixed \emph{as a number of significant figures} and
not as a distance.

Hold that sentence. It is the whole of section 2.

A float therefore has three fields: a \emph{sign} bit, an \emph{exponent}, and
a \emph{significand} holding the bits of $m$. You do not have to take that on
trust, and this program's method is that you should not.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\code{struct} converts between a Python float and the sixty-four bits your
machine actually stores, so the three fields can be read off directly.

\transcript{p01-bits}

Two of those three lines do a job. $\num{1.0}$ and $\num{2.0}$ have the same sign and the
same significand and differ only in the exponent. $\num{1.0}$ and $\num{1.5}$ have the
same sign and the same exponent and differ only in the significand.

What has the exponent done between $\num{1.0}$ and $\num{2.0}$?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{It went up by one, from $1023$ to $1024$.}
Doubling a number adds one to its exponent and leaves the significand alone,
because that is what multiplying by $2$ does in base two \dash{} exactly as
multiplying by $10$ in base ten moves the point and leaves the digits. The
stored exponent is offset so that negative exponents need no sign of their
own; $1023$ is the offset, so $\num{1.0}$ has a true exponent of $0$.

The budgets, for a double:
\[ \underbrace{1}_{\text{sign}} + \underbrace{\val{p01.fp64.ebits}}_{\text{exponent}}
   + \underbrace{\val{p01.fp64.mbits}}_{\text{significand}}
   = \val{p01.fp64.width} \]
and a \code{float32} splits its $\val{p01.fp32.width}$ bits
$1 + \val{p01.fp32.ebits} + \val{p01.fp32.mbits}$. Those two numbers \dash{}
the exponent budget and the significand budget \dash{} are the entire
specification of a format. Everything else in this program is derived from
them.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{notationbox}
\textbf{Significand, not mantissa.} The standard says \emph{significand} for
the stored bits of $m$, and reserves \emph{mantissa} for the fractional part
of a logarithm, which is a different object that Program~\ref{prog:F03} would
recognise. In practice both words are used for both things and no confusion
results, but the two budgets that define a format are usually quoted as
\enquote{exponent bits} and \enquote{significand bits}, and those are the words
this program uses.
\end{notationbox}

There is one more piece of housekeeping, and it buys a bit for nothing. Since
$1 \le m < 2$, the leading binary digit of $m$ is always $1$ \dash{} so it is
never stored. A double's $\val{p01.fp64.mbits}$ stored bits therefore carry
$53$ significant bits. That is the \emph{implicit leading one}, and section 5
is about what happens when it has to be given up.
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{p01-three-fields}{The three fields of a float, and the job each
one does.}{a float's three fields}

\section{The gap grows with magnitude}
\label{sec:P01-gap}
\index{floating point!spacing of representable numbers}
\index{numbers!machine representation}

\begin{fr}
The distance from $1$ to the next double above it is $\val{p01.gap.one}$. Read
off the bits, not deduced: increment the sixty-four-bit pattern by one and
subtract.

Now a prediction, and write it down before reading on. The distance from a
billion to the next double above \emph{it} \dash{} is it the same
$\val{p01.gap.one}$, or larger, or smaller?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Larger. Very much larger: $\val{p01.gap.billion}$.}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Floating-point error is not a small random wobble. It is a spacing,
and the spacing is a fixed fraction of the number.}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
distance to the next double above & \\
\midrule
$1$ & $\val{p01.gap.one}$ \\
$1000$ & $\val{p01.gap.thousand}$ \\
a billion & $\val{p01.gap.billion}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The reason is section 1's, and there is nothing else in it. The significand
budget fixes the number of \emph{significant figures}, and a fixed number of
significant figures is a fixed \emph{relative} precision. So the absolute gap
has to grow in step with the number, and it does.

Anybody who reasons about floating point as though it had a fixed absolute
accuracy \dash{} a tolerance of $10^{-9}$, say, applied to every comparison in
a codebase \dash{} has the wrong model, and it fails at exactly the magnitudes
where it matters.
\end{trapbox}

Take that on trust and you have learnt nothing, so do the division. Go back to
the table above, divide each of its three gaps by the number it sits beside,
and say what the three answers have in common.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{The same number, give or take a factor of two: about $2^{-52}$.}

The relative version of the gap is one number rather than a table, and it has
a name.

\textbf{Machine epsilon} is the gap between $1$ and the next representable
number up. For a double that is $\val{p01.fp64.eps}$, which is $2^{-52}$ and
therefore just the significand budget written as a number. The factor of two
either side is where the number falls between one power of two and the next;
what matters is that dividing by the magnitude leaves a constant behind, and
that is why one number can replace a table.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
 & \code{fp64} & \code{fp32} & \code{fp16} & \code{bf16} \\
\midrule
significand bits & $\val{p01.fp64.mbits}$ & $\val{p01.fp32.mbits}$
  & $\val{p01.fp16.mbits}$ & $\val{p01.bf16.mbits}$ \\
epsilon & $\val{p01.fp64.eps}$ & $\val{p01.fp32.eps}$
  & $\val{p01.fp16.eps}$ & $\val{p01.bf16.eps}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Epsilon is the answer to \enquote{how many digits do I actually have}, and it
is the only honest form of that question: $\val{p01.fp32.eps}$ is about
$\val{p01.fp32.digits}$ decimal digits and $\val{p01.bf16.eps}$ is about
$\val{p01.bf16.digits}$.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Now the demonstration everybody has met and few have had explained. In a
Python prompt, what does \code{0.1 + 0.2} evaluate to?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$\val{p01.sum.shown}$
\end{ansblock}

\transcript{p01-tenth}

Program~\ref{prog:F01} gave the reason and this program gives the machinery.
The reason is place value: in base two $\tfrac{1}{10}$ repeats for ever, as
$\tfrac{1}{3}$ does in base ten, so no finite significand holds it. The
machinery is the last line \dash{} what \code{0.1} actually is, to twenty
places, is $\val{p01.tenth.stored}$.

So \code{0.1} was already wrong before any arithmetic happened, and so was
\code{0.2}, and the sum of two roundings is off by $\val{p01.sum.gap}$. That
is exactly one gap \emph{at $\num{0.3}$}, which is the smallest error the
format can have there \dash{} and a quarter of the gap at $1$, which is the
next section's point arriving early.

So \code{0.1 + 0.2 == 0.3} is the wrong question to have asked. Write down the
question you would ask in its place.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Whether the two are close enough \dash{} with close enough measured as a
fraction of their size rather than as a fixed amount.}

\textbf{Equality is not a question you should ask.} \code{a == b} on two
computed floats asks whether two chains of rounding landed on the identical
member of a finite set, which is not what you wanted to know and is not stable
under a change of compiler, library or hardware.

\begin{aibox}
Two training runs report a validation loss of $\val{p01.loss.a}$ and
$\val{p01.loss.b}$. They differ by $\val{p01.loss.diff}$, and the gap between
neighbouring \code{fp32} numbers at that magnitude is $\val{p01.loss.gap}$
\dash{} so the two runs did not report different numbers so much as adjacent
ones. The seventh decimal place of an \code{fp32} loss is not a property of the
model; it is a property of the order in which the batch happened to be reduced.
A leaderboard sorted on it is sorting on the hardware.
\end{aibox}

The multiple is of epsilon and the size is the size of the numbers being
compared, which is the gap table doing its second job. A tolerance that does
not scale is far too tight at a billion, where it is smaller than a single
gap, and far too loose at a thousandth, where it spans billions of them.
\end{fr}

\section{Order changes the answer}
\label{sec:P01-order}
\index{floating point!non-associativity}
\index{troubleshooting!two devices, two totals}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:F04} wrote a sum with a sigma and then said, in a warning
box, that a machine computes an approximation to it in an order you did not
choose \dash{} and left this program to say why a sum of gradients is not
associative and why two accelerators therefore disagree in the last bits. This
is that frame.

Take three numbers: $a = \val{p01.assoc.big}$, and $b$ and $c$ both equal to
$\val{p01.assoc.small}$. In exact arithmetic $(a + b) + c$ and $a + (b + c)$
are the same number, and you have never once questioned that.

Are they the same number on your machine?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
No.
\[ (a + b) + c = \val{p01.assoc.left}, \qquad
   a + (b + c) = \val{p01.assoc.right} \]
\end{ansblock}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Addition in floating point is not associative}, and the mechanism is
the one section 2 established rather than anything new.

Each operation rounds to the nearest representable number, immediately. On the
left, $a + b$ is rounded first: $\val{p01.assoc.small}$ is smaller than the gap
at $1$, so the sum rounds back to $1$ and $b$ is gone. Adding $c$ then loses
$c$ the same way, and the total is exactly $1$.

On the right, $b + c$ is added first. Two tiny numbers of similar size add
without difficulty, giving $2 \times \val{p01.assoc.small}$, which is now large
enough to move the result off $1$.

\textbf{Nothing is broken and neither answer is a bug.} Both are the correctly
rounded result of the operations in the order they were written. It is the
mathematics that has an extra property \dash{} associativity \dash{} which the
machine does not.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The consequence is one you have probably already seen and possibly filed as a
defect.

Two accelerators reduce the same gradients into a total. They split the work
differently, so they add the same numbers in different orders, and they report
totals that differ in the last few bits. Change the number of devices and the
loss curve changes in the sixth decimal place. Re-run with the same seed on a
different card and the numbers do not match.

None of that is a fault. It is the frame above, at scale. A reduction is a
choice of order, the library chooses one, and a different partition is a
different choice.

\begin{warning}
\textbf{So bit-exact reproducibility across hardware is a thing you buy, not a
thing you have.} It costs a fixed reduction order, which usually costs
parallelism, and it is worth paying for a debugging session and rarely worth
paying for a training run. What you should expect by default is agreement to a
relative tolerance, not agreement in the last bit.
\end{warning}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
There is a sharper version of the left-hand case above, and it is the one to
carry away.

Predict this one before reading on. In Python, what does
\code{1.0 + 1e-17 == 1.0} evaluate to?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{\code{True}. The sum is $\val{p01.swamp.sum}$, exactly.}
Not \enquote{approximately $1$}, and not \enquote{$1$ plus a rounding error}.
The addition moved nothing whatsoever. It is a no-op that returns successfully.

The threshold is exact and it follows from the gap. Under round-to-nearest, a
value smaller than \emph{half} the gap at the running total rounds away
completely, because the nearest representable number to the result is still
the number you started with. Half the gap at $1$ is $\val{p01.swamp.at.one}$,
and $\val{p01.swamp.tiny}$ is comfortably under it.

So there is a size below which a contribution is not small. It is zero.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
And because the gap is a fixed fraction of the number, so is the threshold.
Half an epsilon is the whole rule:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
in this format & a contribution below this fraction of the total is lost \\
\midrule
\code{fp64} & $\val{p01.swamp.fp64}$ \\
\code{fp32} & $\val{p01.swamp.fp32}$ \\
\code{fp16} & $\val{p01.swamp.fp16}$ \\
\code{bf16} & $\val{p01.swamp.bf16}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Read the \code{fp32} row against an afternoon's work: a gradient of $10^{-8}$
added to a running total of $1$ is not a small update, it is no update. And
the \code{bf16} row says a contribution below $\val{p01.swamp.bf16.pct}$ per
cent of the total does nothing at all, which is why an accumulator is very often kept
in a wider format than the values going into it.

\begin{rigourbox}
\textbf{What this program stops short of.} One contribution vanishing is a
fact about the encoding and is settled above. What it costs when a million of
them vanish in a row, and the catalogue of things to do about it \dash{}
reordering, compensated summation, a wider accumulator \dash{} is
Program~\ref{prog:P02}, whose subject it is. Program~\ref{prog:F04}'s warning
box divides the two the same way.
\end{rigourbox}

The \code{fp32} row is worth stopping at before the next frame stops at it for
you. It is half the gap at $1$ in that format. Section 2's table gives a
double's gap at three magnitudes: at which of the three is half that gap the
same number?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{At a billion. Half a double's gap there is
$\val{p01.swamp.at.billion}$, which is the \code{fp32} row exactly.}

One number from section 2 is worth a second look, because it connects the two
formats you actually choose between.

Half the gap at $1$ in a double is $\val{p01.swamp.at.one}$. Half the gap at a
billion in a double is $\val{p01.swamp.at.billion}$ \dash{} and half the gap
at $1$ in an \code{fp32} is $\val{p01.swamp.fp32}$, which is that same number.

That is exact rather than approximate, and it is a property of that particular
magnitude rather than a law: a billion falls in the range of exponents
starting at $2^{29}$, and $29$ is precisely the difference between the two
significand budgets, $\val{p01.fp64.mbits} - \val{p01.fp32.mbits}$. Move to ten
billion and the coincidence is gone.

Useful anyway, as a way of holding the whole subject in one sentence:
\textbf{a double counting near a billion is as coarse as a float counting near
one.} Precision is not a property of a format. It is a property of a format
and a magnitude together.
\end{fr}

\section{Same width, different split}
\label{sec:P01-split}
\index{floating point!fp16 against bf16}
\index{formats!choosing between two of the same width}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:F01} worked out what a model weighs and left a question
open: what \enquote{two bytes per parameter} actually buys, and why
\code{bf16} became the training format while \code{fp16} needs a scaling trick
to work at all. That question is this section.

Both are sixteen bits. Both spend one on the sign. Given section 1, there is
exactly one thing left that can differ between them. What?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
How the remaining fifteen bits are divided between the exponent and the
significand.
\end{ansblock}

That is the only degree of freedom a format has, and the two make opposite
choices:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
 & \code{fp16} & \code{bf16} \\
\midrule
exponent bits & $\val{p01.fp16.ebits}$ & $\val{p01.bf16.ebits}$ \\
significand bits & $\val{p01.fp16.mbits}$ & $\val{p01.bf16.mbits}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\code{bf16} takes three bits off the significand and gives them to the
exponent. Since the width is fixed, that is the only kind of move available:
a bit added to one field is a bit taken from the other.
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{p01-same-width}{Sixteen bits, two ways of spending them, and the
trade that fixes.}{one width, two splits}

\begin{fr}
Work out what those three bits are worth, and do it in the direction that
matters \dash{} the exponent's.

An exponent field of $b$ bits holds $2^{b}$ patterns, two of which are reserved
(one for zero and the subnormals of section 5, one for the infinities), so the
true exponent runs over about $2^{b} - 2$ values, roughly half of them
negative. For $\val{p01.fp16.ebits}$ bits the largest true exponent works out
at $15$.

A significand just under $2$ then puts the ceiling just under $2^{16}$. Give
the number.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p01.fp16.max.exact}$.}
Exactly that, and it is a number worth recognising on sight, because you will
meet it as an \code{inf} in a log file rather than as a documented limit.

Now do \code{bf16}, which has $\val{p01.bf16.ebits}$ exponent bits. The
arithmetic is the same and the answer is $\val{p01.bf16.max}$ \dash{} and set
that beside \code{fp32}'s ceiling, which is $\val{p01.fp32.max}$.

They agree to two figures and not to three, and both halves of that are worth
a moment. They are close because \code{bf16} and \code{fp32} have the same
exponent budget, $\val{p01.bf16.ebits}$ bits each. They are not equal because
the largest value of a format is $(2 - 2^{-m}) \times 2^{127}$, and
\code{bf16}'s shorter significand makes its first bracket slightly smaller
\dash{} a shortfall of $\val{p01.bf16.short.pct}$ per cent.

\textbf{\code{bf16} is \code{fp32} with the bottom of the significand cut
off.} It reaches within half a per cent as far, it resolves far more coarsely,
and a conversion between the two cannot overflow in either direction.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\code{bf16} is not the more precise format. It is the less precise
one, by a factor of eight, and that is what it was designed to be.}

The ratio of the two ceilings is $\val{p01.bf16.over.fp16}$ \dash{} thirty-three
orders of magnitude of extra reach, bought with three bits. What it cost is in
the epsilon row of section 2: $\val{p01.bf16.eps}$ against \code{fp16}'s
$\val{p01.fp16.eps}$, so about $\val{p01.bf16.digits}$ decimal digits of
precision against about $\val{p01.fp16.digits}$.

If you had guessed that the newer format was the more accurate one, that is
the ordinary reading and it is the wrong way round. The design question was
never \enquote{how accurate} but \enquote{what fails first}, and the answer
for training is range, not precision.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Why range fails first is worth one frame, because it is the argument the whole
section is for.

A gradient is a small number, and a gradient in a deep network is a product of
small numbers \dash{} which is Program~\ref{prog:F12}'s result, and it showed
the product running away downwards very fast. Anything below a format's floor
becomes zero and stops contributing. Anything above its ceiling becomes
\code{inf} and poisons everything it touches.

So the failure that ends a training run is a value leaving the representable
range, at one end or the other, and \textbf{a format's range is set by its
exponent budget alone}. Precision degrades gracefully; range does not degrade,
it stops.

\begin{aibox}
That is what \emph{loss scaling} is for. Multiply the loss by a large constant
before the backward pass, so every gradient is multiplied by the same constant
and lifted off \code{fp16}'s floor; divide the gradients by it again before the
update, which restores the mathematics exactly because a constant factor
distributes over the whole backward pass. The constant is usually chosen
dynamically \dash{} raise it until something overflows, then back off.

It is a workaround for $\val{p01.fp16.ebits}$ exponent bits, and it is the
reason \code{bf16} training needs no such machinery: with
$\val{p01.bf16.ebits}$ bits the gradients were never near the floor.
\end{aibox}

That settles the weights and the gradients. It leaves the running sums that
section 3 showed swallowing small contributions whole. Which of the two
budgets does an accumulator need, and therefore which format?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Precision, so \code{fp32} \dash{} an accumulator is nowhere near either
end of the range and everywhere near the swamping threshold.}

One consequence to carry into every mixed-precision decision you make.

The choice of format is a choice about \emph{which} of the two budgets you
are short of, and the two budgets fail differently. Ask which quantity in your
computation is closest to leaving the range, and give the exponent bits to
that. Ask which quantity needs to be told apart from its neighbours, and give
the significand bits to that.

In practice the two answers land in different places, which is why mixed
precision exists at all: weights and gradients in sixteen bits because they
need range and tolerate coarseness, and the accumulators \dash{} the running
sums that section 3 showed swallowing small contributions whole \dash{} in
\code{fp32}, because that is where precision is what runs out.
\end{fr}

\section{Two floors, not one}
\label{sec:P01-floors}
\index{floating point!subnormal numbers}
\index{floating point!underflow}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:F03} promised this section by name \dash{} what a subnormal
is, and why a format has two thresholds rather than one \dash{} and here is the
first half of the debt.

A double's smallest \emph{normal} number \dash{} the smallest one with the
implicit leading $1$ of section 1 still in place \dash{} is
$\val{p01.fp64.minnorm}$. That is what the exponent budget reaches down to.

What is directly below it? Write down what you think happens between
$\val{p01.fp64.minnorm}$ and zero.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Not zero, and not nothing. There is a second range of numbers, and a second
floor a long way below the first.
\end{ansblock}

The smallest number a double can represent at all is $\val{p01.fp64.minsub}$,
which is smaller than the smallest normal by a factor of about $10^{16}$. The
numbers in between are the \emph{subnormals}, and they exist because of the
implicit leading one.

Once the exponent has bottomed out, the only way to go lower is to let the
significand start with a zero \dash{} to give up the implicit $1$ and shift the
digits right. Each step down costs one more bit of significand. So a subnormal
buys range at the cost of precision, one bit at a time, and by the time you
reach the second floor there is a single significant bit left.
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{p01-two-floors}{Normals, then subnormals, then a second floor.}{a
format's two floors}

\begin{fr}
Both floors, in all four formats, and the gap between them is the interesting
column:

Notice the \code{bf16} row. Its smallest normal matches \code{fp32}'s exactly,

Both ends together are worth one line: a double runs from
$\val{p01.fp64.minsub}$ to $\val{p01.fp64.max}$, which is over six hundred
orders of magnitude. That is why a double so rarely makes you think about
either end, and why a sixteen-bit format makes you think about both.

\begin{warning}
\textbf{Subnormals are not free, and on some hardware they are not even
present.} Processors and libraries commonly offer a \emph{flush-to-zero} mode
that replaces every subnormal result with zero, and the reason such a switch
exists at all is that handling them can be markedly slower than ordinary
arithmetic. This book does not measure that cost, because it is a fact about a
chip rather than about the mathematics \dash{} but the existence of the switch
is worth knowing, because it means the floor you are actually computing
against may be the higher one.
\end{warning}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:F11} is owed something too, and it is one sentence now that
the machinery is here.

That program measured the error in a finite difference as $h$ shrinks and found
a U: the error falls, bottoms out, and then climbs again. The left branch is
calculus \dash{} the chord is not yet the tangent. The right branch is this
program. Subtracting two nearly equal numbers leaves a result whose leading
significant digits have cancelled, so the difference is carried by whatever
bits were at the bottom, and the fewer of those there are the coarser the
answer. Dividing that by a tiny $h$ magnifies it.

\begin{rigourbox}
\textbf{Which is as far as P01 takes it.} The mechanism has a name \dash{}
catastrophic cancellation \dash{} and a catalogue of remedies, and both are
Program~\ref{prog:P02}'s. What belongs here is why it happens: not that the
subtraction is inaccurate, but that its inputs were rounded before it started,
and the subtraction removed everything the two had in common and left the
rounding behind.
\end{rigourbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
One last frame, and it is the sentence to leave with.

Every surprise in this program came from one fact, stated in frame 2: the
machine holds finitely many numbers, so it holds a selection and rounds
everything else to the nearest member of it. The gaps in the selection grow
with magnitude; two floors mark where the selection stops at the bottom and
the exponent budget marks where it stops at the top; and rounding after every
operation is what makes order matter.

Nothing here is a defect and nothing here has a fix. What it has is a shape,
and knowing the shape is what lets you tell a numerical failure from a
modelling one \dash{} which is the difference between an afternoon and a week.

Program~\ref{prog:P02} takes the same arithmetic and asks the engineering
question: given that this is what you have, which algorithms survive it and
which do not.
\end{fr}

\begin{summarybox}
\sumitem{1--3}{\result{\textbf{A float is Program~\ref{prog:F01}'s scientific notation
  with the ten changed to a two and the mantissa's digits capped}:
  $\pm\, m \times 2^{e}$ with $1 \le m < 2$. A double has
  $\val{p01.fp64.width}$ bits, so it holds at most $2^{64}$ numbers \dash{}
  finitely many. It keeps a selection and rounds everything else to the
  nearest member of it, and that one sentence generates the rest}.}
\sumitem{4--6}{\result{\textbf{Three fields}: a sign bit, an exponent carrying the
  size, and a significand carrying the precision, split
  $1 + \val{p01.fp64.ebits} + \val{p01.fp64.mbits}$ in a double. They can be
  read with \code{struct} rather than taken on trust \dash{} $\num{1.0}$ and $\num{2.0}$
  differ only in the exponent, $\num{1.0}$ and $\num{1.5}$ only in the significand. The
  leading $1$ of $m$ is never stored, which buys a bit and is what section 5
  is about giving up}.}
\sumitem{7--8}{\result{\textbf{The gap grows with the number}:
  $\val{p01.gap.one}$ at $1$, $\val{p01.gap.thousand}$ at $1000$,
  $\val{p01.gap.billion}$ at a billion. A fixed budget of significant bits is a
  fixed \emph{relative} precision, so the absolute spacing has to scale. A
  single absolute tolerance applied across a codebase has the wrong model and
  fails where it matters}.}
\sumitem{9}{\result{\textbf{Machine epsilon} is the gap at $1$, which is the
  significand budget as a number: $\val{p01.fp64.eps}$, $\val{p01.fp32.eps}$,
  $\val{p01.fp16.eps}$, $\val{p01.bf16.eps}$ \dash{} about
  $\val{p01.fp64.digits}$, $\val{p01.fp32.digits}$, $\val{p01.fp16.digits}$
  and $\val{p01.bf16.digits}$ decimal digits}.}
\sumitem{10--12}{\result{\code{0.1 + 0.2} gives $\val{p01.sum.shown}$ because
  \code{0.1} is stored as $\val{p01.tenth.stored}$ and was wrong before any
  arithmetic happened. \textbf{Equality is not a question you should ask};
  tolerances must be relative. Two \code{fp32} losses differing in the seventh
  decimal differ by less than epsilon at that magnitude, so the difference is
  the reduction order rather than the model}.}
\sumitem{13--15}{\result{\textbf{Addition is not associative.} With
  $\val{p01.assoc.small}$ twice, $(a+b)+c$ gives $\val{p01.assoc.left}$ and
  $a+(b+c)$ gives $\val{p01.assoc.right}$: rounding happens after every
  operation, so a small value added to a large one can be lost while two small
  ones added together survive. Two accelerators partitioning a reduction
  differently therefore disagree in the last bits, and neither is wrong}.}
\sumitem{16--18}{\result{\textbf{Below half a gap, a contribution is not small, it is
  zero} \dash{} \code{1.0 + 1e-17} returns $\val{p01.swamp.sum}$ exactly. The
  threshold is half an epsilon of the running total: $\val{p01.swamp.fp32}$ in
  \code{fp32}, $\val{p01.swamp.bf16}$ in \code{bf16}, which is why accumulators
  are kept wider than the values entering them. What a million such losses cost
  is Program~\ref{prog:P02}'s}.}
\sumitem{19}{\result{Half a double's gap at a billion is exactly half an \code{fp32}
  epsilon, because $29$ is the difference of the two significand budgets.
  \textbf{Precision is a property of a format and a magnitude together}, never
  of a format alone}.}
\sumitem{20--21}{\result{\code{fp16} and \code{bf16} are both sixteen bits and differ
  only in where they cut: $\val{p01.fp16.ebits}$ exponent bits against
  $\val{p01.bf16.ebits}$. The width is fixed, so \textbf{a bit added to one
  field is a bit taken from the other}}.}
\sumitem{22--23}{\result{\code{fp16}'s ceiling is $\val{p01.fp16.max.exact}$, computed from
  its exponent budget rather than looked up. \code{bf16}'s is
  $\val{p01.bf16.max}$, which is \code{fp32}'s $\val{p01.fp32.max}$ \dash{} the
  same exponent budget. \textbf{\code{bf16} is \code{fp32} with the bottom of
  the significand removed}, so converting between them cannot overflow}.}
\sumitem{24--26}{\result{\textbf{\code{bf16} is the \emph{less} precise format} \dash{}
  $\val{p01.bf16.eps}$ against $\val{p01.fp16.eps}$ \dash{} bought
  $\val{p01.bf16.over.fp16}$ times the reach. Range fails first because a value
  outside the range stops existing while precision only coarsens, which is what
  loss scaling works around in \code{fp16} and why \code{bf16} needs none. The
  format choice is a question about which budget you are short of}.}
\sumitem{27--29}{\result{\textbf{A format has two floors.} Below the smallest normal
  ($\val{p01.fp64.minnorm}$ for a double) the implicit leading $1$ is given up
  one bit at a time, giving the \textbf{subnormals} down to a second floor at
  $\val{p01.fp64.minsub}$. A format's subnormal range is as deep as its
  significand is wide, so \code{bf16}'s is shallow. Flush-to-zero modes exist,
  so the floor you compute against may be the higher one}.}
\sumitem{30--32}{\result{Program~\ref{prog:F03}'s $\val{p01.f03.prob}$ reads back as
  $\val{p01.f03.readback}$ \dash{} \textbf{a number this book computes and
  cannot type in}, which is why it was computed as a logarithm. A coin-flipping
  model's product is exactly zero after $\val{p01.coin.fp64}$ tokens in
  \code{fp64} and $\val{p01.coin.fp16}$ in \code{fp16}}.}
\sumitem{33--35}{\result{Program~\ref{prog:F12}'s $\val{p01.f12.prod}$ is a small
  number in \code{fp64} and \textbf{exactly zero in \code{fp32}}, so a
  vanishing gradient is not always a gradient that became small \dash{} often
  it is one that left the number system. And Program~\ref{prog:F11}'s
  right-hand branch is subtraction leaving the rounding behind. Both mechanisms
  become a discipline in Program~\ref{prog:P02}}.}
\end{summarybox}

\begin{furtherproblems}
\item Explain why $1 \le m < 2$ makes the leading bit of $m$ free to omit, and
  what would go wrong if the standard had chosen $0 \le m < 1$ instead.
  \answerto{With $1 \le m < 2$ the leading binary digit is always $1$, so it
  carries no information and need not be stored. With $0 \le m < 1$ the leading
  digit can be $0$, the representation stops being unique \dash{} the same
  value has many forms \dash{} and no bit is saved.}
\item Half the gap at a billion in a double equals half an \code{fp32} epsilon
  exactly. Show why, and find another magnitude where the same thing happens.
  \answerto{A billion lies between $2^{29}$ and $2^{30}$, so the gap is
  $2^{29-52} = 2^{-23}$, and \code{fp32}'s epsilon is $2^{-23}$ because it has
  $\val{p01.fp32.mbits}$ significand bits. It happens for every value in
  $\intco{2^{29}}{2^{30}}$ and nowhere else \dash{} at $2^{30}$ itself the
  binade changes and the gap doubles.}
\item A running total in \code{bf16} sits at $100$. Give the smallest
  contribution that can still change it.
  \answerto{About $\val{p01.swamp.bf16} \times 100$, so roughly $\num{0.4}$. The
  threshold is half an epsilon of the total, and it scales with the total.}
\item \code{bf16} and \code{fp32} have the same exponent budget. Say what that
  means for a conversion in each direction.
  \answerto{Neither direction can overflow or underflow, because the
  representable range is the same. Converting down loses significand bits and
  nothing else; converting up is exact.}
\item Loss scaling multiplies the loss by a constant before the backward pass
  and divides the gradients by it afterwards. Explain why this does not change
  the mathematics.
  \answerto{Differentiation is linear, so scaling the loss scales every
  gradient by the same constant; dividing them all by it again restores the
  original values exactly, in exact arithmetic. What it changes is which
  gradients fell below the format's floor while being computed.}
\item Suppose a format's subnormals were removed, so that anything below the
  smallest normal became zero. Say what would get worse and what would get
  better.
  \answerto{Worse: the floor rises abruptly by a factor of about $10^{16}$ in
  a double, so underflow arrives much sooner and the loss of the smallest
  values is sudden rather than gradual. Better: every representable number has
  full precision and the hardware has one fewer case to handle, which is why
  flush-to-zero modes exist.}
\item Program F11's finite-difference error rises again as $h$ gets small.
  Explain the rise using nothing but the gap.
  \answerto{$f(x+h)$ and $f(x)$ are each stored to a relative precision of
  about epsilon, so each carries an absolute error of about $\epsilon|f(x)|$.
  Subtracting removes the part they share and leaves those errors, which are
  then divided by $h$. The smaller $h$ is, the larger the magnified error.}
\item Argue that a format with a $64$-bit significand and a $4$-bit exponent
  would be a poor choice for training, without computing its ceiling exactly.
  \answerto{Four exponent bits give about fourteen usable exponents, so the
  representable range spans a few orders of magnitude at most. Gradients in a
  deep network span far more than that, so almost everything would flush to
  zero or overflow, while the nineteen decimal digits of precision would be
  measuring quantities that no longer exist.}
\end{furtherproblems}
