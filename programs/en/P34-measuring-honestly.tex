\program{Measuring a model honestly}
\label{prog:P34}
\index{evaluation}

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{1--7}{say what a benchmark score is an estimate of, and which half
  of its error more items can reach}
\outcome{8--15}{re-weight one evaluation set and say what that does to an
  ordering nobody thought was in question}
\outcome{16--19}{say what narrowing a reported interval costs, in items}
\outcome{20--28}{say what an imperfect judge does to a measured difference,
  and why its agreement figure does not tell you}
\outcome{29--35}{price a model per task rather than per token, and say which
  second quantity a per-token figure leaves out}
\outcome{36--43}{name three published measures that are computed correctly
  from the wrong quantity}
\outcome{44--47}{say which claims in this program have not been checked
  against a trained model, and why saying so is not a hedge}
\outcome{48--52}{name the three reasons a table in a book is blank, and say
  which of them is a debt}
\end{outcomes}

\begin{quiz}
\item Your evaluation set has $\val{p27.items}$ items and your model scores
  $\val{p27.near.hi}$ per cent. What is that a number about?
  \teachesat{1--2}
  \answerto{An estimate of the accuracy on the population of tasks you care
  about. The set is a sample and the score is an estimator, so
  Program~\ref{prog:P26}'s two axes and Program~\ref{prog:P27}'s interval
  both apply to it unchanged.}
\item Which half of a benchmark's error do more items reduce?
  \teachesat{3--5}
  \answerto{Only the sampling half. The distance between what the benchmark
  measures and the task you care about does not fall with the item count at
  all, and past about $\val{p34.mis.n}$ items it is the larger of the two.}
\item Two models are $\val{p27.gap.pts}$ points apart on one evaluation set.
  Both models are then held completely fixed and the set's mix of short and
  long prompts is changed by $\val{p34.mix.shift}$ items. What happens?
  \teachesat{11--14}
  \answerto{The ordering reverses. The scores become $\val{p34.mix.alt.b}$
  and $\val{p34.mix.alt.a}$, and one model's own score ranges over
  $\val{p34.mix.swing}$ points across the possible mixes.}
\item A judge model marks answers imperfectly. What does that do to the
  difference it reports between two models?
  \teachesat{21--23}
  \answerto{It multiplies it by one constant, the same for every pair. If
  the judge marks a correct answer right $\val{p34.judge.a}$ per cent of the
  time and a wrong one right $\val{p34.judge.b}$ per cent, every measured gap
  is $\val{p34.judge.atten}$ of the true one.}
\item Model B costs $\val{p34.bill.price.times}$ times as much per token as
  model A. Which is dearer?
  \teachesat{30--31}
  \answerto{Not answerable from that. A price per token is half a ratio: the
  bill is the price times the tokens a task takes, and at
  $\val{p34.bill.tok.b}$ tokens against $\val{p34.bill.tok.a}$ model B is
  $\val{p34.bill.saving.pct}$ per cent cheaper per task.}
\item A probe reports $\val{p34.probe.i}$ nats of mutual information between
  a layer and a label, on $50$ items. How much of that is the estimator?
  \teachesat{38--39}
  \answerto{$\val{p34.probe.share.pct}$ per cent of it.
  Program~\ref{prog:P31} measured the plug-in estimator returning
  $\val{p31.bias.n50}$ nats, in expectation, on data with no dependence at
  all.}
\end{quiz}

\section{An evaluation set is a sample}
\label{sec:P34-a-sample}
\index{evaluation!as an estimation problem}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:P27} left you holding a leaderboard: $\val{p27.items}$
items, the top model at $\val{p27.near.hi}$ per cent and the next at
$\val{p27.near.lo}$, and an exact test saying the gap between them is the
least informative outcome the arithmetic permits.

This program is about everything else on that page. Start with the score
itself. You ran a fixed set of items, you counted, and you divided. The
number is $\val{p27.near.hi}$ and it is exact arithmetic on what you ran.

So what, exactly, is it an \emph{estimate} of?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{The accuracy on the population of tasks you actually care about.}

Nobody reports a benchmark score because they care about those two hundred
items. They report it because the items are meant to stand in for a job.
That makes the set a \textbf{sample} and the score an \textbf{estimator},
and it puts the whole of Program~\ref{prog:P26} in the room.

Notice what calling it a sample costs. Nobody drew those two hundred items at
random from anything; somebody wrote them, for a purpose, on an afternoon.
Treating the set as a sample is therefore a modelling decision rather than an
observation \dash{} and it is the decision that licenses every formula this
program is about to borrow.

Program~\ref{prog:P26} split an estimator's error into two pieces and proved
the split exactly. Name them.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Bias squared, plus variance.}

Read on an evaluation set the two pieces have names an engineer already uses.

The \textbf{variance} is the sampling of items: run a different two hundred
and you would get a different number. Program~\ref{prog:P27} owns it
entirely, and it is what the interval on the report describes.

The \textbf{bias} is the distance between what the benchmark measures and
the job you are hiring the model for. Its items were written by somebody
else, for somebody else's traffic.

One of those two falls as you add items. Which?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Only the variance.}

The variance of a proportion is $p(1-p)/n$, so it falls as $1/n$ and can be
driven as low as your budget allows. The bias is a property of the
\emph{set}, not of its size: an item count of ten thousand written from the
same wrong assumption is wrong by exactly as much as two hundred.

Take a model whose true accuracy is $\num{0.715}$, on a benchmark
$\val{p34.mis.pts}$ points away from your task. At what item count do the two
contribute equally to the error in what you report?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Set the two equal and solve for $n$:
\[ \frac{p(1-p)}{n} = \text{bias}^{2}
   \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
   n = \frac{p(1-p)}{\text{bias}^{2}}
     = \frac{\num{0.715} \times \num{0.285}}{\num{0.02}^{2}} , \]
which first tips at $n = \val{p34.mis.n}$.
\end{ansblock}

That is not a large number. It is about two and a half times the set
Program~\ref{prog:P27} was arguing over.

Program~\ref{prog:P27}'s own leaderboard ran $\val{p27.lb.n}$ items. Which of
the two contributes more there, and by how much?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{The mis-specification, by a factor of about $\val{p34.mis.ratio}$.}

At a thousand items the benchmark's distance from your task contributes
roughly twice what the sampling does \dash{} and \textbf{only the sampling
half appears in the interval on the report}. The other half is not small, is
not reported, and does not move when you buy more items.

So the sentence this section exists for: \textbf{more items buy precision and
never buy validity.}

That leaves the question Program~\ref{prog:F08} handed here by name. If the
interval cannot tell you whether a score means anything on your data, what
can?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Only a comparison between the benchmark's items and your own traffic. It is
a reading job and a sampling job, not an arithmetic one: draw from what your
system actually receives, and ask whether the benchmark's items look like a
sample from it.
\end{ansblock}

That is worth saying plainly because it is the one part of an evaluation
that cannot be bought. Every other question in this program has a formula
underneath it. This one has a design decision underneath it, and a design
decision that nobody wrote down is a bias nobody can estimate.

\mermaidfig{p34-two-errors}{The two errors in a reported score, and the one
  the interval describes}{two errors, one interval}
\end{fr}

\section{The same items, weighed differently}
\label{sec:P34-remix}
\index{evaluation!the mix as a choice}

\begin{fr}
Now hold everything about the two models fixed, and change nothing about
what they answered. Only the \emph{set} is going to move.

Split Program~\ref{prog:P27}'s $\val{p27.items}$ items into two kinds:
$\val{p34.mix.short}$ short prompts and $\val{p34.mix.short}$ long ones. Every
benchmark has a split of this shape, whether or not it reports one.

Model B gets $\val{p34.mix.b.short}$ per cent of the short items right and
$\val{p34.mix.b.long}$ per cent of the long ones. Model A gets
$\val{p34.mix.a.short}$ and $\val{p34.mix.a.long}$ \dash{} better on the long
prompts, worse on the short. Score both on the whole set.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Half the items are of each kind, so each score is the plain average:
\[ B = \tfrac{1}{2}(\num{0.80}) + \tfrac{1}{2}(\num{0.63}) = \val{p27.near.hi}\%,
   \qquad
   A = \tfrac{1}{2}(\num{0.74}) + \tfrac{1}{2}(\num{0.68}) = \val{p27.near.lo}\% . \]
\end{ansblock}

Those are Program~\ref{prog:P27}'s two committed figures, to the digit. This
is the same leaderboard, with one thing about it written down that was there
all along.

The even split was a choice somebody made. Suppose they had weighted the
short prompts by $w$ instead. What happens to the gap?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{It moves in a straight line, because a score is a weighted average.}

Each model's score is linear in $w$, so the gap between them is too:
\[ B - A = (\num{0.63} - \num{0.68}) + w\bigl[(\num{0.80}-\num{0.74}) -
   (\num{0.63}-\num{0.68})\bigr] = -\num{0.05} + \num{0.11}\,w . \]

At $w = \frac{1}{2}$ that is $+\num{0.005}$, which is
Program~\ref{prog:P27}'s $\val{p27.gap.pts}$-point gap.

Where does it vanish?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{At $w = \frac{5}{11}$, which is $\val{p34.mix.tie.w}$ per cent short
  prompts.}

That is not a strange mixture. It is a set with a few more long prompts than
short ones, which is what you would get by writing the items on a different
afternoon.

The even split puts $\val{p34.mix.short}$ short items in the set. How many of
the $\val{p27.items}$ have to change kind before the leaderboard's top two
change places?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Ten. At $\val{p34.mix.shift}$ fewer short items the scores are
\[ B = \val{p34.mix.alt.b}\% , \qquad A = \val{p34.mix.alt.a}\% , \]
so A now leads by $\val{p34.mix.alt.gap}$ points.
\end{ansblock}

Neither model was retrained, retuned or re-prompted. Every answer either of
them gave is the answer it gave before. Ten items out of two hundred moved
from one bucket to the other, and the ordering the leaderboard reports is the
other way round.

\transcript{p34-remix}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The gap is the small quantity here, so it is worth asking how large the
\emph{other} one is.

Model B alone, over every mix from all-long to all-short: what is the range
of scores it can be reported as having?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{From $\val{p34.mix.b.long}$ to $\val{p34.mix.b.short}$ per cent \dash{}
  $\val{p34.mix.swing}$ points.}

Set that beside the two numbers the leaderboard is actually arguing about.
The gap under discussion is $\val{p27.gap.pts}$ points.
Program~\ref{prog:P27} computed that this evaluation set could not
distinguish the two models until the gap reached
$\val{p27.net.needed.pts}$ points.

\begin{trapbox}
One model's score moves $\val{p34.mix.swing.times}$ times further across the
possible mixes than the difference Program~\ref{prog:P27} says would be
meaningful.

The mix is not a hyperparameter, it is not in the interval, and on most
leaderboards it is not reported. A quantity that moves a score more than any
model difference does, and that nobody records, is the definition of an
uncontrolled variable.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
None of that is a criticism of the benchmark. A set of items \emph{has} to
have some mix; there is no neutral one, and refusing to choose is not
available.

What it means is narrower and it is Program~\ref{prog:F01}'s sentence
arriving. A score is not \enquote{how good the model is}. It is \emph{how
good on this mixture}, and the mixture is the second quantity. Report the
first without the second and you have quoted a ratio with one of its two
numbers missing \dash{} which is the shape this book has been naming since
its first program.

\mermaidfig{p34-same-items}{One evaluation set, two mixes, and both models
  untouched}{a weighted average moves with its weights}
\end{fr}

\section{What a narrower interval costs}
\label{sec:P34-interval}
\index{evaluation!the cost of precision}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:P27} committed one interval and this section prices it.
A leaderboard entry at $\val{p27.lb.p.pct}$ per cent on $\val{p27.lb.n}$
items has a standard error of $\val{p27.lb.se}$ points, and under that
program's own correction for reading forty entries the margin is
$\val{p27.lb.margin}$ points.

Suppose that is too wide to act on and you want half of it. How many items?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p34.halve.times}$ times as many \dash{} $\val{p34.halve.n}$.}

Precision goes as $1/\sqrt{n}$, so halving an interval is squaring the
factor. It is worth saying in the currency it is actually paid in: four
times the items to write, four times the items to label, and four times the
inference bill to score them.

Push it one step further. To state a single model's accuracy to within one
point, at the same correction, how many items?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
The count scales as the square of the margin it buys:
\[ n = \val{p27.lb.n} \times
   \left(\frac{\val{p27.lb.margin}}{\num{1.0}}\right)^{2}
   \approx \val{p34.one.pt.n} . \]
\end{ansblock}

Nearly ten thousand items, to say one number to within one point.

Program~\ref{prog:P27} computed a different count from the same machinery:
how many items are needed to \emph{tell two models apart} at one point, when
both are run on the same prompts. How do the two compare?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p27.n.rho00}$ against $\val{p34.one.pt.n}$ \dash{} a factor of
  $\val{p34.detect.ratio}$.}

Stating one model's accuracy to a point and separating two models by a point
cost about the same, and both are two orders of magnitude past the set in
front of you.

\begin{note}
That is the honest reading of an interval: it is a \textbf{budget
statement}. It says what the evaluation you have already paid for is able to
resolve, and the answer is usually \enquote{less than the difference being
discussed}. Nobody has ten thousand items per model, which is why almost
every published comparison is under-powered rather than wrong.
\end{note}
\end{fr}

\section{The judge, and what it does to a difference}
\label{sec:P34-judge}
\index{evaluation!judge model}

\begin{fr}
Ten thousand items is not a labelling budget anybody has, so the items get
marked by another model.

A judge is an instrument, and an instrument has two error rates rather than
one. Write
\begin{align*}
  a &= \Prob(\text{judge marks correct} \mid \text{actually correct}), \\
  b &= \Prob(\text{judge marks correct} \mid \text{actually wrong}).
\end{align*}

A model's true accuracy is $p$. What accuracy does the judge report for it?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$p\,a + (1-p)\,b$.}

The judge marks a fraction $a$ of the correct answers correct, and wrongly
marks a fraction $b$ of the incorrect ones correct. Both contribute.

Nobody reports one accuracy on its own, though. Two models with true
accuracies $p_{1}$ and $p_{2}$ go through the same judge. What does it
report for the \emph{difference} between them?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Subtract the two, and everything that does not carry a $p$ cancels:
\[ \bigl[p_{1}a + (1-p_{1})b\bigr] - \bigl[p_{2}a + (1-p_{2})b\bigr]
   = (a - b)(p_{1} - p_{2}) . \]
\end{ansblock}

That is exact, and two things about it are worth stopping on.

The reported gap is the true gap multiplied by \textbf{one constant}, so an
imperfect judge does not add noise to an effect size \dash{} it \emph{shrinks}
it, systematically, in one direction. And the constant $a - b$ \textbf{does
not depend on $p$ at all}, so the same shrinkage applies to a comparison at
the top of a leaderboard and one at the bottom.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Put numbers on it. A judge marks a correct answer correct
$\val{p34.judge.a}$ per cent of the time and a wrong answer correct
$\val{p34.judge.b}$ per cent, so $a - b = \val{p34.judge.atten}$. Two models
are truly $\val{p34.judge.true.gap}$ points apart, and the judge reports
$\val{p34.judge.seen.gap}$.

You are not going to see the true gap, so you have to detect the reported
one. What does the shrinkage do to the number of items you need?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{It multiplies it by $\val{p34.judge.items.times}$.}

The effect shrinks by $a - b$ and the sampling noise does not, so the ratio
of the two shrinks by $a - b$ as well; and Section~\ref{sec:P34-interval}'s
arithmetic says a count scales as the square of the resolution it buys. So
the item count is divided by $(a-b)^{2}$ \dash{} more than half again as many
items, for exactly the same power, bought by nothing but the marking.

That is not a small correction to a budget. It is also not on anybody's
report.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
So you would like to know $a - b$ for the judge you are using. What a judge's
card reports is \textbf{agreement} with human labels: the fraction of items
on which the judge and a person say the same thing, which for a model of
accuracy $p$ is
\[ \text{agreement} = p\,a + (1-p)(1-b) . \]

At this program's accuracy and these two rates that is
$\val{p34.judge.agree.pct}$ per cent, which is a respectable card.

Does knowing the agreement tell you the attenuation?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
No. Agreement pins the combination $p\,a - (1-p)\,b$ and nothing more, so at
$\val{p34.judge.agree.pct}$ per cent the card is consistent with every judge
whose attenuation lies in
\[ \val{p34.judge.atten.lo} \;\le\; a - b \;\le\;
   \val{p34.judge.atten.hi} . \]
\end{ansblock}

The two ends differ by enough to matter: through
$(a-b)^{2}$ they are $\val{p34.judge.spread.pct}$ per cent apart in the item
count they demand. Two judges with \emph{identical} agreement on the same
labelled sample, and half again the annotation budget between them.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
There is exactly one accuracy at which agreement does pin the attenuation,
and it is worth knowing which.

The two rates enter agreement with coefficients $p$ and $-(1-p)$, so they
balance \dash{} and the combination collapses to $a - b$ \dash{} only when
$p = \frac{1}{2}$. Then agreement determines the attenuation exactly, as
$2\times\text{agreement} - 1$.

So the single case in which a judge's card is sufficient is the case where
the model is right half the time, which is the one accuracy nobody is
reporting a leaderboard about.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:P28} priced a judge too, and it is worth being precise
about the difference, because the two corrections do not substitute for each
other.

That program takes the probability a judge \emph{states} \dash{} it says
$\val{p28.judge.says.pct}$ per cent and means $\val{p28.judge.means.pct}$
\dash{} and corrects it in odds. This section takes the judge's
\emph{verdict} and corrects what it does to a measured difference. One is a
statement about a number the judge reports about itself; the other is a
statement about the instrument's effect on your experiment.

Calibrating the first leaves the second exactly where it was.

\mermaidfig{p34-judge-shrinks}{An imperfect judge multiplies every effect
  size by one constant}{the verdict, not the stated probability}
\end{fr}

\section{The bill, which is a product}
\label{sec:P34-bill}
\index{cost!per task against per token}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:F01} opened this book by saying that almost every claim
you will read about a model \dash{} faster, cheaper, better \dash{} is a
ratio quoted without its two quantities, and it deferred the reading of such
claims to here.

Cost per token is that shape exactly. It is one quantity, and the thing
anybody actually pays is a product of two.

What is the other one?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{The number of tokens a task takes.}

\[ \text{cost per task} \;=\; \text{price per token} \times
   \text{tokens per task} . \]

Nobody buys tokens. They buy answered questions, resolved tickets, reviewed
diffs \dash{} and a model that says more to reach the same answer spends the
difference.

Model B costs $\val{p34.bill.price.times}$ times as much per token as model A
and averages $\val{p34.bill.tok.b}$ tokens per task against A's
$\val{p34.bill.tok.a}$. Which is dearer to run?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Multiply the two ratios:
\[ \frac{\text{B per task}}{\text{A per task}}
   = \val{p34.bill.price.times} \times \val{p34.bill.tok.times}
   = \val{p34.bill.task.times} , \]
so B is $\val{p34.bill.saving.pct}$ per cent \emph{cheaper} per task while
being half again as dear per token.
\end{ansblock}

The two ratios pull in opposite directions and only their product is billed.

At what verbosity would the answer flip \dash{} how many tokens per task
could B afford before A became the cheaper of the two?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p34.bill.breakeven}$ tokens.}

B has to be at least a third terser than A to pay for its price, and at
$\val{p34.bill.tok.b}$ tokens it is. Move it to
$\val{p34.bill.breakeven}$ and the two are exactly level; move it past and
the per-token figure becomes right again.

So neither number decides anything on its own, and the break-even is a
property of the pair rather than of either model.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
There is a third quantity behind the same two, and Program~\ref{prog:P03}
has it. Serving a conversation holds a cache of
$\val{p03.kv.per.token.mib}$ MiB per token in flight, so a task's length
costs memory as well as money \dash{} $\val{p34.bill.cache.a}$ MiB for A and
$\val{p34.bill.cache.b}$ for B.

Nothing about the price per token appears in that. What does it decide?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{How many conversations fit on one card at once \dash{} B serves
  $\val{p34.bill.conc.times}$ times as many.}

The cache is the same per token for both models, so it cancels out of the
ratio entirely and what is left is the task length. The terser model is
cheaper per task \emph{and} carries more concurrent users on the same
hardware, and the card that says it is
$\val{p34.bill.price.times}$ times the price mentions neither.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:P29} met this identity in a different currency and it is
worth putting the two side by side, because they behave differently.

There, bits per character is bits per token times tokens per character, and
\textbf{the model's contribution cancels}: the same model on the same text
reports $\val{p29.bpc.a}$ and $\val{p29.bpc.b}$ bits per character at
$\val{p29.tpc.a}$ and $\val{p29.tpc.b}$ tokens per character, a factor of
$\val{p29.bpc.ratio}$ that is a fact about the tokeniser and about nothing
else.

Here neither factor cancels. Both the price and the length belong to the
model, which is precisely why quoting one of them settles nothing.

  costs}{only the product is billed}
\end{fr}

\section{Measures that look like measurements}
\label{sec:P34-measures}
\index{evaluation!measures that mislead}

\begin{fr}
Three published quantities are computed correctly and answer a question
nobody asked. Two of them this book has already measured, and they are worth
collecting here because the shape they share is the point.

Program~\ref{prog:P29} took two models' losses converted to bits per
character. Under different tokenisers, what cancels out of that comparison?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{The model.}

Bits per character is bits per token times tokens per character, and the
second factor belongs to the tokeniser. Compare two systems that tokenise
differently and the difference you measure is the tokenisers'. Two losses
are comparable under one tokeniser and under no other condition, and stating
that condition costs a sentence.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:P31}'s is sharper, because the number is not merely
ambiguous \dash{} it is positive when the truth is zero.

A probe reports $\val{p34.probe.i}$ nats of mutual information between a
layer's activations and a label, on a table built from $50$ items. Before
reading anything into it: what does that estimator report on data with no
dependence whatever?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$\val{p31.bias.n50}$ nats \dash{} in expectation, exactly, at that item
count. Program~\ref{prog:P31} summed it over every table rather than
sampling it.
\end{ansblock}

So $\val{p34.probe.share.pct}$ per cent of the reported figure is the
estimator rather than the layer. The effect may well be real; a fifth of it
is not.

The bias falls as $1/N$. How many items before it is under a twentieth of
that reported figure?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{About $\val{p34.probe.n}$.}

Four times the items to reduce the artefact to a twentieth of the claim, on
a measurement that is routinely reported from a few dozen. And unlike the
sampling error, this one does not straddle the truth \dash{} it is in one
direction, so it never averages away across the layers of a paper.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:P31} carries the third as well, and it is one inequality
read at its two ends.

A probe scores badly on a layer. Does that mean the layer carries little
about the label?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{No. A probe's score is a lower bound, and a loose one.}

Program~\ref{prog:P31} measured the best channel in a family reaching
$\val{p31.dpi.best}$ nats against the $\val{p31.dpi.ixy}$ that were there,
leaving more than a quarter behind. The same inequality read the other way
says a \emph{better} probe cannot mean more information, because the ceiling
was fixed before anybody trained one.

So one number is routinely over-read in both directions, by the same people,
in the same paper.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Three measures, three different ways of not being a measurement of what they
are quoted for, and one shape between them: \textbf{a quantity computed
correctly from the wrong thing.}

That is Program~\ref{prog:P33}'s four silent failures arriving one level up.
There, a training step could compute a mean of means or a rounded sum and
raise nothing, because a mean of means is a mean. Here a benchmark can
report bits per character, or a probe's plug-in estimate, or an accuracy on
an unrecorded mix, and every one of them is arithmetic nobody could fault.

A wrong answer announces itself. A right answer to the wrong question does
not, and that is what makes the design the whole of the job.
\end{fr}

\section{What this book has not checked}
\label{sec:P34-not-checked}
\index{measurement!what is not claimed}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:P17} deferred one claim here by name: whether a flat
minimum generalises better than a sharp one. It is the most repeated
empirical claim in optimisation and this book has said nothing about it.

Ask Program~\ref{prog:P32}'s question of it. Which half of settling that
needs a trained model, and which half is a statement about the design?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
All of it needs trained models, and that is the unusual answer.

The claim compares two \emph{trained} minima, so there is nothing to derive:
you need many models, a definition of flatness that survives
Program~\ref{prog:P17}'s rescaling, and enough runs per condition to beat the
variance Program~\ref{prog:P27} would demand.
\end{ansblock}

So it is stated as outstanding rather than hedged. Programs~\ref{prog:P08},
\ref{prog:P11}, \ref{prog:P19}, \ref{prog:P20}, \ref{prog:P25},
\ref{prog:P31} and \ref{prog:P32} each carry one of these, and each says what
would settle it.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The same question is owed by this program about its own three findings. Which
of the re-mix, the judge's attenuation and the bill needs a trained model?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{None of them.}

Each is a statement about a \emph{design} rather than about a model. The
re-mix is arithmetic on a weighted average; the attenuation is an identity
in two conditional probabilities; the bill is a product of two ratios. Every
one is exact and none of them would be more true with a model behind it.

\begin{warning}
What \emph{would} need one is the empirical half: whether real evaluation
sets have mixes as influential as the constructed one in
Section~\ref{sec:P34-remix}, and how far apart real judges' attenuations sit
at equal agreement.

Both are measurable and neither is measured here. What would settle the
first is a published benchmark with its items categorised and re-weighted;
the second needs a labelled sample scored by two judges.
\end{warning}
\end{fr}

\section{The standard}
\label{sec:P34-standard}
\index{measurement!the standard a number has to meet}

\begin{fr}
This book's two companion volumes both carry a table with nothing in it, and
so does this one. That is deliberate in all three, and it is not one
decision repeated.

Before reading on: a table in a technical book is blank. Name the reasons it
might be, and say which of them is a debt somebody owes you.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Three, and only the first is a debt.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The experiment has not been run.} A debt \dash{} and the book
  owes you what would settle it and what it costs.
\item \textbf{The number is a property of your machine.} Not a debt. A table
  filled in by somebody else would be actively misleading, and what belongs
  there is the method.
\item \textbf{It was measured and is marked indicative}, because it moves
  with a choice nobody standardised.
\end{enumerate}
\end{ansblock}

The companion volumes carry one of each. The Agent Framework volume's
provider table is the second: its own note says the numbers depend on your
hardware and your region, so the methodology is fixed and the numbers are
yours. The LangChain volume prints the first and the third on one page
\dash{} a filled table whose context column is headed \emph{indicative}
beside three blank rows saying that filling them with plausible numbers
would be worse than leaving them blank.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
It is worth being careful with those two books rather than merging them,
because they both happen to print a figure of $\num{2.8}$ times and the
figures are not the same measurement.

One is a context multiple that was measured on scripted replies and marked
indicative for exactly that reason. The other is a token ratio the second
book \emph{refuses to state} without measuring it, saying that a book
claiming it unmeasured would be worse than one admitting it has not.

Neither treats the number as established, which is the only thing the two
have in common. Two books, one figure, two entirely different reasons for
distrusting it \dash{} and reading them as agreement would be this
program's own Section~\ref{sec:P34-measures} defect committed on its own
sources.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
So here is the standard both companion volumes are built on, with the
mathematics of this book behind it at last.

\begin{center}
\emph{A claim needs a method and a number, or it is labelled judgement.}
\end{center}

Every word of that has now been paid for. The \textbf{number} is an estimate,
so Program~\ref{prog:P26} says what its two errors are and
Program~\ref{prog:P27} says how wide it is. The \textbf{method} is the design
\dash{} which population, which mix, which instrument, which denominator
\dash{} and this program has shown that each of those four moves a reported
figure further than the differences people argue about. And
\textbf{judgement} is the honest label for a claim with neither, which is
what Program~\ref{prog:F04} meant by making this the standard a reported
number has to meet.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
That is the end of the mathematics.

You started at Program~\ref{prog:F01} working out what a model weighs, with
no vocabulary for where its parameters were. Program~\ref{prog:P32} derived
that count from the shapes; Program~\ref{prog:P33} read the curve that
training it draws; and this program says what may be claimed about the
result.

The book has argued throughout that the mathematics is not the hard part of
this job and is not optional either \dash{} that the difference between an
engineer who can check a claim and one who repeats it is a few hundred pages
of exactly this. You now have those pages.

What is left is the habit, and it is one question asked of every number
anybody hands you, your own included: \textbf{what was measured, and how
would I know if it were wrong?}
\end{fr}

  Program~\ref{prog:P26}'s two errors both apply: the sampling of items, and
  the distance between the benchmark and your task}.}
\sumitem{4--6}{\result{Only the first falls with the item count. Past about
  $\val{p34.mis.n}$ items the second is larger, and it is not in the

  $\val{p34.mix.shift}$ items out of $\val{p27.items}$ reverses the ordering

\sumitem{17--18}{\result{Precision goes as $1/\sqrt{n}$, so halving an interval is

\begin{testexercises}
\item A benchmark is $3$ points away from your task and your model scores
  $\num{0.60}$ on it. At what item count does the mis-specification
  contribute as much as the sampling?
  \answerto{About $267$. The crossover is $p(1-p)/\text{bias}^{2} =
  \num{0.60} \times \num{0.40} / \num{0.03}^{2}$, and a larger bias moves it
  down: the worse the benchmark fits your task, the sooner more items stop
  helping.}
\item Two models score $\num{0.70}$ and $\num{0.66}$ on the short half of a
  set and $\num{0.62}$ and $\num{0.70}$ on the long half. At what mix do they
  tie?
  \answerto{At $\frac{2}{3}$ short. The gap is
  $(\num{0.62}-\num{0.70}) + w[(\num{0.70}-\num{0.66}) -
  (\num{0.62}-\num{0.70})] = -\num{0.08} + \num{0.12}\,w$, which vanishes at
  $w = \frac{2}{3}$.}
\item Your interval is $\pm 4$ points on $500$ items. How many items for
  $\pm 1$?
  \answerto{$8000$. The count scales as the square of the margin ratio, so
  $500 \times 4^{2}$ \dash{} sixteen times the items for four times the
  resolution.}
\item A judge marks a correct answer right $\num{0.90}$ of the time and a
  wrong one right $\num{0.20}$. Two models are truly $6$ points apart. What
  is reported, and by what factor does your item budget grow?
  \answerto{$\num{4.2}$ points, and the budget grows by
  $1/\num{0.70}^{2} \approx \num{2.04}$ \dash{} the attenuation is
  $a - b = \num{0.70}$ and the count goes as its square.}
\item Model C is $\num{0.80}$ of model D's price per token and uses
  $\num{1.30}$ times as many tokens per task. Which is cheaper per task?
  \answerto{D, by about $4$ per cent: $\num{0.80} \times \num{1.30} =
  \num{1.04}$. The cheaper token loses to the longer answer, which is why
  neither figure decides on its own.}
\item A table in a book is blank and the book says the number depends on your
  hardware. Is that a debt?
  \answerto{No. It is the second of the three reasons: a number that is a
  property of the reader's machine cannot be supplied, and a table filled in
  by somebody else would be actively misleading. What is owed there is the
  method, not the figure.}
\end{testexercises}

\begin{furtherproblems}
\item Show that if a benchmark's bias is $\beta$ and its items are drawn from
  a fixed population, no item count makes the reported figure's mean squared
  error smaller than $\beta^{2}$.
  \answerto{The error is $\beta^{2} + p(1-p)/n$ and the second term is
  strictly positive for every finite $n$, so the total is strictly above
  $\beta^{2}$ and tends to it from above. The floor is set by the set and not
  by its size.}
\item A set has $k$ kinds of item rather than two. Show that the score is
  still linear in the weights, and say what replaces the single tie point.
  \answerto{The score is $\sum_{i} w_{i} s_{i}$ with $\sum_{i} w_{i} = 1$, so
  the gap is $\sum_{i} w_{i}(s_{i} - t_{i})$ \dash{} linear in $w$. The tie
  set is the intersection of that hyperplane with the simplex, so with more
  than two kinds the ties are a whole face rather than a point, and there are
  generally many mixes that reverse an ordering.}
\item Two judges have attenuations $a_{1}-b_{1}$ and $a_{2}-b_{2}$ and one
  scores the other's output. What is the attenuation of the pair?
  \answerto{The product. Each stage multiplies the surviving effect by its
  own constant, so chaining judges compounds the shrinkage geometrically
  \dash{} which is why an automated pipeline that marks with one model and
  filters with another needs more items than either stage suggests.}
\item Model E is $r$ times model F's price per token. Show that the set of
  verbosity ratios at which E is cheaper per task is an interval, and say
  what its endpoint is.
  \answerto{E is cheaper when $r \times (\text{tokens}_{E}/\text{tokens}_{F})
  < 1$, so the ratio must lie in $\intco{0}{1/r}$. The endpoint is $1/r$
  exactly: a model twice the price has to be at least twice as terse, and no
  amount of quality changes that arithmetic.}
\item Program~\ref{prog:P31}'s plug-in bias falls as $1/N$ and a reported
  effect does not fall at all. Show that the item count at which the bias is
  a fixed fraction $f$ of the effect $I$ is inversely proportional to both.
  \answerto{The bias is about $c/N$ for a constant $c$ fixed by the table's
  shape, so $c/N \le fI$ gives $N \ge c/(fI)$. Halving the effect you are
  claiming, or halving the fraction you will tolerate, each doubles the items
  \dash{} so the smaller the finding, the more expensive it is to establish.}
\end{furtherproblems}
