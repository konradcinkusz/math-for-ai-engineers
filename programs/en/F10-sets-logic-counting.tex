\section{What a set throws away}
\label{sec:F10-sets}
\index{set!membership and operations}

\begin{fr}
A \textbf{set} is a collection of things where the only question you may ask is
\emph{is this one in it?}

That question is called \textbf{membership} and it is the whole of the
definition. Everything else about sets is a consequence of the two things they
therefore cannot record.

You have used them without the word. The vocabulary of a tokeniser is a set:
each token is in it or it is not, there is no second copy of a token, and
asking which token is \enquote{third} is a question about the file that
happened to define it rather than about the vocabulary.

Here is a small corpus: $\val{f10.lines}$ lines of log output, made of
$\val{f10.tokens}$ words in total. How many words are in its vocabulary?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
You cannot tell from those two numbers.
\end{ansblock}

Which is the point of asking. $\val{f10.tokens}$ counts every word that went
past, and the vocabulary counts the distinct ones, and nothing about the first
number tells you the second.

For this corpus it is $\val{f10.vocab}$, so each word appears about
$\val{f10.repeat}$ times on average. Log output repeats itself heavily, which
is why the gap here is so wide.

What is the code difference between the two counts?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
One call: \code{len(tokens)} against \code{len(set(tokens))}.
\end{ansblock}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{A list and a set are one function call apart and answer different
questions}, which is why the two get confused in exactly the places it
matters.

\enquote{We trained on ten million tokens} and \enquote{our vocabulary is ten
million} are wildly different statements, and here the two numbers differ by a
factor of $\val{f10.repeat}$. Report the wrong one and you have described a
different system.

The habit that prevents it: when a count appears in a claim, ask whether it
counted \emph{occurrences} or \emph{distinct things}, and say which in the
sentence. Neither number is more correct; they answer different questions and
the words for them are not interchangeable.
\end{trapbox}

So the first thing a set discards is repeats. What is the second?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Order}
$\{1, 2, 3\}$ and $\{3, 2, 1\}$ are the same set, because every membership
question has the same answer for both. Reverse the corpus above and every list
in it changes while the vocabulary does not \dash{} checked in
\code{code/f10\_sets.py}.

That is worth knowing precisely, because it says what a set is a bad model of.
A sequence of tokens is not a set: order is the whole content of a sentence. A
vocabulary is a set: order is an artefact of whichever file wrote it down.

\begin{aibox}
This is why a tokeniser is usefully described as a \emph{function} from text
into a vocabulary. The vocabulary is a set, so it has no first or last member;
the token \emph{ids} are a separate thing, an arbitrary numbering chosen by
whoever built it, and two tokenisers with identical vocabularies can number
them differently.

The consequence is one people hit: token ids are not portable between models,
and an id that survives a change of tokeniser survives by coincidence. What
is portable is the string, because the string is what membership was ever
about.
\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Notation, which is short and worth having.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
$x \in A$ & $x$ is in $A$ \\
$x \notin A$ & $x$ is not in $A$ \\
$\lvert A \rvert$ & how many things are in $A$ \\
$\emptyset$ & the set with nothing in it \\
$A \subseteq B$ & everything in $A$ is also in $B$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The last one is worth a moment. $A \subseteq B$ says nothing about whether $B$
has anything $A$ does not, so every set is a subset of itself.

The corpus above has $\val{f10.lines}$ lines and $\val{f10.distinct.lines}$
distinct ones. Which of those two numbers is a set size?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{f10.distinct.lines}$, the distinct count}
The other is an occurrence count, and the same three questions apply at any
grain. \enquote{How many log lines
did we see} and \enquote{how many kinds of log line are there} are the
occurrence count and the set size again, and the second is the one that tells
you how many distinct things your system does.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
One more piece of vocabulary before the operations.

Two sets are \textbf{disjoint} when nothing is in both. That is worth a name
because it is the special case where nearly everything about counting gets
easier, and the next section's union rule turns on it.
\end{fr}

\section{Union, intersection, and the mask}
\label{sec:F10-operations}
\index{set!union and intersection}

\begin{fr}
Three operations, and each is defined by what it does to the membership
question.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
& written & $x$ is in it when \\
\midrule
union & $A \cup B$ & $x$ is in $A$ \emph{or} in $B$ \\
intersection & $A \cap B$ & $x$ is in $A$ \emph{and} in $B$ \\
difference & $A \setminus B$ & $x$ is in $A$ \emph{and not} in $B$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Read the right-hand column: \textbf{each operation is one word of ordinary
logic}, which section 3 makes the point of.

One property of all three is worth having before you use them. Each takes two
sets and returns a set, so they compose without any special cases:
$A \cap (B \cup C)$ is a set for the same reason $A \cup B$ is, and you can go
on building without checking at each step that the result is still the kind of
thing the next operation accepts.

Two evaluation sets. The first has $\val{f10.eval.a}$ examples, the second has
$\val{f10.eval.b}$. How many examples are there altogether?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Not enough information: it depends on how many are in both.
\end{ansblock}

Adding them gives $\val{f10.eval.naive}$, and that is right only if the two
are disjoint. Here $\val{f10.eval.shared}$ examples are in both, so the true
size of the union is $\val{f10.eval.union}$.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Adding two set sizes counts the overlap twice.} The rule that fixes it
is the only one in this program that needs writing down:
\[ \lvert A \cup B \rvert = \lvert A \rvert + \lvert B \rvert
   - \lvert A \cap B \rvert \]
subtracting the shared part once, because it was counted once too often. The
script checks it on sixteen hundred pairs.

What makes this expensive is the shape of the error rather than its size.
Reporting $\val{f10.eval.naive}$ examples when there are $\val{f10.eval.union}$
overstates coverage by $\val{f10.eval.overcount}$ \dash{} and every one of the
$\val{f10.eval.overcount}$ is an example the model was scored on twice, so it
also weights those examples double in whatever average comes out. The count is
wrong and the score is wrong, and only the count is visible.

Program~\ref{prog:P12} generalises this to three sets and more, where it is
called inclusion--exclusion and the signs alternate. Two sets is the case you
will meet daily and it is this one line.
\end{trapbox}

\mermaidfig{f10-overlap}{Adding two counts counts the shared part twice, so it
comes off once.}{why two set sizes do not add}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
How many of the $\val{f10.eval.a}$ examples in the first set are \emph{not} in
the second?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{f10.eval.onlya}$}
From $\val{f10.eval.a} - \val{f10.eval.shared}$, which is the difference
$A \setminus B$. And notice that the difference is not symmetric: $B$ has
$\val{f10.eval.b} - \val{f10.eval.shared}$ examples the first set lacks, which
is a different number.

Now the representation you actually use in code. Instead of holding a set of
things, hold a \textbf{mask}: one yes-or-no per position, in the same order as
the data.

A set of positions and a mask over those positions carry the same information.
What is the mask's advantage?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
It lines up with the data, so it can be applied to it in one operation.
\end{ansblock}

A set of indices tells you which rows you want and then you have to go and get
them. A mask is already the same shape as the data, so the machine can walk
both together, which is what array libraries are built to do.

The correspondence is exact, and it is what makes the vocabulary of this
program useful in code:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
On sets & On masks & In a sentence \\
\midrule
$A \cup B$ & \code{a | b} & in either \\
$A \cap B$ & \code{a \& b} & in both \\
$A \setminus B$ & \code{a \& \textasciitilde b} & in the first and not the second \\
$\lvert A \rvert$ & \code{a.sum()} & how many \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The last row is the one worth pausing on: \textbf{counting a mask is adding
it up}, because a yes is a one and a no is a zero. Every count in the rest of
this program could have been written as a sum, which is
Program~\ref{prog:F04}'s sigma doing set theory.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Which of these two is a set, and which is a mask?

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
$\{2, 5, 6\}$ & \code{[0, 0, 1, 0, 0, 1, 1, 0]} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
The first is a set of positions; the second is a mask over eight positions.
They say the same thing.
\end{ansblock}

Positions $2$, $5$ and $6$ are the ones marked, counting from zero. The mask
knows how long the data is and the set does not, which is the one piece of
information they do not share \dash{} and it is why an empty mask still has a
length and the empty set does not.
\mermaidfig{f10-set-and-mask}{The same information twice, and every operation
on one has a twin on the other.}{a set and a mask are one thing}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
One consequence worth having before the next section.

\code{a.sum()} counts a mask, and \code{a.mean()} is that count divided by how
many positions there are. So a mask's mean is \emph{the fraction of the data
it selects}, and reading a mean of a boolean array as a fraction is not an
approximation \dash{} it is what the arithmetic does.

That is the cheapest way there is to check a filter: apply it and look at the
mean. A filter you expected to drop a few per cent and which reports
$\num{0.4}$ has not done what you meant, and it says so before you have looked
at a single row.
\end{fr}

\section{And, or, not}
\label{sec:F10-logic}
\index{set!and the logic of a filter}

\begin{fr}
The last section's right-hand column was three words of logic, so this section
is about those words on their own.

Two conditions, $p$ and $q$. \textbf{And} is true when both are, \textbf{or} is
true when at least one is, and \textbf{not} swaps true and false. Written out
there are four cases and no subtlety in any of them.

The subtlety is entirely in \emph{or}, and it is a fact about English rather
than about logic. \enquote{Tea or coffee} in a sentence usually means one or
the other and not both.

A filter keeps rows where \code{is\_long or is\_flagged}. A row is both long
and flagged. Is it kept?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Yes}
\enquote{$p$ or $q$} in logic and in every programming language you will use
means \textbf{at least one, possibly both}, and this row has two. Wanting
exactly one is a different operation \dash{} \emph{exclusive or} \dash{} and
the three words in this section do not have it, so it has to be built out of
them.

That ambiguity is worth one more sentence, because it is where a
specification and its implementation part company. A requirement written in
English \dash{} \enquote{reject requests that are oversized or malformed}
\dash{} has to be read twice: once as the writer meant it and once as the
compiler will take it. They agree here and they do not always, and the place
they disagree is a requirement nobody thought was ambiguous.

Now the operation this section exists for. Negate \enquote{spam \textbf{and}
short}.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
\enquote{not spam \textbf{or} not short}
\end{ansblock}

\textbf{The \emph{and} becomes an \emph{or}}, and that is the half people drop.

The reason is worth one sentence rather than a rule to memorise. To fail
\enquote{both} you only have to fail \emph{one} of them, so failing the
conjunction is a much weaker requirement than failing each part \dash{} and
\emph{or} is the weaker word.

The same argument the other way round gives the twin:
\[ \lnot(p \land q) = \lnot p \lor \lnot q \qquad
   \lnot(p \lor q) = \lnot p \land \lnot q \]
which are \textbf{De Morgan's laws}, checked in the script on all four
assignments.

Now the version that costs money. A filter is meant to keep everything that is
not both spam and short. Somebody writes \code{not spam and not short}. What
does it actually keep?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Only the rows that are neither spam nor short.
\end{ansblock}

Which is a much smaller set. On $\val{f10.rec.total}$ records with every
combination equally represented, the correct filter keeps
$\val{f10.rec.right}$ and the wrong one keeps $\val{f10.rec.wrong}$ \dash{}
$\val{f10.rec.lost}$ records silently discarded.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{The wrong negation of a conjunction always keeps too little, never too
much}, and that is what makes it hard to notice.

A filter that keeps too much shows you rows you did not expect, and you go and
look. A filter that keeps too little shows you nothing at all: the rows it
dropped are not in front of you, the pipeline runs, the numbers come out, and
the only symptom is a dataset smaller than it should be \dash{} which is
exactly the symptom everybody attributes to the data.

Two habits, both cheap. Take the mean of the mask and compare it with what you
expected, as the previous section said. And when you negate a compound
condition, write the negation out rather than putting a \code{not} in front of
the whole thing and distributing it by eye.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
One more, because it is the same law wearing different clothes and it catches
people who know De Morgan perfectly well.

A query keeps documents that are
\begin{center}\code{recent and (english or german)}\end{center}
and you want its complement. Write it.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
\code{not recent or (not english and not german)}
\end{ansblock}

Two applications of the law, outside in: the \code{and} becomes an \code{or},
and then the inner \code{or} becomes an \code{and} under its own negation. The
brackets have to stay, and where they go changes the answer.

That is the whole of the logic this program needs. What a claim of the form
\emph{if $p$ then $q$} does and does not license, and what changes when you
swap two quantifiers, are a different subject and belong to
Program~\ref{prog:P14}, which is where reading a theorem gets its own program.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
A last observation tying the two sections together.

Every filter you write is a set: the set of rows it keeps. Every combination
of filters is a set operation. And every question about \enquote{how many rows
survive} is a question about the size of that set, which is where the next
section starts.

\textbf{So the operations of this section and the last are the same
operations}, and whether you call them set operations or boolean operations is
a question about which representation is in front of you.
\end{fr}

\section{Counting without listing}
\label{sec:F10-counting}
\index{counting!without listing}

\begin{fr}
Everything so far has counted things by having them. This section counts
things nobody could list.

The first rule is the one you already use. There are $\val{f10.grid.a}$
models, $\val{f10.grid.b}$ precisions and $\val{f10.grid.c}$ batch sizes to
try, and every combination is allowed. How many runs is that?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{f10.grid}$}
From $\val{f10.grid.a} \times \val{f10.grid.b} \times \val{f10.grid.c}$.

This is the \textbf{product rule}: \textbf{independent choices multiply}. It is
worth stating with its condition attached, which is
Program~\ref{prog:F06}'s habit generalised: that program made a rule of
stating the condition every time you divide by a letter, and this is the same
move on a different operation. \emph{Independent} means every choice of the
first leaves the same options open for the second. Drop that and
the multiplication is wrong.

Suppose one of the models cannot run at the lowest precision. How many runs
is the grid now?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{f10.grid.less.one}$}
Not $59$, which is the answer the arithmetic invites. What the constraint
removes is not a run but a \emph{pair}: that model at that precision is gone
at every one of the $\val{f10.grid.c}$ batch sizes, so $\val{f10.grid.c}$
runs go with it. The choices are no longer independent, since which model you
pick changes which precisions are open to you, and the thing the constraint
names is not the thing you are counting.

That is the whole content of the condition, and it is why a grid search over a
constrained configuration cannot be sized by multiplying.

Notice where that constraint usually lives. It is rarely written down beside
the grid; it is enforced somewhere further in, as a validation error or a
crash, so the count is wrong from the start and the discovery arrives runs
later. Counting the exclusions when you write the grid down is a minute's work
and is the only point at which the number is cheap to get right.

Now the second rule. A set has $3$ elements. How many subsets does it have,
counting the empty set and the whole thing?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$8$}
And the reason is the product rule again, applied to a choice you might not
have noticed you were making. To build a subset, go through the elements one
at a time and decide \emph{in or out}. That is $3$ independent yes-or-no
choices, so $2 \times 2 \times 2$.

For $n$ elements it is \textbf{$2^{n}$ subsets}, one factor of $2$ per
element.

A set of $\val{f10.features}$ features, then. How many subsets?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{f10.subsets}$}
Which is $2^{\val{f10.features}}$, and is more than a million.

Now add one more feature. How many?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{f10.subsets.one.more}$}
It doubles, because every existing subset now has two versions \dash{} one
taking the new element and one not.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{Just try all the combinations} is a plan whose cost you have
to compute before you propose it}, and doubling per feature is not a rate
anybody's intuition handles.

$\val{f10.features}$ features give $\val{f10.subsets}$ subsets, which a
computer will do. Forty features give more than a million million. The step
from one to the other is twenty more yes-or-no choices, which does not
\emph{feel} like the difference between a coffee break and a geological era.

The habit: when a plan enumerates combinations, write down the count before
writing the loop. It is one multiplication and it is the difference between a
plan and a wish.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The third growth is the one this book will keep meeting, and it comes from
counting \emph{pairs} rather than subsets.

From $\val{f10.docs}$ documents, how many pairs can you form? Each pair is two
different documents, and a pair is the same pair whichever order you name it
in.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{f10.pairs}$}
Reasoned in two steps. Each of the $\val{f10.docs}$ documents can be paired
with each of the other $\val{f10.docs} - 1$, which is the product rule and
gives $\val{f10.docs} \times (\val{f10.docs} - 1)$. But that counts every pair
twice, once from each end, so halve it.

\[ \text{pairs} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2} \]

The \enquote{halve it because order does not matter} step is a set fact:
$\{a, b\}$ and $\{b, a\}$ are one pair, because a set discards order. That is
the second of section 1's two discards, doing arithmetic.

\mermaidfig{f10-three-growths}{Three ways a count grows, and each is the
product rule with a different independent choice in it.}{multiply, double,
square}
\end{fr}

\section{When a count becomes a bill}
\label{sec:F10-cost}
\index{counting!as a cost}

\begin{fr}
A count stops being a curiosity as soon as something has to be done once per
thing counted.

Deduplicating a corpus by comparing every document with every other one does
one comparison per pair. At $\val{f10.docs}$ documents that is
$\val{f10.pairs}$ comparisons, which is fine.

The corpus doubles to $\val{f10.docs2}$ documents. How many comparisons now?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{f10.pairs2}$}
A factor of $\val{f10.pairs.ratio}$ \dash{} \textbf{a shade over four, for
twice the input}.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Twice the data is four times the work as soon as the work is per
pair}, and the factor is a shade over four rather than exactly four:
$\frac{4n-2}{n-1}$, which is $\num{4.020}$ at a hundred documents and
$\val{f10.pairs.ratio}$ at $\val{f10.docs}$, falling towards four as the
corpus grows.

That precision is not pedantry, it is the reason the figure was computed
rather than reasoned about. The script asserted \enquote{just under four}
before this frame was written and \textbf{failed on the first run}, because
the ratio approaches four from above and not below. A frame quoting
\enquote{four times} flatly would have been wrong in the direction nobody
checks.

The consequence is the one to carry: a nested loop over a collection is
counting pairs, so its cost is governed by that formula whatever the loop
looks like. The pipeline that ran overnight on last month's corpus does not
run overnight on twice the corpus \dash{} it runs for four nights.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
That growth has a name and a notation, and this program deliberately stops
before them.

\enquote{The cost grows like $n^{2}$} is a statement about how a quantity
behaves as $n$ gets large, and it needs its own definition, its own warnings
about what it does \emph{not} say, and a worked account of why the faster of
two implementations today can be the slower one at scale.
Program~\ref{prog:P03} does all three.

What you have from here is the count itself, which is the thing the notation
is about. Being able to say \emph{this loop does $\val{f10.pairs}$
comparisons} is more useful in a design review than being able to say
\emph{this loop is quadratic}, and it is available to you now.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The last use of counting in this program is the one the rest of the book
depends on most, and it takes two numbers rather than one.

Of $\val{f10.docs}$ requests, $\val{f10.faults}$ failed. Write down the
fraction that failed.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\dfrac{\val{f10.faults}}{\val{f10.docs}}$}
A count on top and a count underneath. \textbf{Every naive probability is two
counts and a division}, and the counting is the part this program has been
about.

The hard part is not the division. It is choosing what goes underneath \dash{}
requests? users? sessions? requests that got as far as the model? \dash{} and
each choice gives a different number, all of them correct answers to different
questions.

\begin{aibox}
This is why an accuracy figure with no denominator stated is not a
measurement. \enquote{Ninety-nine per cent accurate} is a fraction whose
numerator and denominator have both been left out, and the two most common
readings of it \dash{} per request and per session \dash{} can differ by
orders of magnitude on the same system.

The discipline this book keeps recommending is one line: \textbf{when you
report a rate, report the two counts it came from.} They are what somebody
else needs to check it, and they are usually easier to obtain than the rate.
\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
What probability \emph{is} \dash{} a measure obeying three rules, what
conditioning does to it, and how Bayes' theorem falls out of the definition in
one line \dash{} is Program~\ref{prog:P23}, and it is deliberately not here.

The reason is the one this book gives whenever it draws a line like this. A
fraction of counts is a naive probability, and it is right exactly when every
outcome is equally likely and you have counted the right things. Both of those
conditions need saying carefully, and saying them carefully needs a program.

What is genuinely yours after this one is the numerator and the denominator,
and the knowledge that choosing the denominator was a decision.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
One closing observation, which is what this program has really been about.

Set, mask, count. A set says which things are in; a mask says the same thing
lined up with the data; a count is the mask added up. Three representations,
one idea, and the operations on each are the operations on the others.

\textbf{Almost everything an engineer does to a dataset is one of those three
seen from a different side} \dash{} filtering is intersection, deduplicating
is taking a set, and sizing a job is counting a set nobody will list. None of
it needed a new idea, and all of it needed the vocabulary to say which one you
meant.
\end{fr}

\begin{summarybox}
\sumitem{1--3}{\result{\textbf{A set answers one question: is this one in it?} So it
  discards repeats, and \code{len(tokens)} against \code{len(set(tokens))} is
  the difference between a corpus and a vocabulary \dash{} $\val{f10.tokens}$
  against $\val{f10.vocab}$ here, a factor of $\val{f10.repeat}$. When a count
  appears in a claim, say whether it counted occurrences or distinct things}.}
\sumitem{4--7}{\result{It discards \textbf{order} too, so a sequence of tokens is a bad
  fit for a set and a vocabulary is a good one \dash{} and token \emph{ids} are
  a separate, arbitrary numbering that does not travel between tokenisers.
  $\in$, $\lvert A \rvert$, $\emptyset$, $\subseteq$; two sets are
  \emph{disjoint} when nothing is in both}.}
\sumitem{8--9}{\result{$A \cup B$, $A \cap B$ and $A \setminus B$ are \emph{or},
  \emph{and} and \emph{and not}. \textbf{Adding two set sizes counts the
  overlap twice}: $\lvert A \cup B \rvert = \lvert A \rvert + \lvert B \rvert -
  \lvert A \cap B \rvert$. Two eval sets of $\val{f10.eval.a}$ and
  $\val{f10.eval.b}$ sharing $\val{f10.eval.shared}$ hold
  $\val{f10.eval.union}$ examples, not $\val{f10.eval.naive}$ \dash{} and the
  $\val{f10.eval.overcount}$ counted twice are also scored twice}.}
\sumitem{10--15}{\result{\textbf{A mask is the same information lined up with the
  data}: \code{|} is union, \code{\&} is intersection, \code{\& \textasciitilde}
  is difference, and \code{.sum()} is the size, because a yes is a one. So
  \code{.mean()} on a mask is the fraction it selects, and reading it is the
  cheapest check a filter has}.}
\sumitem{16--18}{\result{\emph{Or} means \textbf{at least one, possibly both}, in logic
  and in every language you will use, whatever it means in English. And
  \textbf{negating \emph{and} gives \emph{or}}:
  $\lnot(p \land q) = \lnot p \lor \lnot q$, because failing \emph{both} only
  requires failing one}.}
\sumitem{19--22}{\result{\textbf{The wrong negation of a conjunction always keeps too
  little}, which is why it is not noticed: of $\val{f10.rec.total}$ records the
  right filter keeps $\val{f10.rec.right}$ and \code{not spam and not short}
  keeps $\val{f10.rec.wrong}$, dropping $\val{f10.rec.lost}$ with no symptom
  but a small dataset. Every filter is a set and every combination of filters
  is a set operation}.}
\sumitem{23--24}{\result{\textbf{Independent choices multiply}, and \emph{independent}
  is a condition to state rather than assume: $\val{f10.grid.a}$ models times
  $\val{f10.grid.b}$ precisions times $\val{f10.grid.c}$ batch sizes is
  $\val{f10.grid}$ runs, and one forbidden model-and-precision pair takes
  $\val{f10.grid.c}$ of them with it}.}
\sumitem{25--28}{\result{\textbf{A set of $n$ elements has $2^{n}$ subsets}, one
  yes-or-no per element: $\val{f10.features}$ features give
  $\val{f10.subsets}$, and one more feature doubles it. Write the count down
  before writing the loop \dash{} it is one multiplication and it is the
  difference between a plan and a wish}.}
\sumitem{29--30}{\result{\textbf{$n$ things make $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ pairs}, halved
  because a set discards order and $\{a,b\}$ is $\{b,a\}$}.}
\sumitem{31--33}{\result{\textbf{Twice the data is a shade over four times the work
  when the work is per pair}: $\val{f10.docs}$ documents give
  $\val{f10.pairs}$ comparisons and $\val{f10.docs2}$ give $\val{f10.pairs2}$,
  a factor of $\val{f10.pairs.ratio}$. The ratio is $\frac{4n-2}{n-1}$ and
  falls towards four \emph{from above} \dash{} the script asserted the opposite
  and failed. The notation for that growth is
  Program~\ref{prog:P03}'s; the count is yours now, and it is the more useful
  half in a design review}.}
\sumitem{34--37}{\result{\textbf{Every naive probability is two counts and a
  division}, and the hard part is choosing the denominator, because requests,
  users and sessions all give correct answers to different questions. Report
  the two counts, not the rate alone. What a probability \emph{is} belongs to
  Program~\ref{prog:P23}. Set, mask, count: three representations of one idea,
  and almost everything done to a dataset is one of them from a different
  side}.}
\end{summarybox}

\begin{testexercises}
\item A log file has $500$ lines of which $40$ are distinct. Give the size of
  the set of distinct lines, and say what the other number counts.
  \answerto{$40$. The $500$ counts occurrences \dash{} how many lines went
  past.}
\item Is $\{3, 1, 3, 2\}$ a different set from $\{1, 2, 3\}$?
  \answerto{No. Repeats and order are both discarded, so they are one set with
  three members.}
\item Sets $A$ and $B$ have $60$ and $80$ members and share $25$. Give
  $\lvert A \cup B \rvert$ and $\lvert A \setminus B \rvert$.
  \answerto{$115$ and $35$. The union is $60 + 80 - 25$; the difference is
  $60 - 25$.}
\item Two sets are disjoint and have $60$ and $80$ members. Give the size of
  their union.
  \answerto{$140$. Disjoint means the intersection is empty, so the correction
  term is zero \dash{} which is the one case where adding is right.}
\item Write the mask expression for \enquote{in $A$ but not in $B$}.
  \answerto{\code{a \& \textasciitilde b}.}
\item A boolean mask over $\num{10000}$ rows has a mean of $\num{0.03}$. How
  many rows does it select?
  \answerto{$300$. The mean of a mask is the fraction it selects, so multiply
  by the number of rows.}
\item Negate \enquote{english or short}.
  \answerto{\enquote{not english and not short}. The \emph{or} becomes an
  \emph{and}.}
\item Negate \enquote{flagged and (long or duplicated)}.
  \answerto{\enquote{not flagged or (not long and not duplicated)}. Twice,
  outside in, and the brackets stay.}
\item A filter meant to drop rows that are both empty and untagged is written
  as \code{not empty and not untagged}. Say in one sentence what goes wrong and
  why nobody notices.
  \answerto{It keeps only rows that are neither, so it drops far more than
  intended \dash{} and the dropped rows are not in front of anybody, so the
  only symptom is a dataset that is smaller than expected.}
\item There are $6$ prompts, $4$ temperatures and $3$ seeds. How many runs?
  \answerto{$72$, by the product rule, provided every combination is allowed.}
\item How many subsets does a set of $10$ elements have, and how many does a
  set of $11$?
  \answerto{$1024$ and $2048$. Each extra element doubles it.}
\item How many pairs can be formed from $50$ items?
  \answerto{$1225$, from $\frac{50 \times 49}{2}$.}
\item An all-pairs job over $\num{5000}$ documents takes an hour. Roughly how
  long over $\num{10000}$?
  \answerto{About four hours \dash{} a shade over, by a factor of
  $\val{f10.t13.ratio}$ here. Twice the input is four times the pairs, and the
  shade over shrinks as the corpus grows.}
\item Of $\num{20000}$ requests, $\num{140}$ produced an error. Give the
  fraction, and name one other denominator somebody might reasonably use.
  \answerto{$\frac{140}{20000}$, which is $\num{0.007}$. Sessions, users, or
  requests that reached the model are all defensible and all give different
  numbers, which is why the denominator has to be stated.}
\end{testexercises}

\begin{furtherproblems}
\item Show that $\lvert A \cup B \rvert = \lvert A \rvert + \lvert B \rvert$
  exactly when $A$ and $B$ are disjoint.
  \answerto{The general rule subtracts $\lvert A \cap B \rvert$, so the two
  agree exactly when that is zero \dash{} which is what disjoint means.}
\item Give the largest and smallest $\lvert A \cup B \rvert$ can be when
  $\lvert A \rvert = 60$ and $\lvert B \rvert = 80$.
  \answerto{$140$ when they are disjoint, and $80$ when $A \subseteq B$. The
  intersection can be anything from $0$ to $60$.}
\item A vocabulary of $\num{50000}$ tokens is built from a corpus of a million
  words. What can you say about the number of words that occurred exactly once?
  \answerto{Nothing from these two numbers alone \dash{} they are the set size
  and the occurrence count, and neither says how the occurrences are
  distributed. It is a different count and needs collecting separately.}
\item Explain why the mean of a boolean mask is a fraction rather than an
  approximation to one.
  \answerto{Because a yes is exactly $1$ and a no is exactly $0$, so the sum is
  the count and dividing by the length is the definition of the fraction.}
\item Two filters are applied one after the other. Which set operation is that,
  and does the order matter?
  \answerto{Intersection, and no: a row survives both or it does not, and
  \emph{and} does not care which was asked first. Whether the \emph{cost} is
  the same is a different question.}
\item You want rows that satisfy exactly one of $p$ and $q$. Write it with
  \emph{and}, \emph{or} and \emph{not}.
  \answerto{Either of
  \begin{center}\code{(p or q) and not (p and q)}\end{center}
  \begin{center}\code{(p and not q) or (q and not p)}\end{center}
  It is called \emph{exclusive or} and most languages have an operator for it,
  but it is not one of the three words this section gives you, so from those it
  has to be built.}
\item A grid search has $\val{f10.grid.a}$ models, $\val{f10.grid.b}$
  precisions and $\val{f10.grid.c}$ batch sizes \dash{} the same grid this
  program sized \dash{} but now two particular model-and-precision pairs are
  unsupported. How many runs?
  \answerto{$\val{f10.grid.less.two}$: the full grid is $\val{f10.grid}$ and
  each unsupported pair removes the $\val{f10.grid.c}$ batch sizes under it.
  The product rule does not apply to the whole grid once the choices are not
  independent, so count the exclusions and subtract.}
\item Somebody proposes evaluating every subset of $30$ features to find the
  best combination, at a second per subset. Say how long it takes and what to
  propose instead.
  \answerto{Over a billion subsets, so over thirty years. Anything that does
  not enumerate: a greedy pass, a single-feature ranking, or a method that
  scores features without trying every combination.}
\item An all-pairs deduplication runs in four hours on today's corpus. The
  corpus will grow tenfold. Give the factor and say what it does to the plan.
  \answerto{About a hundredfold, since the pairs go as $n^{2}$ \dash{} four
  hours becomes over a fortnight. The plan has to change rather than get a
  bigger machine.}
\item This program gave you a numerator and a denominator and stopped. Say what
  question you would have to answer before calling the fraction a probability.
  \answerto{Whether the things counted underneath are the right ones and
  whether they are equally likely. Both need care, and
  Program~\ref{prog:P23} is where they get it.}
\end{furtherproblems}
