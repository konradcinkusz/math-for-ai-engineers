\program{Accumulation, area and expectation}
\label{prog:F13}
\index{integral!as accumulation}
\index{density!height against area}

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{1--5}{estimate an accumulated total as a sum of strips, and say what
  makes the estimate better}
\outcome{6--9}{read the integral sign, and say what each part of it names}
\outcome{10--12}{say how accumulating and differentiating are related}
\outcome{13--17}{say what makes a curve a density, and say what a point on
  one measures}
\outcome{18--22}{write a weighted average as an integral, and say what does
  the weighting}
\end{outcomes}

\begin{quiz}
\item A curve is cut into $n$ strips whose areas are added up. What happens to
  the total as $n$ grows? \teachesat{3--4}
  \answerto{It settles on a number \dash{} $\frac{1}{3}$ for $x^{2}$ over
  $\intcc{0}{1}$, approached from above and with the error halving each time
  $n$ doubles.}
\item What does $\mathrm{d}x$ name in $\int f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$?
  \teachesat{6--7}
  \answerto{The width of a strip. It is not decoration and it is not a
  multiplication sign.}
\item $\int_{0}^{1} x^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x$ is a number. What number, and what
  does it measure? \teachesatone{6}
  \answerto{$\frac{1}{3}$, and it measures the area between the curve and the
  horizontal axis from $0$ to $1$.}
\item How is accumulating related to differentiating? \teachesat{10--11}
  \answerto{Each undoes the other. Accumulate a rate and you recover the
  quantity; differentiate an accumulated total and you recover the rate.}
\item A curve is called a density. What has to be true of it?
  \teachesat{13--14}
  \answerto{It is never negative, and the area under the whole of it is
  exactly $1$.}
\item A density has the value $\val{f13.narrow.height}$ at some point. Is that
  possible? \teachesat{14--15}
  \answerto{Yes. A height is not an area, so nothing bounds it by one \dash{}
  the uniform density on an interval of width $\val{f13.narrow}$ has exactly
  that height.}
\item A curve is never negative and the area under it is
  $\val{f13.unnorm.total}$. How do you turn it into a density?
  \teachesat{16--17}
  \answerto{Divide it by $\val{f13.unnorm.total}$, or equivalently multiply by
  $\val{f13.unnorm.const}$. That divisor is the normalising constant.}
\item Write the shape of a weighted average of $x$ under a density $p$.
  \teachesatone{19}
  \answerto{$\int x\,p(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$: each value multiplied by how much of
  it there is, accumulated.}
\end{quiz}

\section{Adding up many small pieces}
\label{sec:F13-accumulate}
\index{integral!as accumulation}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:F11} asked how fast something is changing. This program asks
the opposite question, and it is the one you meet more often: given a rate,
how much accumulated?

You have done it. A service handles requests at a rate that varies through the
day, and you want the total for the day. What do you actually do?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Chop the day into intervals, multiply each rate by its interval, and add.
\end{ansblock}

Rate times time gives an amount; do it per interval and add up the amounts.
That is the whole idea, and everything else in this program is that idea taken
carefully.

Draw it and the picture is areas. Each interval is a rectangle whose width is
the interval and whose height is the rate, so its area is the amount
accumulated in it, and the total is the total area.

Try it on a curve. Take $y = x^{2}$ from $x = 0$ to $x = 1$, cut it into ten
strips of width $\num{0.1}$, and take each strip's height at its right-hand
edge. What total do the ten rectangles give?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{f13.rect.10}$}
Ten rectangles, each $\num{0.1}$ wide, heights $\num{0.1}^{2}$ up to
$1^{2}$.

That is an over-estimate, because the curve rises and each rectangle is as
tall as its right-hand edge, so every one of them pokes out above the curve.
Take a hundred strips instead and the overshoot shrinks. What do you get?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule
strips & total \\
\midrule
$10$ & $\val{f13.rect.10}$ \\
$100$ & $\val{f13.rect.100}$ \\
$1000$ & $\val{f13.rect.1000}$ \\
$\num{10000}$ & $\val{f13.rect.10000}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{ansblock}

Heading somewhere, from above, and $\frac{1}{3}$ is where.

\textbf{And the rate is knowable rather than merely observable}, which is
better than four rows suggesting a limit. The sum of the first $n$ squares has
a closed form, and putting it in gives
\[ \text{total} = \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{2n} + \frac{1}{6n^{2}} \]
so the error is $\frac{1}{2n}$ plus something smaller, which is why it
\textbf{halves every time $n$ doubles} \dash{} $\val{f13.err.10}$ at ten strips and
$\val{f13.err.10000}$ at ten thousand. Asserted in
\code{code/f13\_accumulation.py} at four sizes.

\mermaidfig{f13-sum-to-integral}{Strips, narrowed, and the limit they settle
on.}{a sum becoming an integral}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
So this is Program~\ref{prog:F11}'s move again, in the other direction.

There the chord's slope was computed for a gap $h$ and then $h$ was shrunk.
Here the strip's area is computed for a width and then the width is shrunk.
Both are limits, and neither is reached by any particular value.

\textbf{The two programs are one idea used twice}: something is estimated with
a finite step, and the answer is what the estimates settle on. That is why the
notation for both was invented at the same time by the same people, and why
they turn out to undo each other, which is section 3.
\end{fr}

\section{The notation}
\label{sec:F13-notation}
\index{integral!notation}

\begin{fr}
The limit of those sums has a name and a sign.

\[ \int_{0}^{1} x^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{1}{3} \]

Read it piece by piece, because every symbol is doing a job that a sigma also
does:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
$\int$ & an elongated S, for \emph{sum} \\
$0$ and $1$ & where the accumulation starts and stops \\
$x^{2}$ & the height of the strip at $x$ \\
$\mathrm{d}x$ & the width of the strip \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Compare it with the sum $\sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i)\,\Delta x$ \dash{}
Program~\ref{prog:F04}'s sigma, with a width attached to each term \dash{} and
the correspondence is exact, symbol for symbol. The integral is that sum with
the step gone to zero, and the sign is a stretched S because that is what it
was drawn to mean.

What does $\mathrm{d}x$ tell you that the rest does not?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Which letter is being accumulated over.
\end{ansblock}

Which matters as soon as there is more than one letter about.
$\int x^{2}t\,\mathrm{d}x$ and $\int x^{2}t\,\mathrm{d}t$ are different
quantities, and only the $\mathrm{d}$ says which.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{$\mathrm{d}x$ is not decoration and it is not a multiplication.} It is
the width of the strip, and it is there because an area needs a width \dash{}
drop it and the expression is a height, which is a different kind of thing.

The tell that it has been misread is an integral written without it, or one
where the $\mathrm{d}x$ has been moved past something that depends on $x$.
Both produce expressions that look plausible and mean nothing.

It is also why the units work out. If $f$ is requests per second and
$\mathrm{d}x$ is seconds, the product is requests, which is what an
accumulated total should be. \textbf{Reading the units off an integral is the
cheapest check there is}, and it catches a dropped $\mathrm{d}x$ immediately.
\end{trapbox}

Compute $\int_{0}^{2} 3\,\mathrm{d}x$, where the height is $3$ everywhere.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$6$}
A rectangle two wide and three tall. Not every integral needs a limit of sums,
and this one is the case where the strips are all the same and adding them is
multiplication.

Two properties worth having, both obvious from the picture and both used
constantly:

\begin{itemize}
\item accumulating from $a$ to $b$ and then from $b$ to $c$ gives the same as
  accumulating from $a$ to $c$;
\item accumulating a sum of two curves gives the sum of the two accumulations,
  which is Program~\ref{prog:F12}'s sum rule seen from the other side.
\end{itemize}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
One thing this program is \emph{not} going to do, stated plainly so that you
know it is a decision and not an omission.

\textbf{There is no technique of integration here.} Substitution, integration
by parts and partial fractions are the three that a calculus course spends
most of its time on, and none of them appears in this book, because nothing
this book pays off needs them.

What is needed is what an integral \emph{means}: an accumulated total, an
area, and in a moment a weighted average. Those three readings are used
constantly in the rest of the book and the techniques are not used at all. A
reader who wants them is better served by a calculus text, and a reader who
wants to compute a particular integral is better served by a computer.
\end{fr}

\section{The two directions}
\label{sec:F13-inverse}
\index{integral!and the derivative}

\begin{fr}
Accumulate a rate from $0$ to $x$ and you get a total that depends on $x$.
Call it $A(x)$.

Now push $x$ a little further, by $h$. The extra accumulated is a thin strip:
height $f(x)$, width $h$, so about $f(x)h$. What is
$\frac{A(x+h) - A(x)}{h}$?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{About $f(x)$, and exactly $f(x)$ in the limit}
Which is Program~\ref{prog:F11}'s difference quotient, so what has just been
computed is $A'(x)$.

\[ A'(x) = f(x) \]

\textbf{Differentiating an accumulated total gives back the rate that was
accumulated.} The two operations undo each other, which is the
\textbf{fundamental theorem of calculus} and is why it has that name: it is
the statement that the two halves of the subject are one subject.

\begin{rigourbox}
Stated, not proved. The argument above is the shape of the proof and skips
what makes it one \dash{} that \enquote{about $f(x)h$} has to be controlled as
$h$ shrinks, which needs $f$ to be continuous and needs the control to be
uniform. Any first analysis course does it properly.

What is safe to take is the statement, which is what the rest of the book
uses, and the reason it is believable, which is the strip.
\end{rigourbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
That theorem is also how integrals are computed by hand, and it is worth
seeing once.

To accumulate $x^{2}$ from $0$ to $1$, find something whose derivative is
$x^{2}$. By Program~\ref{prog:F12}'s power rule, $\frac{x^{3}}{3}$ does it.
Then the accumulated total is its value at the top minus its value at the
bottom:
\[ \int_{0}^{1} x^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x
   = \left[\frac{x^{3}}{3}\right]_{0}^{1} = \frac{1}{3} - 0 = \frac{1}{3} \]

which is the number the strips were heading for.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{An integral and an antiderivative are not the same thing.} One is an
accumulated total \dash{} a number, or an area. The other is a function whose
derivative is the one you started with. They are joined by a theorem, and the
theorem is what makes the calculation above legal.

The distinction is worth keeping because the theorem has conditions and the
habit of treating the two as synonyms hides them. It also explains why plenty
of perfectly ordinary functions have an integral you can compute numerically
and no antiderivative anybody can write down \dash{} $e^{-x^{2}}$ is the one
you will meet, and its accumulated total is the normal distribution's, which
is why that distribution's tables exist.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\section{A density}
\label{sec:F13-density}
\index{density!height against area}

\begin{fr}
Here is what the rest of this book needs an integral for.

A quantity that can take any value in a range \dash{} a latency, a score, a
weight \dash{} cannot have a probability at each individual value, because
there are infinitely many of them and they would have to add to one. What is
described instead is a curve, and \emph{areas} under that curve are the
probabilities.

Such a curve is a \textbf{density}. Two things have to be true of it. One is
that it is never negative. What is the other?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
\[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} p(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = 1 \]
\end{ansblock}

\textbf{The total area is exactly one}, because the quantity certainly takes
\emph{some} value, and the area under the whole curve is the probability of
that.

So a density is a curve with a constraint, and the constraint is an integral.
That is the first place in this book where an integral is not optional: you
cannot even say what a density \emph{is} without one.

Now the question this section exists for. A density has the value
$\val{f13.narrow.height}$ at some point. Is that possible?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Yes}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{A height on a density is not a probability, and nothing bounds it by
one.} What is bounded is the \emph{area}.

Take a quantity known to lie somewhere in an interval of width
$\val{f13.narrow}$, equally likely anywhere in it. The area has to be $1$ and
the width is $\val{f13.narrow}$, so the height is
$\val{f13.narrow.height}$. Narrow the interval further and the height rises
without limit, and the area never changes.

This is not an edge case, it is what a density is. The value $p(x)$ answers
\enquote{how much probability per unit of $x$ is there just here}, which is a
\emph{rate}, and a rate is not a probability any more than a speed is a
distance. Program~\ref{prog:F10} made the same point about a count and a
denominator; this is the continuous version of it.

The consequence in practice: a likelihood printed by a library can be greater
than one, and it is not a bug. Two of them can be compared as a ratio, and
neither can be read as a probability.
\end{trapbox}

\mermaidfig{f13-height-not-area}{The area is bounded and the height is not,
and narrowing the interval separates them.}{why a density can exceed one}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
One more operation, and it is the one you will do most often.

Here is a curve: $x(1-x)$ on $\intcc{0}{1}$. It is never negative there, so it
has the first property of a density. It accumulates to
$\val{f13.unnorm.total}$, so it does not have the second.

How do you fix it?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Divide by $\val{f13.unnorm.total}$ \dash{} that is, multiply by
$\val{f13.unnorm.const}$.
\end{ansblock}

And then the area is one, because dividing every height by the total divides
the area by the total.

That divisor is the \textbf{normalising constant}, and you have met it before
without the word. It is the denominator of a softmax, which
Program~\ref{prog:F07} built by exponentiating and then dividing by the total
so that the results add to one. Same operation: \textbf{make it non-negative,
add it up, divide by the sum.} A sum for a list, an integral for a curve.

\begin{aibox}
The constant is also why a great deal of practical work never computes one. In
a lot of settings the shape of a density is easy to write down and its total
is an integral nobody can do, so what is available is the curve
\emph{up to a constant}.

That is usually enough, because most questions are ratios and the constant
cancels: which of two values is more likely, or how much more likely. The
questions that need the constant are the ones that ask for a probability
outright, and the machinery invented to avoid computing it \dash{} sampling
methods, variational bounds \dash{} exists because that one integral is hard
and everything else is not.
\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\section{A weighted average}
\label{sec:F13-average}
\index{integral!as a weighted average}

\begin{fr}
The last shape this program owes the book.

An ordinary average adds the values and divides by how many there are. But if
some values occur more than others, you want each one counted according to how
much of it there is \dash{} a \textbf{weighted average}, which for a list is
$\sum_i x_i p_i$ with the weights adding to one.

A density is a continuous set of weights that integrates to one. So what
replaces that sum?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
\[ \int x\,p(x)\,\mathrm{d}x \]
\end{ansblock}

Each value multiplied by how much of it there is, accumulated. The sigma
becomes an integral and $p_i$ becomes $p(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$, which is the weight
of a strip.

Try it on the density from the last section, the normalised $x(1-x)$. Before
computing anything: it is symmetric about $x = \frac{1}{2}$, so what must the
weighted average be?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{f13.centre}$}
Symmetry gives it, and the integral confirms it \dash{} which is the right way
round, because a number that confirms an argument is worth more than one that
replaces it.

Now one where symmetry gives nothing. The curve $2x$ on $\intcc{0}{1}$ is a
density (its area is $1$; check that), and it puts more weight at the top end.
Its weighted average is $\val{f13.lop.centre}$, not $\num{0.5}$.

\textbf{So the weighted average is not the middle of the range}, and the only
way to know where it is, is to do the accumulation.

\mermaidfig{f13-weighted-average}{An average, weighted, with the weighting
done by a density.}{from a sum to an integral}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
That integral has a name, and this program deliberately does not give it one.

\textbf{It is an expectation}, and what it is the expectation \emph{of} is a
random variable, which is Program~\ref{prog:P24}'s subject and needs
Program~\ref{prog:P23}'s account of what a probability is first. Naming it
here would mean using three words this book has not defined.

What is yours after this program is the \emph{shape} \dash{} a value times a
density, accumulated \dash{} and the knowledge that it is what an average
becomes when the thing being averaged over is continuous. Every expectation in
the rest of the book is that shape, and every one of them will be recognisable
because of this frame.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
That is the Foundation part.

Thirteen programs, and the last three are worth naming together because they
are one arc. Program~\ref{prog:F11} defined a rate as a limit of differences.
Program~\ref{prog:F12} made rates computable and found that a network is a
product of them. This program went the other way: a total as a limit of sums,
and the two directions turned out to undo each other.

\textbf{Nothing after this assumes nothing.} From here the book has arithmetic,
algebra, functions, vectors, sets, and both halves of the calculus, and it
starts building on them rather than beside them. Program~\ref{prog:P01} is
next and it is about what your machine actually computes, which is a good
place to start being careful.
\end{fr}

\begin{testexercises}
\item A service runs at $30$ requests per second for $10$ seconds and then
  $50$ for $20$ seconds. Give the total, and say what shape you just computed.
  \answerto{$1300$ requests, from $30 \times 10 + 50 \times 20$. Two
  rectangles, which is the whole idea of accumulation with only two strips.}
\item Compute $\int_{0}^{4} 5\,\mathrm{d}x$.
  \answerto{$20$: a rectangle four wide and five tall.}
\item In $\int_{1}^{3} x t^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t$, which letter is accumulated over
  and which is along for the ride?
  \answerto{$t$ is accumulated over, because of the $\mathrm{d}t$; $x$ is a
  constant as far as this integral is concerned.}
\item An integrand is in requests per second and $\mathrm{d}x$ is in seconds.
  Give the units of the result, and say what that catches.
  \answerto{Requests. It catches a dropped $\mathrm{d}x$ immediately, because
  without it the expression is a rate rather than an amount.}
\item $A(x)$ accumulates $f$ from $0$ to $x$. Give $A'(x)$.
  \answerto{$f(x)$, by the fundamental theorem.}
\item Compute $\int_{0}^{2} x^{3}\,\mathrm{d}x$ using an antiderivative.
  \answerto{$4$, from $\left[\frac{x^{4}}{4}\right]_{0}^{2} = 4 - 0$.}
\item Is $f(x) = x - 1$ on $\intcc{0}{2}$ a density?
  \answerto{No. Its total area is $0$, and worse, it is negative on
  $\intco{0}{1}$ \dash{} it fails both conditions.}
\item A uniform density spans an interval of width $\num{0.02}$. Give its
  height.
  \answerto{$50$. The area must be $1$, so the height is
  $\frac{1}{\num{0.02}}$.}
\item A curve is non-negative with total area $\num{2.5}$. Give the
  normalising constant.
  \answerto{$\num{0.4}$, since dividing by $\num{2.5}$ is multiplying by
  $\frac{1}{\num{2.5}}$.}
\item A library reports a likelihood of $\num{3.2}$. Is something wrong?
  \answerto{Not necessarily. A density's height can exceed one; only areas are
  bounded.}
\item The uniform density on $\intcc{0}{1}$. Give
  $\int_{0}^{1} x\,p(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$ without computing an integral.
  \answerto{$\num{0.5}$, by symmetry: the density is flat, so the weighted
  average is the midpoint.}
\item Say in one sentence why $2x$ on $\intcc{0}{1}$ has a weighted average
  above $\num{0.5}$.
  \answerto{Because it puts more weight at the top end of the range, so the
  larger values count for more \dash{} and the value is
  $\val{f13.lop.centre}$.}
\end{testexercises}

\begin{furtherproblems}
\item The left-hand rule takes each strip's height at its left edge instead.
  Say whether it over- or under-estimates $\int_{0}^{1} x^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x$,
  and by roughly how much at $n$ strips.
  \answerto{Under-estimates, by about $\frac{1}{2n}$ \dash{} the mirror of the
  right-hand rule's overshoot, because the curve rises. Averaging the two is
  the trapezium rule and is much better than either.}
\item Show that $\int_{a}^{b} f + \int_{b}^{c} f = \int_{a}^{c} f$ from the
  strip picture.
  \answerto{The strips of the first two accumulations are exactly the strips
  of the third, partitioned at $b$; adding two groups of the same strips gives
  the whole.}
\item Explain why the fundamental theorem needs $f$ to be well behaved, using
  the strip argument.
  \answerto{The argument approximates the extra accumulated area by $f(x)h$,
  which needs $f$ not to vary much across the strip. A function that jumps
  inside every interval breaks that approximation and the theorem with it.}
\item A density is doubled everywhere. What has gone wrong, and what would fix
  it?
  \answerto{Its total area is now $2$, so it is no longer a density. Divide by
  $2$ \dash{} which is normalising, and it is why the constant exists.}
\item Two curves have the same shape but one is squeezed horizontally by a
  factor of $10$. If both are densities, how do their heights compare?
  \answerto{The squeezed one is ten times taller, because the area is fixed at
  $1$ and the width has been divided by ten. It is
  Program~\ref{prog:F05}'s inside-the-bracket squash with the area held
  constant.}
\item Why is a ratio of two densities' values meaningful when neither value is
  a probability?
  \answerto{Because the normalising constant is the same for both and cancels,
  so the ratio says how much more probability per unit there is at one place
  than another \dash{} which is a comparison the individual numbers cannot
  support.}
\item Give a reason why $\int x\,p(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$ deserves to be called an
  average, in terms of the discrete case.
  \answerto{Because it is $\sum_i x_i p_i$ with the sum replaced by an
  integral and $p_i$ by $p(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$; every part of the discrete
  weighted average has a counterpart and nothing has been added.}
\item A density is symmetric about $c$. What is its weighted average, and what
  does that save you?
  \answerto{$c$. It saves the integral entirely, which is why noticing
  symmetry first is worth more than computing faster.}
\item This program stopped before naming its last integral. Say what would
  have to be defined first, and why the order matters.
  \answerto{A probability and a random variable \dash{} what the density is
  the density of. Naming the expectation first would use three undefined
  words, and the shape is usable without them.}
\item Program~\ref{prog:F11} shrank a difference and this one shrank a strip.
  Say what the two limits have in common and where they differ.
  \answerto{Both are limits of a finite-step estimate as the step goes to
  zero, and neither is reached by any particular step. They differ in what is
  estimated \dash{} a ratio in one case, a total in the other \dash{} and the
  fundamental theorem says the two operations undo each other.}
\end{furtherproblems}
