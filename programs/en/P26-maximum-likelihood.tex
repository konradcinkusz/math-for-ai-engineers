\program[Estimation and likelihood]{Estimation and maximum likelihood}
\label{prog:P26}
\index{estimator}
\index{maximum likelihood}

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{1--8}{name the two ways an estimator can be wrong and split a mean
  squared error into them}
\outcome{10--17}{say what the $n-1$ corrects and what it leaves uncorrected}
\outcome{18--23}{write down a likelihood and find the parameter that
  maximises it}
\outcome{25--33}{derive the training loss of a language model from the
  probability of its data}
\outcome{34--39}{read a weight decay as a statement about how large the
  weights are believed to be}
\outcome{41--46}{write the score and use it to differentiate through a draw}
\end{outcomes}

\begin{quiz}
\item An estimator is unbiased. Name something that still makes it useless.
  \teachesat{1--2}
  \answerto{Variance. Unbiased says the ensemble of estimates is centred on
  the truth; it says nothing about how far any one of them is from it, and
  the two are separate axes.}
\item A sample variance divides by $n-1$ rather than $n$. What exactly does
  that fix? \teachesat{11--12}
  \answerto{One bias, exactly: the $n$-denominator version averages
  $\frac{n-1}{n}$ of the population variance, and dividing by $n-1$ instead
  makes the average exactly right.}
\item And is the square root of that corrected variance an unbiased estimate
  of the standard deviation? \teachesat{13--14}
  \answerto{No. A square root is concave, so by Jensen its average is below
  the root of the average \dash{} the corrected sample standard deviation
  understates, and \code{ddof=1} does not fix it.}
\item A model assigns probabilities to the tokens it saw. What does training
  it to minimise cross-entropy do to those probabilities?
  \teachesat{26--27}
  \answerto{Maximises their product. Cross-entropy against a one-hot target
  is the negative log-likelihood of the observed tokens divided by their
  count, so minimising it is maximum likelihood and nothing else.}
\item You add a weight decay of $\lambda$. State what that says about the
  weights, rather than what it does to them. \teachesat{37--38}
  \answerto{That they are believed to be Gaussian about zero with a
  particular width. A penalty is the logarithm of a prior, and the width is
  fixed by $\lambda$.}
\end{quiz}

\section{Two ways of being wrong}
\label{sec:P26-two-ways}
\index{estimator!bias and variance}
\index{measurement!mean squared error}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:P21} took a batch gradient apart and established one thing
about it: averaged over every batch that might have been drawn, it is the
gradient you wanted. That is what \emph{unbiased} means, and it proved it
exactly rather than by demonstration.

Then its own trapbox said unbiased is not enough. This program is what it is
not enough of.

Before reading on: an estimate is unbiased. Name a second way it can still be
wrong.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
It can be all over the place. Unbiased is a statement about the
\emph{ensemble} of estimates and says nothing about any one of them.
\end{ansblock}

So there are two axes and they are independent of each other.

\textbf{Bias} is how far the average estimate sits from the truth.
\textbf{Variance} is how far the estimates sit from their own average. An
estimator can be wrong in either way, in both, or \dash{} at a cost \dash{} in
one to buy the other.

Everything in this section is exact. Take the population
$\val{p26.pop.n}$ numbers $1$, $2$, $3$, $6$, draw
$\val{p26.draw.n}$ independently, and there are $\val{p26.samples}$ possible
samples; the script enumerates all of them over fractions with no tolerance
anywhere.

The population mean is $\val{p26.pop.mean}$ and its variance is
$\val{p26.pop.var}$. What are the bias and the variance of the sample mean?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Bias $0$, variance $\val{p26.var.unbiased}$}

Unbiased, which is Program~\ref{prog:P21}'s result on this population, and a
variance of $\sigma^{2}/n$, which is Program~\ref{prog:P25}'s rate. Nothing
here is new; it is the baseline the rest of the section is measured against.

Now change the estimator. Instead of the sample mean $\bar{x}$, use
$c\,\bar{x}$ for some fixed $c$ between $0$ and $1$ \dash{} the same estimate,
pulled towards zero.

Say what that does to each of the two axes.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
It introduces a bias, and it multiplies the variance by $c^{2}$.
\end{ansblock}

The bias is $(c-1)\mu$, because the average estimate is now $c\mu$ rather than
$\mu$. The variance carries the square, because Program~\ref{prog:P24}'s
$\Var(cX) = c^{2}\Var(X)$ says so.

So shrinking trades one axis against the other, and the obvious question is
whether the trade is ever worth taking. To ask it at all you need a single
number that both axes feed into.

What would you measure?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
The average squared distance from the truth \dash{} the
\textbf{mean squared error}.
\end{ansblock}

And it splits, exactly:
\[ \mathrm{MSE} \;=\; \Ex\bigl[(\hat\theta - \theta)^{2}\bigr]
   \;=\; \text{bias}^{2} + \Var(\hat\theta) \]

The script checks that as an identity at twenty-one shrinkages rather than at
one convenient value, over fractions, so it is a fact about the estimator and
not an arithmetic coincidence.

The \emph{squared} distance is doing work in that sentence and it is worth
saying why. An average absolute error does not split at all: there is no
identity that separates it into a part about the centre and a part about the
spread. Squaring is what makes the two axes add rather than interact, which
is the same reason Program~\ref{prog:P24} keeps a variance and not an average
distance, and it is the reason this loss and not another one is the one that
has a decomposition to talk about.

Read it before turning over: does $c = 1$ \dash{} the unbiased choice
\dash{} minimise it?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
No. On this population the best $c$ is $\val{p26.shrink.c}$.
\end{ansblock}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
& $c = 1$ & $c = \val{p26.shrink.c}$ \\
\midrule
bias & $0$ & $\val{p26.shrink.bias}$ \\
variance & $\val{p26.var.unbiased}$ & $\val{p26.shrink.var}$ \\
mean squared error & $\val{p26.mse.unbiased}$ & $\val{p26.mse.shrunk}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{A deliberately biased estimator is closer to the truth}, by
$\val{p26.shrink.gain.pct}$ per cent of the mean squared error, and the two
columns add up to their own bottom row to the digit printed.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{Unbiased is what you want.}}

Unbiased is one of the two axes and it is the one that is easy to check,
which is why it gets quoted. It is not the one that says how far your answer
is from the truth. An unbiased estimator with large variance is worse, on
every measure a practitioner cares about, than a slightly biased one that
sits still.

The word to be suspicious of is \emph{best}. Best at what? Nothing is best
until a loss has been named, and \enquote{unbiased} is not a loss.
\end{trapbox}

One honest caveat, because it is what keeps this from being a recipe: the
optimal $c$ above depends on $\mu$, which is the thing being estimated. You
cannot compute it.

So what is the finding worth, if the best shrinkage is unavailable?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
That a bias introduced on purpose can be a good trade \dash{} which is what
every regulariser in this book is.
\end{ansblock}

You have already met one. Program~\ref{prog:P20}'s weight decay pulls every
parameter towards zero, biasing every one of them, and it is used because the
variance it removes is worth more than the bias it adds.

That is the same trade as the table above, on a model instead of a mean, and
\S\ref{sec:P26-prior} says exactly what the $\lambda$ in it is.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\mermaidfig{p26-two-axes}{Two axes, and the one number that adds
them.}{bias, variance, error}

Two ways of being wrong, and a loss that weighs them. The rest of the program
is about choosing an estimator at all.
\end{fr}

\section{The $n-1$, and what it does not fix}
\label{sec:P26-n-minus-one}
\index{estimator!the n-1 correction}
\index{variance!sample}

\begin{fr}
The most famous bias in statistics, and the one almost nobody can explain.

A sample variance divides by $n-1$ rather than by $n$. Every library has a
flag for it and every course states it. Before reading on, say what goes
wrong if you divide by $n$.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
It comes out too small, and not occasionally \dash{} on average, every time.
\end{ansblock}

The reason is one sentence and it is worth having rather than remembering.
A sample variance measures spread about the \emph{sample} mean, and the
sample mean is fitted to that very sample, so it sits closer to the data than
the true mean does. Distances measured from it are therefore too short.

Notice what that argument does not depend on. It says nothing about which
numbers were drawn, only about how the estimator was built, so the shortfall
is a property of the recipe rather than of your data. That is why the fix can
be a fixed factor applied once and not something estimated alongside
everything else.

That is a bias, so it has a size. Guess the factor before turning over.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Exactly $\frac{n-1}{n}$}

Not approximately, and not for large $n$ only. Enumerated over every sample
at $n = 2$, $3$, $4$ and $5$ on the population from \S\ref{sec:P26-two-ways},
over fractions:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
population variance & $\val{p26.pop.var}$ \\
average of the $n$-denominator sample variance &
  $\val{p26.var.n.expected}$ \\
average of the $(n-1)$-denominator sample variance &
  $\val{p26.var.n1.expected}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

At $n = \val{p26.draw.n}$ the naive version is short by
$\val{p26.var.understate.pct}$ per cent, because $\frac{n-1}{n}$ is a half
there. Dividing by $n-1$ instead makes the average exactly right, which is
the whole of what the correction corrects.

So that is settled. Now the question the flag's name invites: is the square
root of the corrected variance an unbiased estimate of the standard
deviation?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
No, and by more than you would expect.
\end{ansblock}

Same population, same enumeration, the corrected sample standard deviation
averaged over every sample:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rrr}
\toprule
$n$ & average corrected $s$ & short by \\
\midrule
$2$ & $\val{p26.sd.mean.2}$ & $\val{p26.sd.short.2}\%$ \\
$3$ & $\val{p26.sd.mean.3}$ & $\val{p26.sd.short.3}\%$ \\
$5$ & $\val{p26.sd.mean.5}$ & $\val{p26.sd.short.5}\%$ \\
$10$ & $\val{p26.sd.mean.10}$ & $\val{p26.sd.short.10}\%$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The population standard deviation is $\val{p26.pop.sd}$. At $n = 2$ the
corrected estimate is short by nearly a quarter.

Program~\ref{prog:P19} has the reason in one line. Say which line.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Jensen's inequality, for a \textbf{concave} function.
\end{ansblock}

A square root is concave, and Program~\ref{prog:P19} states the concave case
in as many words: the function of an average is at least the average of the
function, so
\[ \Ex\bigl[\sqrt{s^{2}}\bigr] \;\le\; \sqrt{\Ex[s^{2}]} \;=\; \sigma \]

An unbiased estimate of a quantity does not survive a non-linear function
applied to it. \textbf{Correcting the variance cannot correct the standard
deviation, because the correction and the square root do not commute.}

And Program~\ref{prog:P19}'s equality case says when the gap closes: only
when the sample variance is the same for every sample, which it is not. The
shortfall above falls with $n$ for the same reason \dash{} the estimates
spread less.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{\code{ddof=1} gives me an unbiased standard deviation.}}

It gives an unbiased \emph{variance}. Nothing in any library gives an
unbiased standard deviation, because the correction depends on the
distribution and there is no flag for it.

The size is what makes this worth knowing rather than pedantry. Over five
runs of an experiment \dash{} which is what people actually report a standard
deviation of \dash{} the number is short by about
$\val{p26.sd.short.5}$ per cent, and it is short in the direction that makes
results look more reproducible than they are.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\transcript{p26-still-short}

Two comparisons. The first says the corrected variance is right and the naive
one is not; the second prints the corrected standard deviation beside the
population's.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
So what should you do about it?

Nothing dramatic, and the honest answer has three parts. Quote the variance
where the reader can take a root themselves, because the variance is the
quantity that adds and the one this book keeps for that reason. Know the
size of the bias at the $n$ you have. And treat a standard deviation over a
handful of runs as the rough number it is.

None of that needs a correction factor, and reaching for one is how the
distributional assumption gets smuggled in.
\end{fr}

\section{The parameter that makes the data least surprising}
\label{sec:P26-mle}
\index{maximum likelihood!definition}
\index{likelihood!as a function of the parameter}

\begin{fr}
Both sections so far took an estimator as given and asked how wrong it was.
Now the question underneath: where does an estimator come from at all?

You have data and a family of distributions indexed by a parameter. Say what
would make one value of that parameter better than another.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
It makes the data you actually saw more probable.
\end{ansblock}

That is the whole idea, and it is a rule for turning data into a number
rather than a fact about the world. The \textbf{likelihood} is
$\Prob(\text{data} \mid \theta)$, read as a function of $\theta$ with the
data held fixed, and \textbf{maximum likelihood} picks the $\theta$ that
maximises it.

Program~\ref{prog:P23} already warned about the word, and the warning
matters here: the likelihood is a probability of the \emph{data}, not of the
parameter. It does not integrate to one over $\theta$ and it is not a
distribution over $\theta$. Treating it as one is what
Program~\ref{prog:P28} does properly, with a prior, and this section does
not.

The data are $n$ independent observations, so the likelihood is a product of
$n$ terms. Give two reasons to take its logarithm.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
It turns the product into a sum, and it stops the product underflowing.
\end{ansblock}

The first is Program~\ref{prog:F03}'s, which is where this book turns
products into sums, and it is what makes the derivative tractable: a sum
differentiates term by term where a product of $n$ things does not. The
second is Program~\ref{prog:P02}'s \dash{} Program~\ref{prog:F03}'s own
sequence probability was $\val{f03.seq.prod}$, a number no format can hold.

And the logarithm cannot move the maximum, because
Program~\ref{prog:F05} proved that a strictly increasing function keeps the
order of a list.

So: write the log-likelihood, differentiate, set to zero, which is
Program~\ref{prog:P15}'s recipe for a maximum. Try it. Twenty trials give
seven successes; what rate maximises the likelihood?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p26.bern.mle}$}

The obvious answer, and it is worth seeing that it is derived rather than
assumed. With $k$ successes in $n$ trials the log-likelihood is
$k\ln p + (n-k)\ln(1-p)$ up to a constant, and
\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}p}
   \Bigl[k\ln p + (n-k)\ln(1-p)\Bigr]
   \;=\; \frac{k}{p} - \frac{n-k}{1-p} \]
which is zero exactly at $p = k/n$. The script checks that over fractions and
then checks every other rate on a thousand-point grid is strictly worse, so
it is the maximum rather than a stationary point.

\textbf{Every rate anybody has ever computed by dividing successes by trials
was a maximum likelihood estimate}, and this is the first time the book has
said so.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Now the same recipe on a Gaussian, because its answer collides with
\S\ref{sec:P26-n-minus-one} on purpose.

Maximising the log-likelihood of $\val{p26.gauss.n}$ observations over the
mean gives the sample mean, $\val{p26.gauss.mu}$ here. Over the variance it
gives the average squared distance from that mean \dash{} with $n$ in the
denominator, which comes out as $\val{p26.gauss.mle.var}$ against the
unbiased $\val{p26.gauss.unbiased.var}$.

Say what that makes maximum likelihood.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Biased.
\end{ansblock}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{Maximum likelihood gives you the right answer.}}

It gives the answer that makes your data most probable, which is a different
thing and is not the same as unbiased. The Gaussian variance is the standing
example: the estimate maximum likelihood hands you is short by exactly the
$\frac{n-1}{n}$ of the previous section, and the correction everybody applies
is a correction \emph{away} from the maximum likelihood answer.

Two methods, two answers, and the disagreement is not a mistake in either.
They are optimising different things.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{rigourbox}
\textbf{Not proved here: why maximum likelihood is worth using at all.}

The case for it is asymptotic. As $n$ grows the estimate converges on the
true parameter, and among a broad class of estimators its variance goes to
the smallest achievable. Both statements are theorems with hypotheses, both
need machinery beyond this book, and the second has a name \dash{} the
Cramér--Rao bound \dash{} that a reader will meet and can look up.

What is worth taking instead is the shape of the promise. It is
\emph{asymptotic}: maximum likelihood is a good idea in the limit and it is
biased at any finite $n$, which is exactly what the Gaussian variance shows.
Nothing about it says the answer at your sample size is unbiased, and the
previous section says it is not.
\end{rigourbox}
\end{fr}

\section{Training a language model is maximum likelihood}
\label{sec:P26-training}
\index{cross-entropy!as a negative log-likelihood}
\index{maximum likelihood!and language models}

\begin{fr}
This is what the section before it was for, and it is the frame
Program~\ref{prog:P18} promised.

A model reads a sequence and, at each position, produces a distribution over
the vocabulary. The token that actually came next has some probability under
that distribution. Call the model's parameters $\theta$ and the observed
tokens $t_1, \dots, t_n$.

Write the probability the model assigns to the whole observed sequence.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\prod_{i=1}^{n} p_\theta(t_i \mid t_{<i})$}

Which is a likelihood: a probability of the data, read as a function of the
parameters. So maximum likelihood says to maximise it over $\theta$.

Now do the two things \S\ref{sec:P26-mle} said to do. Take the logarithm,
which turns it into a sum. Then, because optimisers minimise, negate it. Then
divide by $n$, so that sequences of different lengths are comparable.

The negation is the only one of the three that is pure convention. Maximising
a quantity and minimising its negative are the same problem, and every
optimiser in Program~\ref{prog:P20} was written to go downhill, so the sign
is there for the machinery rather than for the mathematics. What it buys in
return is that the number reads as a cost, which is why one run's is
comparable with another's.

Write what you have.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \ln p_\theta(t_i \mid t_{<i})$}

\begin{note}
\textbf{The definition Program~\ref{prog:P18} gave and left unjustified.}
That program needed the derivative of the cross-entropy long before this
book could say what the loss was for, so it defined
$L = -\sum_j y_j \ln p_j$, said the justification was here, and got on with
the Jacobian.

Here it is. With a one-hot target every term but one is zero and
$L = -\ln p_c$ for the observed token, so the average cross-entropy over a
sequence \textbf{is} the expression above, character for character.

\textbf{The cross-entropy loss is the negative log-likelihood of the observed
tokens, per token, and there is nothing else in it.} Training a language
model is maximum likelihood. It was never anything else.
\end{note}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The chain is worth following backwards, because three programs turn out to be
quoting one computation.

Program~\ref{prog:F02} has a per-token loss of $\val{p26.f02.loss}$ nats.
Program~\ref{prog:F03} takes that, turns it into a per-token probability and
raises it to the power of $\val{p26.f03.tokens}$ tokens, getting a sequence
probability whose base-ten logarithm it commits.

Undo it: take that committed logarithm, divide by the token count and negate.
The script does exactly that and gets $\val{p26.back.loss}$ nats, agreeing
with Program~\ref{prog:F02} to within what rounding the committed exponent to
two decimals can account for \dash{} a tolerance derived rather than chosen.

\textbf{A loss and a sequence probability are the same number written two
ways}, and the build now fails if either program moves.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\mermaidfig{p26-one-quantity}{One quantity, arrived at from two
directions.}{likelihood and loss}

Three consequences fall out of the reframing, and each is a thing people do
without a reason to hand.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The first. \textbf{Label smoothing} replaces the one-hot target with
$1-\varepsilon$ on the observed token and $\varepsilon/K$ spread over all
$K$ of them, and it is usually introduced as a regularisation trick.

Read it as maximum likelihood instead. The loss $-\sum_j t_j \ln p_j$ is
minimised over the simplex when $\vect{p}$ equals $\vect{t}$, whatever
$\vect{t}$ is.

So what is label smoothing maximum likelihood \emph{for}?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
For the smoothed distribution \dash{} a target that was never observed.
\end{ansblock}

Which is a real claim about what it does. At
$\varepsilon = \val{p26.ls.eps}$ over $\val{p26.ls.classes}$ classes, the
optimum is $\val{p26.ls.true}$ on the observed token and
$\val{p26.ls.other}$ on each of the others \dash{} found by search rather
than quoted \dash{} so the optimal gap between the winning logit and the rest
is $\val{p26.ls.gap}$.

\textbf{That is the point of it, and it is a number.} Against a one-hot
target the optimum wants $p_c = 1$, which needs an infinite logit gap, so the
loss keeps pushing for ever and can never arrive. Smoothing names an
attainable optimum instead. It is not noise added for luck; it is a
different, reachable target.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The second. \textbf{Class weighting} multiplies each example's loss by a
weight, and reads as \enquote{caring more about the rare class}.

As maximum likelihood it is sharper than that: weighting an example by $w$ is
the log-likelihood of a sample in which that example appeared $w$ times. So
weighting does not make the model better at the rare class \dash{} it
estimates the parameters of a \emph{different population}, one in which the
rare class is commoner than in yours.

Whether that is what you want is a question about deployment, and the
reframing is what lets you ask it.

The third is smaller and it is already on the page. Say why the loss is
divided by the token count rather than summed.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
So a long sequence and a short one are comparable.
\end{ansblock}

A log-likelihood grows with the number of tokens, so a sum measures the
corpus as much as the model. Per token it is an average, and
Program~\ref{prog:P19} says what averaging a per-token loss does and does not
survive \dash{} you may average the losses, and you may not average the
perplexities.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{Cross-entropy measures the distance between two
distributions.}}

It is not a distance: it does not vanish when the two agree, and it is not
symmetric. What vanishes is the \emph{excess} of the cross-entropy over the
target's own entropy, and that excess has a name and is
Program~\ref{prog:P30}'s.

For this program the reading is narrower and complete: against a one-hot
target the cross-entropy is a negative log-likelihood, and its minimum is
wherever the likelihood is largest.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\section{A penalty is the logarithm of a prior}
\label{sec:P26-prior}
\index{regularisation!as a prior}
\index{weight decay!what its lambda is}

\begin{fr}
Maximum likelihood asks only what makes the data probable. Suppose you also
have an opinion about the parameter before seeing any data \dash{} that the
weights of a network are probably small, say.

Program~\ref{prog:P23} gives the machinery: multiply the likelihood by a
prior and you have something proportional to the posterior. Maximising that
instead is \textbf{maximum a posteriori}, or MAP.

Take the logarithm of a product of two things. What happens to the objective?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
The log-prior is \emph{added} to the log-likelihood.
\end{ansblock}

\[ \ln\bigl[\Prob(\text{data} \mid \theta)\,\Prob(\theta)\bigr]
   \;=\; \ln \Prob(\text{data} \mid \theta) + \ln \Prob(\theta) \]

Negate it, as \S\ref{sec:P26-training} did, and the second term becomes
something added to a loss and minimised alongside it.

That is the shape of every regularised objective anybody has ever written.
Now put a Gaussian prior of width $\tau$ on each weight, centred on zero, and
say what the added term is.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\dfrac{\lVert \vect{w}\rVert^{2}}{2\tau^{2}}$, plus a constant}

Because the logarithm of $e^{-w^{2}/2\tau^{2}}$ is $-w^{2}/2\tau^{2}$, and
the normalising factor does not depend on $\vect{w}$ so it cannot move the
minimum.

\textbf{A quadratic penalty is the logarithm of a Gaussian prior.} That is
the whole of the correspondence, and it is one line.

So the penalty is not a device bolted onto the objective for luck. It is what
you get by writing down a belief and taking a logarithm, and the strength of
the penalty is a statement about the \emph{width} of the belief.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Which lets a hyperparameter be read rather than tuned.

Program~\ref{prog:P20} settled weight decay by adding $\lambda\vect{w}$ to
the gradient and measured what that does. Match the two gradients \dash{}
$\lambda\vect{w}$ against $\vect{w}/\tau^{2}$ \dash{} and
\[ \lambda \;=\; \frac{1}{\tau^{2}}
   \qquad\text{so}\qquad \tau \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}} \]

The script matches them on the gradients Program~\ref{prog:P20} actually adds
rather than on a formula, at five values of $w$, because the correspondence
depends on a convention \dash{} whether the penalty carries a half \dash{}
and Program~\ref{prog:F04} recorded what happens when a convention is quoted
from memory.

Program~\ref{prog:P20} ran at $\lambda = \val{p26.p20.lambda}$. What belief
is that?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Every weight Gaussian about zero with width $\val{p26.prior.tau}$}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rr}
\toprule
$\lambda$ & implied prior width $\tau$ \\
\midrule
$\num{0.001}$ & $\val{p26.tau.0001}$ \\
$\num{0.01}$ & $\val{p26.tau.001}$ \\
$\val{p26.p20.lambda}$ & $\val{p26.tau.01}$ \\
$\num{1.0}$ & $\val{p26.tau.10}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{A weight decay is a width, and reading it as one makes it
checkable.} If the trained weights of a layer have a spread nothing like the
$\tau$ your $\lambda$ implies, the prior and the data disagree, and that is a
thing you can look at rather than a knob you turn.

Nothing in the table is new arithmetic. It is one reciprocal square root and
it has never been printed beside a $\lambda$.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{Weight decay is a trick that happens to work.}}

It is the exact consequence of one belief, and the belief is stateable: the
weights are Gaussian about zero. Every other prior gives a different
penalty \dash{} a Laplace prior gives the absolute value rather than the
square, which is why $L_1$ produces zeros and $L_2$ does not, and that is a
fact about the shape of a distribution at the origin rather than a fact about
optimisers.

The reason it reads as a trick is that the prior is never written down. It is
there whether or not anybody writes it, and its width is
$1/\sqrt{\lambda}$.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{warning}
\textbf{A MAP is a point, and that is a real limitation.}

Everything above maximises the posterior and then throws it away, keeping
only the argument of the maximum. So a MAP estimate cannot say how sure it
is, cannot say whether the maximum was sharp or nearly flat, and cannot be
propagated into a later calculation.

Keeping the whole posterior is a different subject and it is
Program~\ref{prog:P28}'s: a distribution over the parameter rather than a
point, with credible intervals and the arithmetic that makes them
computable. This program stops where the maximum is found.
\end{warning}

\mermaidfig{p26-prior-is-penalty}{Where a penalty comes from, in one
step.}{a belief, logged}
\end{fr}

\section{The score, and a debt from Part VI}
\label{sec:P26-score}
\index{score function}
\index{estimator!score-function}

\begin{fr}
One derivation is outstanding and it is not this program's own.

Program~\ref{prog:P21} met a quantity that is an average over a random draw
and needed its gradient. It named two ways of getting one, measured what
separates them \dash{} three orders of magnitude of variance \dash{} and
then said, in as many words, that the algebra behind them was
Program~\ref{prog:P24}'s or this program's to give. Program~\ref{prog:P24}
did not give it.

It is four lines, and it is the same object \S\ref{sec:P26-mle} set to zero.
Look back at that derivative: what was the thing whose zero located the
maximum?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{The derivative of the log-probability}

\[ s(x, \theta) \;=\; \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta}
   \ln p(x \mid \theta) \]

It is called the \textbf{score}, and maximum likelihood is the instruction
\emph{set the total score of your data to zero}. That is all
\S\ref{sec:P26-mle} was doing when it wrote $k/p - (n-k)/(1-p) = 0$.

Now a question about the score itself. Average it over the data the model
says you will see \dash{} that is, take its expectation under
$p(x \mid \theta)$, at the same $\theta$. What do you get?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Zero, exactly}

Checked over fractions at $\val{p26.score.ps}$ different rates, with no
tolerance anywhere.

The reason is one line: $\Ex[s] = \sum_x p \cdot \frac{1}{p}
\frac{\partial p}{\partial \theta} = \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta}
\sum_x p = \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} 1 = 0$. The probabilities sum to
one whatever $\theta$ does, so their derivative sums to nothing.

\textbf{That is why setting the score to zero is the right instruction} and
not merely a convenience: at the true parameter the score already averages
to zero, so an estimator that makes the observed score zero is aiming at the
right condition.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Now the debt. You want the gradient of $\Ex_{x \sim p_\theta}[f(x)]$, where
$\theta$ moves the distribution and $f$ is anything at all \dash{} a reward,
a simulator, a human rating.

The obstacle Program~\ref{prog:P21} named is that you cannot differentiate a
draw. But the expectation is a sum over $x$ of $p(x \mid \theta) f(x)$, and
$p$ is differentiable even though the draw is not.

Differentiate that sum and see what the score buys you.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\nabla_\theta \Ex[f] = \Ex\bigl[f(x)\,s(x,\theta)\bigr]$}

Because $\nabla p = p \nabla \ln p$, so
\[ \nabla_\theta \sum_x p\,f
   \;=\; \sum_x f\,\nabla_\theta p
   \;=\; \sum_x p\, f\, \nabla_\theta \ln p \]
and the last expression is an expectation, which means it can be estimated by
sampling.

\textbf{That is Program~\ref{prog:P21}'s score-function estimator, and it is
four lines of maximum likelihood.} The script checks it against the
derivative taken the other way round, exactly over fractions, at every rate
and $\val{p26.score.cases}$ different functions.

It is also what a policy gradient is. Multiply the reward you got by the
gradient of the log-probability of the action you took, and average: that
expression is not an approximation to anything, it is an identity, and the
only thing wrong with it is the variance Program~\ref{prog:P21} measured.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
So the program closes on one object seen four times.

The score is the derivative of a log-probability. Setting it to zero is
maximum likelihood. Averaging it gives zero, which is why that is the right
condition. Multiplying it by a reward and averaging differentiates through a
draw. And adding a log-prior to the log-likelihood before any of that is
every regularised objective there is.

\mermaidfig{p26-set-it-to-zero}{One derivative, and the four things asking
for it.}{score, four uses}

What is not here is what to do when the maximum is not where you want to
stop. Program~\ref{prog:P28} keeps the whole posterior rather than its peak,
and Program~\ref{prog:P27} asks whether a difference between two estimates is
larger than the noise in them. Both are questions this program has now made
askable and neither is its own.
\end{fr}

\begin{testexercises}
\item An estimator has bias $\num{0.4}$ and variance $\num{0.09}$. A second
  has bias $0$ and variance $\num{0.30}$. Which is closer to the truth on
  average, and by how much?
  \answerto{Mean squared error is $\text{bias}^{2} + \Var$, so the first is
  $\num{0.16} + \num{0.09} = \num{0.25}$ and the second is $\num{0.30}$. The
  biased one is closer, by $\num{0.05}$ \dash{} which is the section's point
  in two lines of arithmetic.}
\item You measure a quantity five times and report the mean and the sample
  standard deviation with \code{ddof=1}. State one thing that number is not,
  and say in which direction it errs.
  \answerto{It is not an unbiased estimate of the standard deviation. The
  correction makes the \emph{variance} unbiased, and a square root is
  concave, so by Jensen the reported figure understates the spread \dash{} by
  around $\val{p26.sd.short.5}$ per cent at $n = 5$ on this program's
  population, and always in the direction that makes results look more
  reproducible than they are.}
\item A coin gives $3$ heads in $10$ throws. Write the log-likelihood, find
  the maximum likelihood estimate, and say why the answer needs no
  calculation once the previous frames are in hand.
  \answerto{$3\ln p + 7\ln(1-p)$ up to a constant, whose derivative
  $3/p - 7/(1-p)$ vanishes at $p = \num{0.3}$. It needs no calculation
  because the Bernoulli maximum likelihood estimate is $k/n$ for every $k$
  and $n$, which the program derives once.}
\item A training run reports a cross-entropy of $\num{2.0}$ nats per token
  over $500$ tokens. Give the log-probability the model assigns to that
  sequence, and say whether the probability itself is worth computing.
  \answerto{$-\num{2.0} \times 500 = -1000$ nats. The probability is
  $e^{-1000}$, which no floating-point format holds \dash{} which is
  Program~\ref{prog:P02}'s reason for working in logarithms and
  Program~\ref{prog:F03}'s own example, and it is why the loss is reported
  and the likelihood is not.}
\item Two teams use weight decays of $\num{0.01}$ and $\num{1.0}$ on the same
  architecture. State what each is claiming about the weights, and say which
  claim is the stronger.
  \answerto{Widths of $\val{p26.tau.001}$ and $\val{p26.tau.10}$ about zero.
  The second is much the stronger claim \dash{} a tenfold larger $\lambda$ is
  a prior narrower by $\sqrt{10}$, so it asserts far more confidently that
  the weights are small, and it will be far more visibly wrong if they are
  not.}
\end{testexercises}

\begin{furtherproblems}
\item Show that for the shrunk estimator $c\bar{x}$ the mean squared error is
  minimised at $c = \mu^{2}/(\mu^{2} + \sigma^{2}/n)$, and say what happens
  to it as $n$ grows.
  \answerto{The error is $\mu^{2}(c-1)^{2} + c^{2}\sigma^{2}/n$; differentiate
  and set to zero. As $n$ grows $\sigma^{2}/n$ goes to zero and the optimum
  goes to $1$, so the shrinkage is worth less the more data you have \dash{}
  which is the same statement as \enquote{regularise a small model harder}.}
\item The score has expectation zero at the true parameter. Say what that
  makes the total score of $n$ observations, and use
  Program~\ref{prog:P25} to say how it behaves as $n$ grows.
  \answerto{A sum of $n$ independent quantities each of mean zero, so it has
  mean zero and a variance of $n$ times one term's \dash{}
  Program~\ref{prog:P25}'s first identity. Divided by $n$ it concentrates on
  zero at the $1/\sqrt{n}$ rate, which is why the estimate that makes the
  observed average score zero converges on the truth.}
\item A Laplace prior on each weight is proportional to
  $e^{-\lvert w\rvert/b}$. Give the penalty it corresponds to and say what
  distinguishes it from the Gaussian case in one sentence.
  \answerto{$\lVert\vect{w}\rVert_{1}/b$, the sum of absolute values. Its
  derivative does not vanish as $w$ approaches zero, where the Gaussian's
  does, so it keeps pushing all the way in and produces exact zeros \dash{}
  a fact about the shape of the prior at the origin rather than about the
  optimiser.}
\item Label smoothing at $\varepsilon$ over $K$ classes gives an optimal
  logit gap of
  \[ \ln\Bigl(\frac{1 - \varepsilon(K-1)/K}{\varepsilon/K}\Bigr) \]
  Say what happens to it as $\varepsilon$ goes to zero, and why that is the
  right behaviour.
  \answerto{It goes to infinity, because the denominator does to zero: with
  no smoothing the target is one-hot and the optimum is unattainable. The
  smoothing parameter is exactly the thing that makes the optimum finite,
  so the limit reproducing the unsmoothed case is the check that the formula
  is right.}
\item A colleague weights the rare class in a classifier by $10$ and reports
  that accuracy on that class improved. State what was estimated, and one
  measurement that would say whether it was the right thing to estimate.
  \answerto{The parameters of a population in which the rare class is ten
  times commoner than in the data. Whether that is right depends on the
  deployment mix, so the measurement is the class frequencies where the
  model will actually run \dash{} if they match the reweighted mix the choice
  was principled, and if they match the original one it traded overall
  performance for a number on a slide.}
\end{furtherproblems}
