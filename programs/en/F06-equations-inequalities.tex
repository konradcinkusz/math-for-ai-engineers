\section{Solving is undoing}
\label{sec:F06-undoing}
\index{equation!checking by substitution}

\begin{fr}
An equation is a question. $3x + 7 = 22$ asks: \emph{which value of $x$ makes
these two expressions the same number?}

You answer it by undoing what was done to $x$, and there is exactly one rule.
\textbf{Whatever you do to one side, do to the other.} The equals sign is a
claim that the two sides are the same number, and the same operation applied
to the same number gives the same answer, so the claim survives.

The machine here takes $x$, multiplies by $3$, then adds $7$. So undo the
addition first, because it was done last:
\begin{align*}
3x + 7 &= 22 \\
3x &= 15 \\
x &= 5
\end{align*}

That is Program~\ref{prog:F05}'s inverse arriving as a procedure rather than
as a graph: \textbf{meet the operations in the reverse of the order they were
applied.}

Solve $5 - 2x = 1$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$x = 2$}
Subtract $5$ from both sides to get $-2x = -4$, then divide both sides by $-2$.

Now the habit that makes this program worth its place, and it costs one line.
\textbf{Put the answer back into the original equation.} Here
$5 - 2 \times 2 = 5 - 4 = 1$, which is what was asked for. That check catches a
sign error, a dropped term and an arithmetic slip, and it does so without your
having to find the mistake.

The rest of this program will check every solution it produces, and so does
\code{code/f06\_equations.py}, which refuses to build the book if a solution
printed here fails to satisfy the equation it came from.

Solve $3x + 4 = x + 10$, where $x$ appears on both sides.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$x = 3$}
Gather the $x$ terms on one side first: subtract $x$ from both sides to get
$2x + 4 = 10$, then proceed as before. Check: $9 + 4 = 13$ and $3 + 10 = 13$.

Which side you gather on does not matter. Subtracting $3x$ instead gives
$4 = -2x + 10$, then $-6 = -2x$, then $x = 3$ again. Pick whichever leaves you
with a positive coefficient, because that is the version in which you are less
likely to lose a sign.

Solve $\frac{x}{4} - 1 = 2$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$x = 12$}
Add $1$ to both sides, then multiply both sides by $4$. Check:
$\frac{12}{4} - 1 = 2$.

Fill in the missing step:
\begin{align*}
\tfrac{x}{4} - 1 &= 2 \\
\tfrac{x}{4} &= \blank \\
x &= 12
\end{align*}
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$3$.
\end{ansblock}

Now one to answer quickly. No working, and do not check it.

\textbf{Solve $x^{2} = 4x$.}
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$x = 0$ or $x = 4$}
If you wrote $x = 4$ alone, you divided both sides by $x$ \dash{} and you have
no idea whether $x$ is zero.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Dividing both sides by an unknown throws away a solution.} It is the
one move in this section that is not reversible, and it is not reversible for
exactly one value: division by zero is not an operation, so the step you took
is only valid on the assumption you were trying to test.

The reasoning that produced it is sound everywhere else. Dividing both sides
by $3$ is safe because $3$ is not zero and never will be; dividing both sides
by $x$ is the same gesture applied to something whose value is the question.

Do it the other way and nothing is lost. Bring everything to one side and
factorise:
\[ x^{2} - 4x = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad x(x - 4) = 0 \]
A product is zero when one of its factors is, so $x = 0$ or $x = 4$, and both
check: $0 = 0$ and $16 = 16$.

The habit to carry: \textbf{when you cancel something, say out loud what you
are assuming is not zero.} If you cannot say it, you have not solved the
equation \dash{} you have solved a different one.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Two more things an equation can do, and neither is a mistake.

$x = x + 1$ has \textbf{no solution}. Subtract $x$ from both sides and it says
$0 = 1$, which is false whatever $x$ is, so no value can rescue it.

$2x = x + x$ has \textbf{every} solution. Subtract $x + x$ and it says
$0 = 0$, which is true whatever $x$ is. It is not an equation to be solved but
an identity: a statement that two ways of writing the same thing agree.

Both arrive again in section 4 with a picture attached, because two equations
in two unknowns can fail in the same two ways and there the failure is visible
as two lines that never meet, or as one line drawn twice.

\begin{note}
The distinction matters as soon as you are solving numerically rather than by
hand. A solver handed an inconsistent system does not always say so; some
return the closest thing they can find, which is a perfectly good answer to a
question nobody asked. Program~\ref{prog:P08} makes that precise, because
\emph{closest} needs a definition before it means anything.
\end{note}
\end{fr}

\section{Making a letter the subject}
\label{sec:F06-subject}
\index{formula!rearranging}

\begin{fr}
Rearranging a formula is the same skill with the answer left as letters.
Program~\ref{prog:F02} already made $x$ the subject of $y = wx + b$:
\[ x = \frac{y - b}{w} \]
subtract the bias, then divide by the weight, which is the reverse of the
order in which they were applied.

The only new thing here is that any letter can be the target. To make $w$ the
subject of the same formula, undo what was done to $w$ instead: it was
multiplied by $x$ and then $b$ was added, so subtract $b$ and divide by $x$.

Write $w$ as the subject.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
\[ w = \frac{y - b}{x} \qquad (x \ne 0) \]
\end{ansblock}

Note the condition, and note that it is not decoration. At $x = 0$ the formula
reads $y = b$ whatever $w$ is, so the data cannot tell you the weight \dash{}
which is the same observation Program~\ref{prog:F05} made from the other end
when it forbade the bias and found the line pinned to the origin.

\textbf{State the condition every time you divide by a letter.} It is the
previous section's habit written into a formula rather than into a solution.

Now a rearrangement worth doing in full, because you will meet it. A unit
computes $\sigma(wx + b)$ and reports a probability $p$. Recover $x$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
\[ x = \frac{\logit(p) - b}{w}, \qquad
   \logit(p) = \ln\!\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right) \]
\end{ansblock}

Three undoings, in reverse order. The unit multiplied by $w$, added $b$, then
applied $\sigma$. So apply $\sigma^{-1}$, which Program~\ref{prog:F05} named
the logit; subtract $b$; divide by $w$.

Take $w = 2$ and $b = -3$, and read the report $p = \num{0.5}$. Then
$\logit(\num{0.5}) = 0$, so $x = \frac{0 + 3}{2} = \num{1.5}$ \dash{} which is
$-\frac{b}{w}$, the crossing point Program~\ref{prog:F05} derived. The same
number by a second route, and that is the check.

The unit now reports $p = \num{0.8}$. What was $x$?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$x = \val{f06.solve.p8}$}
Because $\logit(\num{0.8}) = \ln 4$, so
$x = \frac{\ln 4 + 3}{2}$.

Substitute it back and $\sigma(2 \times \val{f06.solve.p8} - 3)$ comes to
$\num{0.8}$. That is not a claim: \code{code/f06\_equations.py} performs the
substitution for every probability printed in this section and fails the build
if the residual exceeds one part in $10^{12}$.

The same unit now reports $p = \num{0.99}$. What was $x$?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$x = \val{f06.solve.p99}$}
Now compare the three inputs. From a probability of $\num{0.5}$ to
$\num{0.8}$ the input went from $\num{1.5}$ to $\val{f06.solve.p8}$; from
$\num{0.8}$ to $\num{0.99}$, a much smaller change, it went from
$\val{f06.solve.p8}$ to $\val{f06.solve.p99}$ \dash{} further still.

That is the flattening of the logistic seen from the inverse side: near the
ends, a large movement in the input buys very little movement in the
probability. Program~\ref{prog:F07} draws that curve, and
Program~\ref{prog:F12} says what it costs a gradient.

It is also why a confident unit is hard to make more confident. The last
percentage point of probability is bought with more movement of the input than
the first fifty, and no amount of training changes that \dash{} it is a
property of the function, not of the fit.

One left. No working \dash{} write the answer down. Solve $x^{2} = 9$.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$x = 3$ or $x = -3$}
Not $x = 3$ alone.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{A square root taken across an equation carries both signs.} The
symbol $\sqrt{9}$ means the non-negative root, $3$, and that is a convention
about the symbol. The \emph{equation} $x^{2} = 9$ is a different object: it
asks which numbers square to nine, and two of them do.

Program~\ref{prog:F05} has the reason in a sentence you have already read.
$x^{2}$ is not one-to-one, so it has no inverse over all the reals, so
\enquote{undo the squaring} is not a single operation and cannot be written as
one. Restrict to $x \ge 0$ and it is, and the answer is $3$.

The consequence in practice: whenever you take a root, a logarithm or any
other inverse across an equation, ask whether the function you are undoing was
one-to-one on the range you care about. If it was not, you are choosing a
branch, and the choice should be deliberate rather than notational.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

meet the operations in the reverse of the order they were applied.}{undoing in
reverse order}

\section{The straight line}
\label{sec:F06-line}
\index{line!gradient and intercept}

\begin{fr}
Every equation of the form
\[ y = mx + c \]
draws a straight line, and the two letters are readable straight off it. $m$
is the \textbf{gradient}: how much $y$ climbs for one unit of $x$. $c$ is the
\textbf{intercept}: the value of $y$ where the line crosses the vertical axis,
because that is where $x = 0$.

These are not new quantities. Program~\ref{prog:F05} called them a stretch and
a shift applied to $f(x) = x$, and $m$ and $c$ are the same two numbers under
the names a geometry course gives them. $w$ and $b$ are the names a machine
learning paper gives them. Three vocabularies, one line.

Give the gradient and the intercept of $y = 3x - 2$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Gradient $3$, intercept $-2$}
Read them off; there is nothing to compute.

Now the other direction. Given two points, what is the gradient of the line
through them? Climb divided by distance across:
\[ m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} \]

Find the gradient of the line through $(1, 3)$ and $(2, 5)$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$m = 2$}
From $\frac{5 - 3}{2 - 1}$. Which point you call the first does not matter:
$\frac{3 - 5}{1 - 2}$ is the same $2$, because both signs flip together.

Now find $c$. The line is $y = 2x + c$ and it passes through $(1, 3)$, so
$3 = 2 + c$ and $c = 1$. The line is $y = 2x + 1$ \dash{} the same line
Program~\ref{prog:F05} fitted three points to when it argued for the bias.

Does the point $(3, 7)$ lie on it?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Yes}
Because $2 \times 3 + 1 = 7$. \emph{Lies on the line} and \emph{satisfies the
equation} are the same statement in two vocabularies, and moving between them
is most of what this program is for.

\textbf{And this is the entire linear model.} One input, one output, two
numbers to choose. Everything the rest of the book adds is more inputs, more
outputs, and a function with a corner in it between the layers; the shape
$y = wx + b$ does not change.

How many numbers do you need to pin a line down?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Two}
A gradient and an intercept, or equivalently two points, or a point and a
gradient. Two, however you supply them.

\begin{aibox}
A linear layer with one input and one output holds exactly two numbers, and
training it means choosing them. That is why the smallest interesting model in
this book is not small because it is simple but because the space it can
express is a set of straight lines and nothing else.

Widen the layer to $n$ inputs and it holds $n + 1$ numbers: $n$ weights and
one bias. Program~\ref{prog:P06} makes the weights a matrix and shows that
matrix multiplication is composition, which is Program~\ref{prog:F05}'s
collapse argument done in more than one dimension.
\end{aibox}

When are two lines parallel?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{When their gradients are equal}
And their intercepts are not, or they would be the same line. Parallel means
the same climb everywhere, so the vertical gap between them never changes and
they never meet.

Perpendicular is the other one worth knowing: two lines are at right angles
when their gradients multiply to $-1$. A line climbing at $2$ meets at right
angles a line falling at $\frac{1}{2}$.

What is the equation of the line perpendicular to $y = 2x + 1$ that passes
through the origin?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$y = -\frac{x}{2}$}
Its gradient is $-\frac{1}{2}$, because $2 \times \left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)
= -1$, and it passes through the origin so $c = 0$.

That $-1$ looks arbitrary and is not. Program~\ref{prog:F09} gives vectors a
direction and Program~\ref{prog:P05} gives them an angle, and there
\emph{perpendicular} becomes a dot product equal to zero \dash{} of which the
rule you have just used is the two-dimensional case written out. It is worth
meeting the rule now and the reason later, rather than the other way round.

One line is left over. What is the gradient of the line $x = 3$?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
It has none \dash{} or rather, the formula gives $\frac{y_2 - y_1}{0}$, which
is not a number.
\end{ansblock}

A vertical line climbs infinitely far for no distance across, so the gradient
does not exist and no value of $m$ and $c$ produces it. \textbf{$y = mx + c$
does not describe every line}, which is a limitation of the form rather than
of lines.

It is also Program~\ref{prog:F05}'s vertical-line test arriving from the other
side: $x = 3$ is not a function of $x$ either, because at $x = 3$ every $y$
satisfies it and everywhere else none does. The two facts are one fact.

\begin{note}
The general form $ax + by = c$ covers the vertical case, with $b = 0$, and it
is the form section 4 uses for exactly that reason. It has the disadvantage
that the gradient and the intercept are no longer readable off it, which is
why both forms survive.
\end{note}
\end{fr}

\section{Two equations, two unknowns}
\label{sec:F06-simultaneous}
\index{equation!simultaneous}

\begin{fr}
One equation in two unknowns does not pin either of them down. $x - y = 1$ is
satisfied by $(1, 0)$, by $(2, 1)$, by $(\num{5.5}, \num{4.5})$ and by every
other point on a certain line \dash{} which is exactly what the previous
section said a line is.

Add a second equation and you are asking for a point that satisfies both at
once. Geometrically: a point on both lines.

Here is the pair this section works with:
\begin{align*}
3x + 2y &= 12 \\
x - y &= 1
\end{align*}

The second equation says $x = y + 1$. Put that into the first, in place of
$x$, and you have one equation in one unknown. Do it.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$3(y + 1) + 2y = 12$, so $5y + 3 = 12$, so $y = \num{1.8}$ and
$x = \num{2.8}$.
\end{ansblock}

That is \textbf{substitution}: use one equation to express a letter, then
spend it in the other. And check, as always, in \emph{both} equations \dash{}
one is not enough, because the answer to a substitution automatically
satisfies the equation you substituted into.

\[ 3 \times \num{2.8} + 2 \times \num{1.8} = \num{8.4} + \num{3.6} = 12
   \qquad \num{2.8} - \num{1.8} = 1 \]

Both hold, so $(\num{2.8}, \num{1.8})$ is the point where the two lines cross.

There is a second route. Multiply the lower equation by $2$ and add it to the
upper. What happens?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
The $y$ terms cancel: $3x + 2y + 2x - 2y = 12 + 2$, so $5x = 14$ and
$x = \num{2.8}$.
\end{ansblock}

That is \textbf{elimination}: scale one or both equations until a letter
appears with equal and opposite coefficients, then add. Same answer, fewer
fractions, and it is the route that generalises \dash{} with three unknowns
substitution becomes unpleasant and elimination does not.

Neither route is a new idea. Multiplying an equation through by $2$ is the
balance rule of section 1; adding two equations is doing the same thing to
both sides of the first, where the thing is the second. The second equation's
equals sign is the guarantee: it says its two sides are the same number, so
adding one of them to the left and the other to the right adds the same thing
to both.

Now the two failures. Solve $2x + y = 5$ together with $4x + 2y = 7$.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{There is no solution}
Multiply the first by $2$ and it says $4x + 2y = 10$. The second says
$4x + 2y = 7$. So $10 = 7$, which is false whatever $x$ and $y$ are.

That is section 1's $x = x + 1$ with a picture: both equations describe lines
of gradient $-2$, they have different intercepts, and parallel lines never
meet. \textbf{No point lies on both, so there is no point to find.}

Now solve $2x + y = 5$ together with $4x + 2y = 10$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Every point of the line $2x + y = 5$}
The second equation is the first multiplied by $2$. It is the same line
written twice, and it adds no information at all.

That is section 1's $2x = x + x$: an identity dressed as an equation. Reduce
the pair and you get $0 = 0$, which is true and useless.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
The two lines & Solutions \\
\midrule
different gradients & exactly one, where they cross \\
equal gradients, different intercepts & none \\
equal gradients, equal intercepts & every point of the line \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Three outcomes and no others}, and you can tell which you have from
the gradients before doing any work. That is worth a moment: it means the
question \emph{does this system have a unique solution} is answerable without
solving it.

Write the gradients of $3x + 2y = 12$ and $x - y = 1$, and say which row of
the table they land in.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$-\frac{3}{2}$ and $1$. Different, so the first row: exactly one solution.
\end{ansblock}

To read a gradient off $ax + by = c$, make $y$ the subject:
$y = -\frac{a}{b}x + \frac{c}{b}$, so the gradient is $-\frac{a}{b}$. That is
section 2's skill spent on a general formula rather than on a particular one
\dash{} and section 2's condition comes with it, because that division needs
$b \ne 0$. The case it excludes is the vertical line of frame $21$, which is
the one line with no gradient to read.

The quantity that decides between the rows has a name you will meet again.
Two lines have equal gradients when $\frac{a_1}{b_1} = \frac{a_2}{b_2}$, which
rearranges to
\[ a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1 = 0 \]

\begin{aibox}
That expression is the \textbf{determinant} of the two-by-two array of
coefficients, and Program~\ref{prog:P09} gives it a meaning: it is the factor
by which the map scales area, so a determinant of zero says the map flattens
the plane onto a line and cannot be undone.

It is worth seeing here first, in a form you can check by hand, because by the
time it arrives as a property of a matrix it will be easy to treat as a
formula to memorise. It is not. It is the answer to \emph{do these two lines
have the same gradient}, and Program~\ref{prog:P08} is where solving
$Ax = b$ turns into the same three outcomes with more letters.
\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\section{Inequalities, and the one rule that changes}
\label{sec:F06-inequalities}
\index{inequality!solving}

\begin{fr}
An inequality asks for a \emph{range} rather than a value. $2x + 1 > 7$ asks
which values of $x$ make the left side exceed the right, and the answer will
be a set rather than a number.

Almost everything carries across from section 1. You may add the same number
to both sides, subtract the same number, and multiply or divide both sides by
the same \emph{positive} number, and the inequality still says something true.

So $2x + 1 > 7$ gives $2x > 6$ gives $x > 3$, exactly as if the sign had been
an equals.

Solve $5x - 2 \le 13$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$x \le 3$}
Add $2$, divide by $5$. And check, which for an inequality means testing a
value inside the range and a value outside it: at $x = 3$ the left side is
$13$, which satisfies $\le$; at $x = 4$ it is $18$, which does not.

That two-sided check is worth doing. A one-sided check cannot distinguish
$x \le 3$ from $x \ge 3$, and telling those apart is the whole content of the
next two frames.

No working on this one. Write down the first answer that comes to you.

\textbf{Solve $-2x > 6$.}
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$x < -3$}
Not $x > -3$. The sign turns round.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Multiplying or dividing an inequality by a negative number reverses
it}, and this is the only step of section 1 that does not survive the move
from equals to greater-than.

Check it on a number rather than trusting the rule. It is true that $2 < 5$.
Multiply both sides by $-1$ and you get $-2$ and $-5$; and $-2$ is
\emph{greater} than $-5$, not smaller. Multiplying by a negative reflects the
number line, and a reflection swaps left and right.

The reason the error is so easy is that every other rule does carry across
unchanged, so you have four correct habits and one exception, and the
exception is invisible in the notation. There is no mark on the page that says
\emph{you are about to divide by a negative}.

The reliable fix is to avoid the step. Add $2x$ to both sides of $-2x > 6$
instead, and you get $0 > 6 + 2x$, then $-6 > 2x$, then $-3 > x$ \dash{} which
is $x < -3$, reached without ever multiplying by a negative number. If you
never divide by a negative you never have to remember to flip.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Confirm it by testing rather than by arguing, because the argument is the
thing that just failed.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
$x$ & $-2x$ & Is $-2x > 6$? \\
\midrule
$-4$ & $8$ & yes \\
$-3$ & $6$ & no, it is equal \\
$0$ & $0$ & no \\
$4$ & $-8$ & no \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The values that work are the ones below $-3$, which is $x < -3$. Confirmed.

Solve $3 - x \ge 1$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$x \le 2$}
Subtract $3$ to get $-x \ge -2$, then multiply by $-1$ and turn the sign:
$x \le 2$. Or move the $x$ across instead and never meet the negative:
$3 \ge 1 + x$, so $2 \ge x$.

Check both ends: at $x = 2$ the left side is $1$, which satisfies $\ge$; at
$x = 3$ it is $0$, which does not.

Two inequalities can be held at once, and the notation stacks them. Write
\enquote{$x$ is at least $-1$ and at most $1$} in symbols.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$-1 \le x \le 1$}
Read it as two statements sharing a middle: $-1 \le x$ and $x \le 1$. Every
operation you perform must be performed on all three parts, which is the
balance rule again with one more side to keep in step.

So doubling gives $-2 \le 2x \le 2$, and negating turns \emph{both} signs and
swaps the ends: $-1 \le -x \le 1$ is the same set here only because it happens
to be symmetric, and $-1 \le x \le 3$ negated is $-3 \le -x \le 1$.

\begin{warning}
\textbf{A compound inequality is not a range you can read off in the order it
is written after you have operated on it.} If the two outer numbers end up
with the larger on the left, you have negated and forgotten to swap. The
statement $3 \le x \le -1$ describes no numbers at all, and it is nearly
always a lost flip rather than an empty set anybody meant.
\end{warning}

Solve $-1 \le 2x + 3 \le 5$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$-2 \le x \le 1$}
Subtract $3$ from all three parts to get $-4 \le 2x \le 2$, then halve all
three. No sign changed, because $2$ is positive.

Now the payoff, and it needs nothing new. A classifier fires when its output
probability exceeds a half:
\[ \sigma(z) > \num{0.5} \]
Program~\ref{prog:F05} showed that $\sigma$ crosses a half at $z = 0$ and is
strictly increasing everywhere.

Rewrite that condition in terms of $z$ alone.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$z > 0$}
And the reason is Program~\ref{prog:F05}'s, not a new one: a strictly
increasing function preserves order, so $\sigma(z) > \sigma(0)$ holds exactly
when $z > \sigma^{-1}(\num{0.5}) = 0$. Applying $\sigma^{-1}$ to both sides of
an inequality is safe \emph{because} $\sigma$ is strictly increasing; had it
been strictly decreasing the sign would have turned, for the same reason
multiplying by $-1$ turns it.

\textbf{A decision threshold is an inequality}, and moving the threshold is
changing the number on the right of it. A classifier tuned to fire at
$\sigma(z) > \num{0.9}$ is one tuned to fire at $z > \logit(\num{0.9})$, which
is $\val{f05.logit.p9}$ \dash{} the same number Program~\ref{prog:F05}
computed, arriving here as a threshold rather than as a round trip.

\begin{note}
This is why a model's threshold can be tuned without recomputing a single
probability. Compare the logits against $\logit(\text{threshold})$ and you get
the same decisions with one exponential fewer per item, which for a large
evaluation set is not nothing. Where to put the threshold is a different and
much harder question, and it is a question about what the two kinds of error
cost you rather than about the arithmetic. This book does not settle it.
\end{note}
\end{fr}

\section{Clipping, and two ways to do it}
\label{sec:F06-clipping}
\index{clipping!by value and by norm}

\begin{fr}
A clipped value is two inequalities enforced rather than solved. To keep $x$
inside $\intcc{-1}{1}$:
\[ \clip(x) = \max(-1, \min(1, x)) \]
Read it inside out, as Program~\ref{prog:F05} taught: $\min(1, x)$ enforces the
upper bound and $\max(-1, \cdot)$ enforces the lower one. Either order works,
and it is worth checking that for yourself on $x = 5$ and $x = -5$.

$\relu$ is the same machine with one bound: $\relu(x) = \max(0, x)$ clips from
below at zero and does nothing above it. Program~\ref{prog:F05} drew it as
half a V; here it is a single inequality applied.

Evaluate $\clip(x)$ between $-1$ and $1$ at $x = \num{0.5}$, $x = 3$ and
$x = -7$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$\num{0.5}$, $1$ and $-1$.
\end{ansblock}

Inside the bounds nothing happens; outside them the value is replaced by
whichever bound it passed. That flatness outside the bounds is the whole
behaviour, and it is what makes clipping cheap and what makes it lose
information: every input above $1$ produces the same output, so from the
output you cannot recover which it was.

Now the same idea applied to something with more than one number in it, which
is where it stops being obvious. A gradient is a list of numbers, and you have
been told to \enquote{clip the gradient at $\num{1.0}$}.

Write down what you think that does, in one sentence, before reading on.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
There are two common answers, they are both in use, and they are different
operations.
\end{ansblock}

\textbf{Clipping by value} applies the scalar rule of the previous frame to
each component on its own. \textbf{Clipping by norm} leaves every component's
share of the vector alone and rescales the whole thing so that its length is
at most the threshold.

Take the gradient
\[ g = (6, \num{0.5}, -\num{0.25}) \]
whose length is
\[ \lVert g \rVert = \sqrt{6^{2} + \num{0.5}^{2} + \num{0.25}^{2}}
   = \val{f06.grad.norm} \]
Both operations are now applied with a threshold of $\num{1.0}$.

\begin{rigourbox}
Two objects arrive here that this program has not defined: the \emph{length} of
a vector with more than one component, and the \emph{angle} between two of
them. Program~\ref{prog:F09} builds the first and Program~\ref{prog:P05} the
second, and neither is needed to follow what comes next \dash{} the one fact
this section uses is the one printed above, that a length is the square root of
the sum of the squared components, so every figure in this section can be
checked with a calculator. The angle is quoted for scale rather than computed.
\end{rigourbox}

Work out what clipping by value gives.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
\[ (\val{f06.clip.value.c1},\; \val{f06.clip.value.c2},\;
   \val{f06.clip.value.c3}) \]
\end{ansblock}

Only the first component was outside the bounds, so only the first component
changed. The other two were already between $-1$ and $1$ and were left exactly
as they were.

Now the question the whole section exists for. That vector has been
\enquote{clipped at $\num{1.0}$}. Is its length at most $\num{1.0}$?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{No. Its length is $\val{f06.clip.value.norm}$}
Above the threshold it was supposedly clipped to.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Clipping by value does not bound the length of the vector, and it does
not preserve its direction either.} Both failures come from the same place:
the rule was applied to each component separately, and neither the length nor
the direction is a property of any one component.

On this gradient it turns the vector through
$\val{f06.clip.value.angle}$ degrees. The step that gets taken is not a
shorter version of the gradient; it points somewhere else.

Clipping by \emph{norm} does what the name \enquote{clip at $\num{1.0}$}
suggests. It multiplies every component by the same factor
$\min\bigl(1,\; \frac{\num{1.0}}{\lVert g \rVert}\bigr)$ \dash{} so a gradient
already shorter than the threshold is left exactly as it is \dash{} giving
\[ (\val{f06.clip.norm.c1},\; \val{f06.clip.norm.c2},\;
   \val{f06.clip.norm.c3}) \]
whose length is $\val{f06.clip.norm.norm}$ exactly and whose direction is the
original one exactly, because scaling every component by one positive number
is a stretch and Program~\ref{prog:F05} showed a stretch changes no
comparison.

The two are one word apart in every library that offers both, they take the
same threshold, and they accept the same argument. \textbf{Read which one you
are calling.}
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Both numbers in that box are computed in \code{code/f06\_equations.py}, and
what the script asserts is not the two figures but the two properties: that
clipping by norm turns the vector through zero degrees and lands its length
exactly on the threshold, and that clipping by value does neither. Change the
gradient and the figures move; the properties do not, and it is the properties
the frame is claiming.

Both results are also checked against a second implementation, for the reason
Program~\ref{prog:F03} learnt the hard way about a claim run on one machine.

Which of the two would you choose, if the problem is that one training step
occasionally takes an enormous jump?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
By norm, because the complaint is about the size of the step and that is what
the norm measures.
\end{ansblock}

The direction of the gradient is the information the step is carrying;
its length is what occasionally goes wrong. Clipping by norm keeps the first
and bounds the second, which is exactly the surgery the problem calls for.
Clipping by value bounds neither the thing you wanted bounded nor keeps the
thing you wanted kept.

That is a judgement about a typical case, not a rule. Program~\ref{prog:P21}
owns the question properly \dash{} it has the noise model that says why the
occasional enormous step happens at all, and clipping is one of several
answers to it.

\begin{aibox}
The threshold itself is a number somebody chose, and the choice is not
innocent: a threshold below the typical gradient norm clips \emph{every} step,
at which point the optimiser is following the direction of the gradient with a
step size you have fixed by hand and the gradient's magnitude is being
discarded entirely. That is a different algorithm from the one you think you
are running, and it is a difference nothing reports.

Print the fraction of steps that get clipped. If it is near one, the threshold
is doing more than protecting you from spikes.
\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{f06-two-clippings}{One gradient, one threshold and two operations
whose names differ by a word. Only one of them bounds the length, and only one
of them keeps the direction.}{clipping by value against by norm}

  every $x$ works (one line twice).}
\item A system of two equations is scaled so that one equation is multiplied
  through by $10^{6}$. Does the solution change? Does anything?
  \answerto{The solution does not \dash{} it is the same pair of lines. What
  changes is how a numerical solver behaves, because the two rows now differ
  enormously in scale. Program~\ref{prog:P02} owns why that matters.}
\item Solve $x^{2} > 4$.
  \answerto{$x > 2$ or $x < -2$. It is not $-2 < x < 2$, which is the solution
  of $x^{2} < 4$; sketching $y = x^{2}$ against $y = 4$ settles it faster than
  any rule, and this is where Program~\ref{prog:F05}'s graph earns its place.}
\item Why does dividing an inequality by a variable require more care than
  dividing an equation by one?
  \answerto{Because you must know the \emph{sign} as well as knowing it is not
  zero: dividing by a negative reverses the inequality. Two cases, and the
  usual advice is to multiply up rather than divide.}
\item A threshold is moved from $\num{0.5}$ to $\num{0.9}$. By how much does
  the logit threshold move, and is the same true moving from $\num{0.9}$ to
  $\num{0.99}$?
  \answerto{From $0$ to $\val{f05.logit.p9}$, and then to $\ln 99$, which is
  about $\num{4.6}$. The second move is larger for a smaller change in
  probability, which is frame $12$'s observation about the logistic seen at
  the threshold.}
\item Show that clipping by norm at threshold $t$ leaves a vector unchanged
  when its length is already at most $t$, and say why the rule is written as a
  conditional rather than as a formula.
  \answerto{The scaling factor $\frac{t}{\lVert g \rVert}$ exceeds $1$ when
  $\lVert g \rVert < t$, so applying it unconditionally would \emph{grow} a
  small gradient. Clipping is a bound, not a normalisation, and the
  conditional is what makes it one.}
\item Give a vector for which clipping by value and clipping by norm agree
  exactly, and say what characterises such vectors.
  \answerto{Any vector already inside the threshold in both senses, for
  instance $(\num{0.1}, \num{0.1})$ at $t = 1$: both leave it alone. Once
  clipping bites, they agree exactly when at most one component is non-zero
  \dash{} truncating that component and rescaling the whole vector are then the
  same operation, in any number of dimensions.}
\item A gradient is $(10, 0, 0, \ldots, 0)$: a single non-zero component,
  in a thousand dimensions. What does each kind of clipping at
  $\num{1.0}$ produce?
  \answerto{By value: the same vector with the component reduced to $1$, which
  here has length exactly $1$ \dash{} so in this special case the two agree.
  By norm: the same thing, since the length was $10$ and every component is
  divided by $10$. \textbf{Clipping by value is safe exactly when the vector
  is a single component}, which is why the trap does not fire on the example
  people usually try first.}
\item Two engineers report that they \enquote{clip gradients at $\num{1.0}$}
  and their training curves differ. Before looking at anything else, what
  should you ask?
  \answerto{Which of the two operations each of them means. It is one word in
  the call, it changes the direction of every clipped step, and neither
  configuration file will say.}
\end{furtherproblems}
