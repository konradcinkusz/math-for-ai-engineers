\program{Rules of differentiation and the chain rule}
\label{prog:F12}
\index{derivative!rules}
\index{chain rule!and backpropagation}

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{1--6}{differentiate a sum, a constant multiple and a power without

\outcome{7--13}{differentiate a product and a quotient, and say what each of

\outcome{14--20}{apply the chain rule to a function of a function, and unpack

\outcome{21--25}{say what a composition of layers differentiates to, and where

\end{outcomes}

\begin{quiz}
\item Differentiate $x^{5}$. \teachesatone{2}
  \answerto{$5x^{4}$: bring the index down and reduce it by one.}
\item Differentiate $3x^{4} - 5x + 2$. \teachesat{4--5}
  \answerto{$12x^{3} - 5$. Each term separately, and the constant goes.}
\item Is the derivative of $f(x)g(x)$ equal to $f'(x)g'(x)$? \teachesat{7--8}
  \answerto{No. On $x^{2}$ and $x^{3}$ at $x = \val{f12.trap.x}$ the right
  answer is $\val{f12.trap.right}$ and that guess gives
  $\val{f12.trap.wrong}$.}
\item Write the product rule. \teachesatone{9}
  \answerto{$(fg)' = f'g + fg'$: differentiate each factor with the other held
  still, then add.}
\item Differentiate $\frac{x^{2}}{x + 3}$. \teachesat{12--13}
  \answerto{$\frac{2x(x+3) - x^{2}}{(x+3)^{2}}$, by the quotient rule.}
\item Write the chain rule for $f(g(x))$. \teachesatone{15}
  \answerto{$f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)$: the outside differentiated at the inside's
  value, times the inside's derivative.}
\item Differentiate $(3x + 1)^{4}$. \teachesat{15--16}
  \answerto{$12(3x+1)^{3}$, which is $4(3x+1)^{3}$ times the inside's
  derivative $3$.}
\item Give $\sigma'$ in terms of $\sigma$. \teachesat{17--18}
  \answerto{$\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)\bigl(1 - \sigma(z)\bigr)$.}
\item What is the largest $\sigma'$ can be, and where? \teachesat{18--19}
  \answerto{$\val{f12.sig.max}$, at $z = 0$.}
\item Differentiate $\sigma(wx + b)$ with respect to $x$. \teachesat{19--20}
  \answerto{$w\,\sigma(wx+b)\bigl(1 - \sigma(wx+b)\bigr)$, by the chain rule.}
\item A network is $\num{40}$ layers deep. How many layer factors
  $w_k\sigma'(z_k)$ does the chain rule put in the derivative with respect to
  the first layer's input?
  \teachesat{22--23}
  \answerto{$\num{40}$, one per layer, all multiplied together.}
\item Each of $\val{f12.depth}$ layers contributes its largest possible
  factor. What is the product at most? \teachesat{26--27}
  \answerto{$\val{f12.vanish.bound}$, and only if every unit sits exactly at
  its centre.}
\item For a logistic layer of weight $w$, how large must $w$ be before the
  layer can amplify a gradient rather than shrink it? \teachesat{28--29}
  \answerto{Above $\val{f12.explode.w}$ in size, because the factor's size is
  $\lvert w \rvert\sigma'$ and $\sigma'$ never exceeds $\val{f12.sig.max}$. A
  weight of $-8$ amplifies exactly as hard as one of $8$.}
\end{quiz}

\section{Four rules instead of a limit}
\label{sec:F12-rules}
\index{derivative!rules}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:F11} computed three derivatives from the definition, and
each took a paragraph of algebra. That does not scale, and it does not need
to: the definition is a machine for \emph{establishing} rules, and once a rule
is established it is used.

This program has four of them and one more that is worth the other four put
together.

Start with the pattern F11 left you looking at. It found that $x^{2}$
differentiates to $2x$, that $mx$ differentiates to $m$, and that a constant
differentiates to $0$. Write $mx$ as $mx^{1}$ and the constant as $cx^{0}$,
and guess the rule for $x^{n}$.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\,x^{n} = n\,x^{n-1} \]
\end{ansblock}

\textbf{Bring the index down as a multiplier and reduce it by one.} Check it
against all three of F11's results: $x^{2} \to 2x^{1}$; $x^{1} \to 1x^{0} = 1$,
so $mx \to m$; $x^{0} \to 0x^{-1} = 0$, so a constant goes.

That is the \textbf{power rule}, and it holds for every $n$ \dash{} negative
and fractional included, which is more than the three cases can show and is
why it is stated rather than inferred. $\frac{1}{x}$ is $x^{-1}$, so it
differentiates to $-x^{-2}$; $\sqrt{x}$ is $x^{1/2}$, so it differentiates to
$\frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}$.

Note the notation that has just appeared. $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}$ is
read \enquote{the derivative with respect to $x$ of}, and it says which letter
is the variable \dash{} which will matter enormously in
Program~\ref{prog:P15}, where there is more than one. For now it means the
same as the prime.

Differentiate $x^{7}$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$7x^{6}$}
Now $\sqrt[3]{x}$, which is $x^{1/3}$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\dfrac{1}{3}x^{-2/3}$}
The index came down and went down by one: $\frac{1}{3} - 1 = -\frac{2}{3}$.

Two more rules, and both are so mild that the only reason to state them is
that the next two are not.

A \textbf{constant multiple} comes straight out: $(cf)' = c f'$. Stretching a
graph vertically by $c$ stretches all its slopes by $c$, which is
Program~\ref{prog:F05}'s outside-the-bracket move seen through a derivative.

A \textbf{sum} differentiates term by term: $(f + g)' = f' + g'$. Adding two
curves adds their heights at every point, so it adds their rates of change
too.

Differentiate $3x^{4} - 5x + 2$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$12x^{3} - 5$}
The power rule on each term, the constant multiple pulled out, and the $2$
gone because a constant has no slope.

\begin{note}
Every rule in this program is checked in
\code{code/f12\_chain\_rule.py} against a derivative computed \emph{without}
it \dash{} a \emph{central} difference, $\frac{f(x+h) - f(x-h)}{2h}$, at
$h = 10^{-5}$. This one agrees to $\val{f12.err.sum}$ over two hundred points.

That is what a finite difference is \emph{for}. Program~\ref{prog:F11} showed
it makes a bad definition; it makes an excellent independent check, provided
the step is chosen where the U-curve is low rather than as small as possible.

\textbf{But the step is not F11's.} That program swept the \emph{forward}
difference and found its best step at $\val{f11.fd.best.h}$; the central form
has a different U-curve, and the script sweeps it here rather than borrowing
the number. Its bottom sits three decades higher, which is where
$h = 10^{-5}$ comes from.
\end{note}

Differentiate $\frac{4}{x} + \sqrt{x}$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$-\frac{4}{x^{2}} + \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$}
Writing them as $4x^{-1}$ and $x^{1/2}$ first, which is the habit worth
building: \textbf{get everything into the form $x^{n}$ and the power rule does
the rest.}

So three rules cover every polynomial and every root. What they do not cover
is two functions \emph{multiplied}, and that is the next section.
\end{fr}

\section{Products and quotients}
\label{sec:F12-product}
\index{derivative!product and quotient rules}

\begin{fr}
A sum differentiates into a sum of derivatives. So here is the guess everybody
makes, and it is worth making it out loud before it is corrected.

$f(x) = x^{2}$ and $g(x) = x^{3}$. Guess the derivative of $f(x)g(x)$ by
differentiating each and multiplying, and then work out the true answer by
multiplying first and using the power rule. Do both at $x = \val{f12.trap.x}$.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
The guess gives $\val{f12.trap.wrong}$ and the truth is
$\val{f12.trap.right}$.
\end{ansblock}

The guess: $f' = 2x$ and $g' = 3x^{2}$, so $f'g' = 6x^{3}$, which at
$x = \val{f12.trap.x}$ is $\val{f12.trap.wrong}$. The truth: $fg = x^{5}$,
which differentiates to $5x^{4}$, and at $x = \val{f12.trap.x}$ that is
$\val{f12.trap.right}$.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Differentiation does not distribute over a product.} It distributes
over a sum, which is exactly why the guess is so natural \dash{} a rule that
worked one line ago is carried one step past its hypotheses, which is the same
shape as Program~\ref{prog:F02}'s $(a+b)^{2}$ and
Program~\ref{prog:F09}'s adding of lengths.

The two expressions cross at exactly $\val{f12.trap.agree}$ points, and both
are accidents of where $5x^{4}$ meets $6x^{3}$. Away from them the guess is
not merely inaccurate: it is a different \emph{power} of $x$, so the ratio
between it and the truth is $\frac{6}{5x}$ and depends entirely on where you
are standing. There is no constant factor to correct for and no range over
which the guess is reliably close.

The reason is worth one sentence, because it explains the correct rule rather
than merely replacing the wrong one. When $x$ moves a little, \emph{both}
factors move, and the product changes because of each of them. The guess
accounts for neither change on its own; it multiplies two rates, which is not
a rate of anything.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
So account for the two changes separately. Hold $g$ still and let $f$ move:
the product changes at rate $f'g$. Hold $f$ still and let $g$ move: it changes
at rate $fg'$. Both happen at once, so add them.

\[ (fg)' = f'g + fg' \]

That is the \textbf{product rule}. Check it on the case that just failed:
$f'g + fg' = 2x \cdot x^{3} + x^{2} \cdot 3x^{2} = 2x^{4} + 3x^{4} = 5x^{4}$,
which is right. Checked against a finite difference to
$\val{f12.err.product}$.

\mermaidfig{f12-product-not-product}{The rule that does distribute, the guess
it invites, and what actually happens instead.}{why a product is not a product
of derivatives}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Use it. Take $f(x) = (x+1)$ and $g(x) = (x-3)$, and apply the product rule
to $fg$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$2x - 2$}
From $1 \cdot (x-3) + (x+1) \cdot 1 = 2x - 2$. And you can check it the slow
way: $(x+1)(x-3) = x^{2} - 2x - 3$, which differentiates to $2x - 2$ by the
power rule.

That check is worth making a habit. \textbf{When a product can also be
expanded, the two routes must agree}, and disagreement means an arithmetic
slip rather than a choice of method.

Now the fourth rule, which is the product rule wearing a disguise. Differentiate
$\frac{f}{g}$.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
\[ \left(\frac{f}{g}\right)' = \frac{f'g - fg'}{g^{2}} \]
\end{ansblock}

The \textbf{quotient rule}. The numerator is the product rule with a minus,
and the order matters because of it: $f'g$ comes first.

Two things make it memorable rather than memorised. The minus is there
because, for a positive $f$, increasing the \emph{denominator} decreases the
whole, so $g$'s contribution has to come with the opposite sign from $f$'s.
That is a mnemonic and not a derivation \dash{} with $f$ negative the whole
\emph{rises} as $g$ does \dash{} and the derivation is further problem $2$,
which gets the rule out of the product rule in one line. And the $g^{2}$
underneath has to be there for the units to work: if $f$ is a loss and $g$ is
a count, then $\frac{f}{g}$ is a loss per item and its rate of change with
respect to $x$
cannot have $g$ appearing only once.

Differentiate $\frac{x^{2}}{x+3}$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\dfrac{2x(x+3) - x^{2}}{(x+3)^{2}}$}
Which simplifies to $\frac{x^{2} + 6x}{(x+3)^{2}}$ if you want it, and does
not need to. Checked to $\val{f12.err.quotient}$.

\begin{note}
The quotient rule is the least used of the four in this book, because most
quotients you will meet are better handled another way \dash{} a softmax is a
quotient and Program~\ref{prog:P18} differentiates it by a route that keeps
the answer readable. It is here for completeness and because the reasoning
behind its minus sign transfers.
\end{note}

That is four rules. Every one of them is about combining functions
\emph{side by side}: added, multiplied, divided. None of them is about putting
one function \emph{inside} another, and that is what a network is.
\end{fr}

\section{The chain rule}
\label{sec:F12-chain}
\index{chain rule!statement}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:F05} built a composition: $f(g(x))$, one machine's output
fed into another. Its frame 34 said in as many words that differentiating one
is this program's job.

Here is the question, and it is worth attempting before reading on. If $g$
changes at rate $g'$ and $f$ changes at rate $f'$, at what rate does $f(g(x))$
change as $x$ moves?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
\[ \bigl(f(g(x))\bigr)' = f'\bigl(g(x)\bigr) \cdot g'(x) \]
\end{ansblock}

\textbf{The rates multiply}, and the outer one is evaluated at the inner one's
\emph{value} rather than at $x$.

The reasoning is the one everybody's intuition already has. If $g$ moves three
times as fast as $x$, and $f$ moves twice as fast as $g$, then $f$ moves six
times as fast as $x$. Gears: two of them in mesh multiply their ratios. That
is the \textbf{chain rule}, and it is the only rule in this program that is
about composition.

The part that is easy to drop is \emph{where} $f'$ is evaluated. $f'$ is a
function, and the value it needs is $g(x)$, not $x$ \dash{} because that is
where $f$ actually is when the composition is being computed.

Differentiate $(3x + 1)^{4}$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$12(3x+1)^{3}$}
Outside is $u^{4}$, whose derivative is $4u^{3}$; evaluated at the inside it
is $4(3x+1)^{3}$. Inside is $3x+1$, whose derivative is $3$. Multiply:
$12(3x+1)^{3}$.

Check it the slow way if you like \dash{} expand $(3x+1)^{4}$ and
differentiate term by term \dash{} and it comes out the same, which is worth
doing once so that the rule stops feeling like a trick.

Differentiate $\sqrt{x^{2} + 1}$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+1}}$}
Outside is $u^{1/2}$ giving $\frac{1}{2}u^{-1/2}$, evaluated at $x^{2}+1$.
Inside is $x^{2}+1$ giving $2x$. Multiply and the $2$s cancel.

Now the one this book actually needs. Program~\ref{prog:F07} gave you the
logistic, $\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$, and measured its steepness
without ever differentiating it. The derivative itself needs the chain rule,
so this is where it arrives.

It is a quotient with a composition inside it, so it takes both of the last
two sections. Work it out, or take the answer and check it.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
\[ \sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)\bigl(1 - \sigma(z)\bigr) \]
\end{ansblock}

Write $\sigma = (1 + e^{-z})^{-1}$ and use the chain rule twice: the outside
$u^{-1}$ gives $-u^{-2}$, the inside $1 + e^{-z}$ gives $-e^{-z}$, and the two
minus signs cancel to leave $\frac{e^{-z}}{(1+e^{-z})^{2}}$. Then split that
into $\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}} \times \frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}}$, and the second
factor is $1 - \sigma(z)$.

Checked against a finite difference to $\val{f12.err.sigmoid}$ over two
hundred points from $-8$ to $8$.

\begin{note}
\textbf{The derivative is expressible in terms of the function itself}, which
is unusual and is not an accident of algebra. It is the reason a framework can
compute a logistic layer's gradient from the value it already stored on the
way forward, without recomputing anything or keeping the input. That is a
convenience specific to the logistic; what generalises, and what
Program~\ref{prog:P16} has to account for, is keeping the forward values at
all.
\end{note}

Notice how long that chain was. Reading $\sigma$ from the inside out, $z$
becomes $-z$, becomes $e^{-z}$, becomes $1 + e^{-z}$, becomes its reciprocal:
four links, and the rule was applied to every one of them. The rest of this
program does exactly that to $\val{f12.depth}$ layers, and nothing about the
argument changes \dash{} which is what a rule that composes buys.

What is the largest $\sigma'$ can be, and where?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{f12.sig.max}$, at $z = 0$}
Because $\sigma(1-\sigma)$ is a product of two numbers adding to $1$, and such
a product is largest when they are equal \dash{} at $\sigma = \frac{1}{2}$,
which is $z = 0$. So $\sigma' \le \frac{1}{4}$ \textbf{everywhere, with no
exceptions}, checked at sixteen thousand points.

That number is small and it is about to be multiplied by itself forty times.
Hold on to it.

One more differentiation first, and it is the unit this book keeps returning
to. Differentiate $\sigma(wx + b)$ with respect to $x$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\,\sigma(wx+b)
   = w\,\sigma(wx+b)\bigl(1 - \sigma(wx+b)\bigr) \]
\end{ansblock}

Chain rule: the outside is $\sigma$, whose derivative is
$\sigma(1-\sigma)$ evaluated at $wx+b$; the inside is $wx+b$, whose derivative
is $w$. Checked to $\val{f12.err.chain}$ at
$w = \val{f12.chain.w}$, $b = \val{f12.chain.b}$.

\textbf{That expression is the whole of one layer's contribution to a
gradient}, and every symbol in it has a job: $w$ because the layer scales its
input, and $\sigma(1-\sigma)$ because the layer bends it. Note what is
\emph{not} in it: $b$. The bias shifts where the bend happens and does not
change how hard it bends, which is why a bias never appears in a chain-rule
factor.
\end{fr}

\section{A network is a composition}
\label{sec:F12-network}
\index{chain rule!and backpropagation}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:F05} established that a deep network is a composition and
nothing else. Write two layers out:
\[ y = \sigma\bigl(w_2\,\sigma(w_1 x + b_1) + b_2\bigr) \]
and differentiate with respect to $x$.

It is worth knowing before you start that this is the only case you will have
to work. Nothing in the two-layer answer uses the fact that there are two
\dash{} the same steps apply to the third layer, and to the fourth, in the same
order \dash{} so what you write here is the general answer with a small $n$ in
it, and the rest of this program is that answer read at $\val{f12.depth}$.

Take it in two steps: what is the derivative of the inner layer, and what does
the outer one do to it?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}
   = \underbrace{w_2\,\sigma'(z_2)}_{\text{outer}} \cdot
     \underbrace{w_1\,\sigma'(z_1)}_{\text{inner}} \]
where $z_1 = w_1x + b_1$ and $z_2 = w_2\sigma(z_1) + b_2$.
\end{ansblock}

\textbf{One factor per layer, multiplied together}, and each factor is the
expression the previous section derived, evaluated at that layer's own input.

Add a third layer. How many factors?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Three}
And the pattern does not change: an $n$-layer network's derivative with
respect to its input is a product of $n$ factors, each of the form
$w_k\,\sigma'(z_k)$.

\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}
   = \prod_{k=1}^{n} w_k\,\sigma'(z_k) \]

using the product notation Program~\ref{prog:F04} gave you. That single
expression is what this program exists to reach.

\mermaidfig{f12-composition-chain}{A layer takes the last one's output, so the
whole thing is one function inside another and its derivative is a product.}
{why a network's derivative is a product}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Now the sentence.

\begin{aibox}
\textbf{Backpropagation is the chain rule applied to a composition of layers,
with the intermediate values kept rather than recomputed.} There is nothing
else in it.

Every factor in that product needs $z_k$, the input to layer $k$. Those values
were all computed on the way forward. So the algorithm is: go forward and keep
them; come back multiplying the factors as you go. \enquote{Backward} names
the direction the multiplication is accumulated in, and nothing more
mysterious.

Two consequences fall out immediately, and both are things people learn the
hard way. The activations have to be \emph{stored}, so memory grows with depth
and with batch size \dash{} which is what gradient checkpointing trades
against recomputation, and is Program~\ref{prog:P16}'s subject. And the
gradient with respect to a weight deep in the network involves every factor
between it and the loss, so nothing about training one layer is local to that
layer.
\end{aibox}

Which raises the question section 5 is about. Those factors are being
multiplied. What does a long product of numbers below one do?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{It goes to zero, fast}
And that is the whole of the vanishing-gradient problem, which the next
section makes arithmetic.

Before it, one caution about what has just been derived. This is all
\emph{one-dimensional}: one input, one output, one number per layer. A real
layer takes a vector and returns a vector, so each \enquote{factor} is a table
of numbers rather than one, and multiplying them is matrix multiplication
rather than arithmetic.

\textbf{The structure is identical and the objects are not.}
Program~\ref{prog:P16} does the vector version, where the factors are called
Jacobians and the question of which order to multiply them in turns out to
decide the cost of the whole computation. What transfers from here is the
shape: one factor per layer, multiplied, evaluated at values kept from the
forward pass.
\end{fr}

\section{What a long product does}
\label{sec:F12-vanishing}
\index{chain rule!vanishing and exploding factors}

\begin{fr}
Take the product from the last section with every weight equal to $1$, so each
factor is just $\sigma'(z_k)$.

Program~\ref{prog:F07} measured what a saturated logistic does: at
$\lvert z \rvert = 6$ it answers a change in its input with about one part in
$\val{f07.slope.ratio6}$ of the response it gives at the centre. That is one
layer. This program computes the same ratio from scratch and asserts it
matches F7's, so the two programs provably quote one computation.

Now put forty of them end to end. The point of asking for the best case
rather than a realistic one is that a best case is a \emph{bound}: whatever a
trained network actually does, it cannot do better than this, so a hopeless
best case settles the question without needing to know where any unit sits.

A network is $\val{f12.depth}$ layers deep and every unit is at its
\emph{best} \dash{} sitting exactly at the centre, where $\sigma'$ is at its
largest. What is the product?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{f12.vanish.bound}$}
$\val{f12.sig.max}$ raised to the power $\val{f12.depth}$.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{That is the best case}, and it is already hopeless. Every unit sitting
precisely at its most responsive point \dash{} which no trained network does
\dash{} still loses twenty-four orders of magnitude between the loss and the
first layer's input.

Put the units where Program~\ref{prog:F07} measured saturation instead, at
$\sigma' = \val{f07.slope.6}$, and the product falls below
$10^{-\val{f12.sat.exponent}}$.

\textbf{Whether that is a small number or no number at all depends on the
format the network is trained in.} In the $32$-bit arithmetic that usually
is, the smallest value there is at all is about
$\val{p01.fp32.minsub}$, so the product underflows to \emph{exactly} zero long
before layer forty and the first layers receive nothing rather than very
little. In $64$-bit it does not: the floor there is about
$\val{p01.fp64.minsub}$ and $10^{-\val{f12.sat.exponent}}$ sits comfortably
above it. Program~\ref{prog:P01} owns both floors, and the script asserts the
product falls between them.

The mechanism is not subtle and that is the point. \textbf{Nothing here is a
property of neural networks.} It is what happens when forty numbers below one
are multiplied, and F7 measured the numbers, and this program multiplied
them.
\end{trapbox}

\mermaidfig{f12-factors-multiply}{One flat layer is survivable; the product of
forty is not, and multiplying is all that happened.}{a gradient vanishing as a
product}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Now put the weights back. Each factor is $w_k\,\sigma'(z_k)$ rather than
$\sigma'(z_k)$, so a large weight could push a factor above one and the
product could grow instead of shrinking.

$\sigma'$ never exceeds $\val{f12.sig.max}$. So how large does $w$ have to be
before a layer can amplify at all?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\lvert w \rvert$ greater than $\val{f12.explode.w}$}
Because the factor's size is at most $\frac{\lvert w \rvert}{4}$, and that
reaches $1$ exactly when $\lvert w \rvert = \val{f12.explode.w}$. The modulus
is not decoration: a weight of $-8$ amplifies exactly as hard as one of $8$,
because what compounds is how much each factor multiplies by and not which way
it points. Checked at four weights across sixteen thousand values of $z$ each.

\textbf{That is a sharp, checkable threshold and it is worth having.} A
logistic layer with a weight of $3$, or of $-3$, cannot make a gradient grow,
no matter where its input sits or how many such layers there are. The two
failure modes are not symmetric: vanishing needs nothing but depth, and
exploding needs a weight past a specific size.

At $w = \val{f12.explode.big.w}$ the best-case factor is
$\val{f12.explode.big.factor}$, so $\val{f12.depth}$ layers multiply the
gradient by $\val{f12.explode.big.depth}$ \dash{} and \emph{that} is what
\enquote{the gradients exploded} means arithmetically.

\begin{aibox}
Both numbers explain choices you have seen made. Gradient clipping, which
Program~\ref{prog:F06} defined as an operation, is a response to the second:
it bounds the product after the fact rather than preventing it.

Careful initialisation is a response to both, and it is a statement about the
distribution the weights are drawn from rather than about any one weight
\dash{} which is why Program~\ref{prog:P25} is where it belongs, since it
needs variance.

And the architecture's own answer \dash{} a path from the loss to the early
layers that is not a long product at all \dash{} is the residual connection,
which Program~\ref{prog:P32} derives. What this program supplies is the reason
any of them is needed, which is a product and a bound on one of its factors.
\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
One honest limit on all of that, because it is easy to over-read.

The bound $\sigma' \le \val{f12.sig.max}$ is exact and the product is exact,
so the arithmetic is not in doubt. What is a modelling choice is
\emph{everything else}: that every layer is a logistic, that the weights are
scalars, that the network is a plain chain with no other path from the loss to
the input.

\textbf{Change any of those and the product changes.} A $\relu$ has a
derivative of $1$ on its positive side, so a chain of them contributes no
shrinking factor at all there \dash{} which is most of the argument for using
it, and Program~\ref{prog:F07} put the flat side of that trade on the record.
A residual connection adds a path whose factors are $1$. A normalisation layer
changes the $z_k$ the factors are evaluated at.

So take from this section the mechanism and the bound, and be careful with the
conclusion. \emph{A deep stack of saturating units has a vanishing gradient}
is established here. \emph{Deep networks have vanishing gradients} is not:
that was a fair description of the networks the argument was first made about,
and each of the three changes above was adopted partly because it stops being
one.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
A closing observation, and it is about the shape of the whole program.

Four rules and one composition rule. The four are about functions side by
side and are, honestly, bookkeeping. The fifth is about functions inside one
another, and it produced: the derivative of the logistic, the derivative of a
unit, the derivative of a network, backpropagation, and both of the failure
modes that make training deep networks hard.

\textbf{That is not because the chain rule is deep. It is because composition
is what a network is}, and a rule about composition is therefore a rule about
everything the network does.

Program~\ref{prog:F13} closes the Foundation part by going the other way
\dash{} accumulating rather than differentiating \dash{} and after that the
book stops assuming nothing and starts building.
\end{fr}

\sumitem{1--3}{\result{\textbf{The power rule}:
  $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}x^{n} = nx^{n-1}$, for every $n$ including
  negative and fractional. It contains all three of
  Program~\ref{prog:F11}'s results as special cases, and the habit that makes
  it do all the work is \textbf{get everything into the form $x^{n}$ first}}.}
\sumitem{4--6}{\result{$(cf)' = cf'$ and $(f+g)' = f' + g'$. Every rule in this program
  is checked against a derivative computed \emph{without} it, by a central
  difference at $h = 10^{-5}$ \dash{} which is what a finite difference is
  for, and is Program~\ref{prog:F11}'s lesson put to work rather than merely
  warned about. \textbf{The step is swept here rather than borrowed}: F11
  measured the \emph{forward} difference and its best step is
  $\val{f11.fd.best.h}$, and the central form's U-curve bottoms three decades
  higher}.}
\sumitem{7--8}{\result{\textbf{Differentiation does not distribute over a product.} On
  $x^{2}$ and $x^{3}$ at $x = \val{f12.trap.x}$ the truth is
  $\val{f12.trap.right}$ and the first guess gives $\val{f12.trap.wrong}$, and
  the two cross at exactly $\val{f12.trap.agree}$ points. Away from those the
  guess is a different \emph{power} of $x$, so their ratio is $\frac{6}{5x}$
  and there is no constant factor that would correct it. Same shape as F2's
  $(a+b)^{2}$ and F9's adding of lengths: a rule carried one step past its
  hypotheses}.}
\sumitem{9--13}{\result{\textbf{$(fg)' = f'g + fg'$}, because when $x$ moves
  \emph{both} factors move and the product changes on account of each; and
  $\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)' = \frac{f'g - fg'}{g^{2}}$, whose minus is a
  mnemonic from a positive $f$ \dash{} raising the denominator lowers the
  whole \dash{} and whose derivation is the product rule applied to
  $f g^{-1}$. When a product can also be expanded, the two routes must agree
  \dash{} a free check worth making a habit}.}
\sumitem{14--16}{\result{\textbf{The chain rule: $\bigl(f(g(x))\bigr)' =
  f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)$.} The rates multiply, like gears in mesh, and the
  outer derivative is evaluated at the \emph{inner one's value}, which is the
  part that gets dropped}.}
\sumitem{17--18}{\result{\textbf{$\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))$}, checked to
  $\val{f12.err.sigmoid}$. The derivative is expressible in terms of the
  function itself, which is why a framework can compute a logistic layer's
  gradient from the value it already stored going forward rather than
  evaluating anything a second time. That is a saving on recomputation;
  \emph{reverse} mode is a separate matter of which end the factors are
  multiplied from, and it is Program~\ref{prog:P16}'s}.}
\sumitem{19--20}{\result{\textbf{$\sigma' \le \val{f12.sig.max}$ everywhere},
  with equality only at $z = 0$, because $\sigma(1-\sigma)$ is a product of
  two numbers adding to one. And one unit's contribution is
  $w\,\sigma(wx+b)(1-\sigma(wx+b))$: $w$ because the layer scales,
  $\sigma(1-\sigma)$ because it bends, and \emph{no $b$}, because a bias moves
  where the bend is and not how hard it bends}.}
\sumitem{21--23}{\result{An $n$-layer network's derivative with respect to its input
  is $\prod_{k=1}^{n} w_k\,\sigma'(z_k)$ \dash{} \textbf{one factor per layer,
  multiplied together}, each evaluated at that layer's own input}.}
\sumitem{24--25}{\result{\textbf{Backpropagation is the chain rule applied to a
  composition of layers, with the intermediate values kept rather than
  recomputed.} There is nothing else in it, and \enquote{backward} names the
  direction the multiplication accumulates in. Two consequences: activations
  must be stored, so memory grows with depth and batch; and a deep weight's
  gradient involves every factor between it and the loss, so nothing about
  training one layer is local to it. The vector version, where each factor is
  a Jacobian and the multiplication order decides the cost, is
  Program~\ref{prog:P16}'s}.}
\sumitem{26--27}{\result{\textbf{$\val{f12.depth}$ layers at their very best give
  $\val{f12.sig.max}^{\val{f12.depth}} = \val{f12.vanish.bound}$}, and no
  trained network sits at its best. Put the units where
  Program~\ref{prog:F07} measured saturation \dash{} $\val{f07.slope.6}$, one
  part in $\val{f07.slope.ratio6}$ of the centre response, and this program
  recomputes both from scratch and asserts they match F7's \dash{} and the
  product falls below $10^{-\val{f12.sat.exponent}}$, which is \emph{exactly}
  zero in $32$-bit arithmetic long before layer forty and an ordinary number
  in $64$-bit}.}
\sumitem{28--29}{\result{\textbf{A logistic chain cannot amplify unless
  $\lvert w \rvert > \val{f12.explode.w}$}, because the factor's size is
  $\lvert w \rvert\sigma' \le \frac{\lvert w \rvert}{4}$. The two failure
  modes are not symmetric: vanishing needs nothing but depth, exploding needs
  a specific weight. At $w = \val{f12.explode.big.w}$ the factor is
  $\val{f12.explode.big.factor}$ and $\val{f12.depth}$ layers multiply by
  $\val{f12.explode.big.depth}$}.}
\sumitem{30--31}{\result{The bound and the product are exact; \textbf{that every layer
  is a logistic in a plain chain is a modelling choice}. A $\relu$ contributes
  a factor of $1$ on its positive side, a residual connection adds a path of
  $1$s (Program~\ref{prog:P32}), and a normalisation changes the $z_k$. \emph{A
  deep stack of saturating units has a vanishing gradient} is established
  here; \emph{deep networks have vanishing gradients} is not, and each of
  those three changes was adopted partly because it stops being true. Four
  rules are bookkeeping; the fifth produced backpropagation and both of its
  failure modes, because composition is what a network is}.}
\end{summarybox}

\begin{testexercises}
\item Differentiate $x^{9}$, $\frac{1}{x^{3}}$ and $\sqrt[4]{x}$.
  \answerto{$9x^{8}$, $-3x^{-4}$ and $\frac{1}{4}x^{-3/4}$. Write the last two
  as $x^{-3}$ and $x^{1/4}$ first.}
\item Differentiate $5x^{3} - \frac{2}{x} + 7$.
  \answerto{$15x^{2} + \frac{2}{x^{2}}$. The middle term is $-2x^{-1}$, whose
  derivative is $+2x^{-2}$.}
\item Differentiate $(x^{2}+1)(x-4)$ twice over: by the product rule, and by
  expanding first.
  \answerto{Both give $3x^{2} - 8x + 1$. The product rule gives
  $2x(x-4) + (x^{2}+1)$; expanding gives $x^{3} - 4x^{2} + x - 4$.}
\item Differentiate $\frac{x+1}{x-1}$.
  \answerto{$\frac{(x-1) - (x+1)}{(x-1)^{2}} = \frac{-2}{(x-1)^{2}}$.}
\item Differentiate $(2x - 5)^{6}$.
  \answerto{$12(2x-5)^{5}$: the outside gives $6(2x-5)^{5}$ and the inside
  gives $2$.}
\item Differentiate $e^{3x}$, given that $e^{x}$ differentiates to itself.
  \answerto{$3e^{3x}$, by the chain rule with an inside derivative of $3$.}
\item Differentiate $\sigma(-x)$.
  \answerto{$-\sigma(-x)\bigl(1 - \sigma(-x)\bigr)$. The inside derivative is
  $-1$.}
\item Give $\sigma'$ at $z = 0$ and say why no value of $z$ beats it.
  \answerto{$\val{f12.sig.max}$. $\sigma(1-\sigma)$ is a product of two numbers
  adding to one, and such a product is largest when they are equal.}
\item A unit is $\sigma(3x - 1)$. Give its derivative at $x = \frac{1}{3}$.
  \answerto{$3 \times \val{f12.sig.max} = \num{0.75}$, because at
  $x = \frac{1}{3}$ the inside is zero, which is where $\sigma'$ peaks.}
\item Write the derivative of a three-layer logistic network with respect to
  its input, using the product notation.
  \answerto{$\prod_{k=1}^{3} w_k\,\sigma'(z_k)$, with $z_k$ the input to
  layer $k$.}
\item Why does a bias never appear in a chain-rule factor?
  \answerto{Because the inside of each layer is $w_kx + b_k$, whose derivative
  with respect to $x$ is $w_k$ \dash{} the bias is a constant and constants
  differentiate to zero.}
\item Ten logistic layers all have weight $1$ and all sit at their centres.
  Give the product.
  \answerto{$\val{f12.sig.max}^{10}$, which is about $\num{9.5e-7}$. Ten
  layers is already a factor of a million.}
\item A logistic layer has weight $\num{3.5}$. Can it amplify a gradient?
  \answerto{No. The factor is at most $\frac{\num{3.5}}{4}$, which is below
  one, so no arrangement of its input can make it grow.}
\item Say in one sentence what has to be stored during the forward pass and
  why.
  \answerto{Each layer's input $z_k$, because every chain-rule factor needs it
  and recomputing them would mean running the network again per layer.}
\end{testexercises}

\begin{furtherproblems}
\item Derive the product rule from the definition, by expanding
  \[ \frac{f(x+h)g(x+h) - f(x)g(x)}{h} \]
  after adding and subtracting $f(x+h)g(x)$ to the numerator.
  \answerto{The expression becomes
  $\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}g(x) + f(x+h)\frac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}$, whose two parts
  settle on $f'g$ and $fg'$. The trick of adding and subtracting a middle term
  is worth recognising; it is how most product-shaped rules are derived.}
\item Show that the quotient rule follows from the product rule applied to
  $f \cdot g^{-1}$, given the chain rule.
  \answerto{$(fg^{-1})' = f'g^{-1} + f(-g^{-2}g') =
  \frac{f'}{g} - \frac{fg'}{g^{2}}$, which is $\frac{f'g - fg'}{g^{2}}$ over a
  common denominator. So it is not a fifth rule.}
\item $f(g(x))$ where both $f$ and $g$ are strictly increasing and
  differentiable. What can you say about the sign of the composition's
  derivative?
  \answerto{It is never negative, and that is as far as it goes: a strictly
  increasing function has $f' \ge 0$ rather than $f' > 0$, because $x^{3}$
  climbs everywhere and has a derivative of $0$ at the origin. So the product
  of two such factors is $\ge 0$ and may be zero. What survives is
  Program~\ref{prog:F05}'s result: the composition is increasing, which is a
  statement about order rather than about a sign.}
\item Show that $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)\sigma(-z)$, and say what that form
  makes obvious.
  \answerto{$1 - \sigma(z) = \sigma(-z)$, so the two forms are the same. It
  makes the symmetry obvious: $\sigma'$ is even, so a unit is exactly as
  responsive at $z$ as at $-z$.}
\item Program~\ref{prog:F07} gave $\tanh x = 2\sigma(2x) - 1$. Differentiate it
  and simplify.
  \answerto{$4\sigma(2x)(1 - \sigma(2x))$, whose maximum is $4 \times
  \val{f12.sig.max} = 1$ at $x = 0$ \dash{} which is the factor of four F7
  measured, arriving here as a consequence rather than an observation.}
\item A chain of $\tanh$ layers with unit weights. Give the best-case
  per-layer factor, and say what that does to the vanishing argument.
  \answerto{$1$, from the previous problem. So the best case does not shrink
  at all \dash{} but $\tanh$ saturates harder than the logistic, so away from
  the centre the factors are smaller. The argument survives; the bound does
  not.}
\item Two networks are $10$ and $\val{f12.depth}$ layers deep, otherwise
  identical logistic chains at their best. Give the ratio of their gradients.
  \answerto{$\val{f12.sig.max}^{-30}$, which is $4^{30}$ \dash{} about
  $10^{18}$. Depth costs exponentially, not proportionally.}
\item Explain why gradient clipping addresses the exploding case and not the
  vanishing one.
  \answerto{Clipping bounds a gradient from above, which is what an exploded
  one violates. A vanished gradient is already inside any bound; there is
  nothing for a clip to do to it.}
\item The chain rule multiplies factors. Reverse mode multiplies them from the
  loss end and forward mode from the input end. Without doing the counting,
  say what would decide which is cheaper.
  \answerto{The shapes at the two ends \dash{} how many outputs against how
  many inputs. A scalar loss and many parameters is one extreme, and
  Program~\ref{prog:P16} does the counting.}
\item This program's argument is arithmetic and its conclusion is not
  universal. State precisely what is established and what is not.
  \answerto{Established: a chain of logistic layers whose weights are smaller
  than $\val{f12.explode.w}$ in size has a gradient bounded by a product of
  factors below
  one, which falls exponentially in depth. Not established: anything about
  networks with other activations, residual paths, or normalisation, each of
  which changes a factor or adds a path.}
\end{furtherproblems}
