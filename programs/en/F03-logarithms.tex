\label{prog:F03}
\index{logarithm!definition}

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{1--7}{say in one sentence what a logarithm is, evaluate one by hand
  where the answer is a whole number, and say why its argument must be
  positive}
\outcome{8--14}{convert between base two, base $e$ and base ten, and say what
  changing the base does to the number}
\outcome{15--24}{turn a product into a sum, a quotient into a difference and an
  index into a multiplier, and do all three backwards}
\outcome{25--29}{undo an exponential, solve $b^{x} = c$ for $x$, and move
  between a probability and a loss in either direction}
\outcome{30--36}{say why the probability of a sequence is computed as a sum of
  logarithms, and work out the length at which a given float format stops being
  able to hold the product at all}
\outcome{37--42}{compute a perplexity from a mean loss, say what a stated
  perplexity claims about a model and what it does not, and tell a summed
  log-probability from a mean one and an arithmetic mean from a geometric one}
\outcome{43--48}{read a logarithmic axis, including what a distance along one
  measures, and say what a straight line on log-log axes means}
\end{outcomes}

\begin{quiz}
\item Evaluate $\logb{2} 64$ and $\logb{10} 1000$. \teachesat{2--3}
  \answerto{$6$ and $3$. Each is an exponent: $2^{6} = 64$ and
  $10^{3} = 1000$.}
\item What is $\logb{b} 1$, for any base $b$, and why? \teachesat{2--3}
  \answerto{$0$, always. It is forced by $b^{0} = 1$, which
  Program~\ref{prog:F01} established, so it is not a convention.}
\item What is $\logb{2}(-8)$? \teachesat{4--5}
  \answerto{It does not exist. No power of $2$ is negative, so nothing answers
  the question the logarithm asks.}
\item What does \code{math.log(1000)} return? \teachesat{8--9}
  \answerto{$\val{f03.ln.thousand}$, the logarithm to base $e$. If you
  expected $3$, you read the name as the base-ten logarithm your schooling
  writes $\mfalogplain 1000$; both readings apply a convention correctly, and
  the notation does not say which one is meant.}
\item Write $\logb{2} x$ in terms of $\ln x$. \teachesat{10--11}
  \answerto{$\logb{2} x = \frac{\ln x}{\ln 2}$, and $1/\ln 2$ is the constant
  $\val{f03.inv.ln.two}$.}
\item A loss of $\val{f02.loss.nats}$ nats \dash{} what is it in bits?
  \teachesat{11--12}
  \answerto{$\val{f03.nats.to.bits}$ bits. One measurement, two units.}
\item Write $\ln(x^{3}y)$ as a sum of logarithms. \teachesat{16--18}
  \answerto{$3\ln x + \ln y$.}
\item Simplify $\ln(a + b)$. \teachesat{20--21}
  \answerto{It does not simplify. There is no rule for the logarithm of a sum,
  and $\ln a + \ln b$ is the logarithm of the \emph{product}.}
\item Make $p$ the subject of $L = -\ln p$. \teachesat{25--27}
  \answerto{$p = e^{-L}$. Negate both sides, then apply $e$ to both sides.}
\item A sequence of $\val{f03.seq.tokens}$ tokens is given probability
  $\val{f02.prob.from.loss}$ at each step. Why is the probability of the whole
  sequence not computed by multiplying? \teachesat{30--32}
  \answerto{The product is about $\val{f03.seq.prod}$, which no float format
  holds. In float32 the running product is exactly zero after
  $\val{f03.f32.zero.at}$ tokens, with no error raised.}
\item A model's mean negative log-likelihood is $\val{f02.loss.nats}$ nats.
  What is its perplexity? \teachesat{37--38}
  \answerto{$\val{f03.ppl}$ \dash{} the exponential of the mean loss.}
\item On an axis whose ticks read $1$, $10$, $100$, equally spaced, what value
  sits halfway between the $10$ tick and the $100$ tick? \teachesat{43--44}
  \answerto{$\val{f03.axis.mid}$, the geometric mean, not $55$. Equal distances
  on that axis are equal ratios.}
\item Which is the bigger fall, $\num{4.0}$ to $\num{2.0}$ or $\num{0.5}$ to
  $\num{0.25}$ \dash{} and on which axis? \teachesat{45--46}
  \answerto{On a linear axis the first, by a factor of eight. On a logarithmic
  axis they are the same gap, because both are a halving.}
\item A quantity plots as a straight line on log-log axes with gradient $2$. What
  is the relationship between the two quantities? \teachesatone{47}
  \answerto{A power law, $y = ax^{2}$. The gradient of the line is the exponent.}
\end{quiz}

\section{What a logarithm is}
\label{sec:F03-what}
\index{logarithm!as an exponent}
\index{indices!read backwards}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:F01} asked what $2^{5}$ is, and you answered $32$. This
program asks the same question from the other end.

\emph{Two to what power is 32?}

The answer is $5$ again, and the name for it is a \emph{logarithm}. It is
written $\logb{2} 32 = 5$ and read: the exponent you must raise $2$ to in order
to get $32$.

In general, $\logb{b} x$ is the exponent that turns $b$ into $x$:
\[ b^{y} = x \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \logb{b} x = y \]
Two lines, one fact, written from the two ends. Everything else in this program
follows from reading that pair in whichever direction is convenient.

What is $\logb{2} 64$?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\logb{2} 64 = 6$}
Because $2^{6} = 64$, and that is the whole of the reasoning. There is no
technique here to acquire.

One sentence is worth carrying out of this frame and into the rest of the book:
\textbf{a logarithm is an exponent, and nothing else.} Every rule in
\S\ref{sec:F03-laws} is a rule about exponents wearing different clothes, and a
reader who keeps hold of that sentence never has to memorise one of them.

Now two more, and the second is not a trick question.

What is $\logb{10} 1000$, and what is $\logb{10} 1$?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$\logb{10} 1000 = 3$, because $10^{3} = 1000$.

$\logb{10} 1 = 0$, because $10^{0} = 1$.
\end{ansblock}

That second one holds for every base, and it is not a convention anybody chose:
$b^{0} = 1$ is Program~\ref{prog:F01}'s own law, so $\logb{b} 1 = 0$ whatever
$b$ is. The companion fact is $\logb{b} b = 1$, from $b^{1} = b$.

Three to fill in, and read each of them as \emph{what exponent gets me there}
rather than as a lookup:
\[ \logb{2} 8 = \blank \qquad \logb{10} 100 = \blank \qquad
   \logb{5} 25 = \blank \]
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$3$, \quad $2$, \quad $2$. \quad Respectively $2^{3} = 8$, $10^{2} = 100$ and
$5^{2} = 25$.
\end{ansblock}

Now the question that decides what a logarithm is allowed to eat.

A logarithm asks \emph{what exponent}, so it can only answer where an exponent
exists. Raise a positive base to any real power at all \dash{} positive,
negative, fractional \dash{} and what comes back is positive. Never zero, never
negative.

So: what is $\logb{2} 0$, and what is $\logb{2}(-8)$?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Neither exists. No power of $2$ is zero and no power of $2$ is negative, so
neither question has an answer to give.

The domain of $\logb{b}$ is the strictly positive reals, and the boundary is a
cliff rather than a slope: as $x$ falls towards zero, $\logb{b} x$ falls
without bound.
\end{ansblock}

\begin{warning}
Your tooling does not agree with itself about this, and the disagreement is
silent. Four calls:

\begin{itemize}
\item \code{math.log(0)} raises \code{ValueError: math domain error};
\item \code{math.log(-8)} raises the same \code{ValueError};
\item \code{np.log(0.0)} returns \code{-inf} and emits
  \code{RuntimeWarning: divide by zero encountered in log};
\item \code{np.log(-8.0)} returns \code{nan} and emits
  \code{RuntimeWarning: invalid value encountered in log}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Two undefined quantities, three different behaviours, in two libraries
you import into the same file.} The standard library stops; the array library
hands you a value and carries on. A \code{nan} that entered a training run
here will surface somewhere else entirely, as a loss that has stopped being a
number, and by then nothing points back at the logarithm that made it.
\end{warning}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The other thing worth knowing before any rules arrive is how slowly a logarithm
grows. Take four values of $x$ spread over eight decades:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rr}
\toprule
$x$ & $\logb{10} x$ \\
\midrule
$10$ & $1$ \\
$1000$ & $3$ \\
$10^{6}$ & $6$ \\
$10^{9}$ & $9$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{A hundred-million-fold increase in $x$ moves the logarithm by eight.}
That is the property every use in this program rests on: a logarithm takes a
range too wide to hold and hands back a range you can write on a page, plot on
an axis, or store in a float.

Here is the first thing it buys. Program~\ref{prog:F01} compared $2^{80}$ with
$10^{24}$ by writing out all twenty-five digits of $2^{80}$.

How many decimal digits has $2^{80}$, using logarithms and one multiplication?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{f03.two.eighty.digits}$ digits}
The number of decimal digits of a positive integer $n$ is
$\lfloor \logb{10} n \rfloor + 1$, and $\logb{10} 2^{80}$ is $80$ copies of
$\logb{10} 2$ added together:
\[ 80 \times \val{f03.log10.two} = \val{f03.two.eighty.exp} \]
Take the floor, add one, and there are $\val{f03.two.eighty.digits}$ digits.

Read the same number the other way and it settles Program~\ref{prog:F01}'s
question directly. The exponent is $\val{f03.two.eighty.exp}$, so $2^{80}$ is
$10^{\val{f03.two.eighty.exp}}$ rather than $10^{24}$ \dash{} it exceeds
$10^{24}$ by $\val{f01.two.eighty.err.pct}$\%, which is exactly the figure
Program~\ref{prog:F01} obtained by dividing two twenty-five-digit integers.

\textbf{One multiplication replaced a twenty-five-digit comparison}, and it did
so because $\val{f03.log10.two}$ is a constant you can carry. It is the most
useful number in this program and it returns in \S\ref{sec:F03-scales} wearing
a different hat.
\end{fr}

\section{The base, and why this book always writes it}
\label{sec:F03-base}
\index{logarithm!change of base}
\index{notation!bits and nats}

\begin{fr}
Three bases carry all of the traffic in this field. Base two, because a bit is
a two-way choice. Base $e$, because it is what calculus produces and what your
library computes by default. Base ten, because a decade is how orders of
magnitude are counted.

Before you read on, cover the rest of the page, and answer this from memory
rather than from a machine.

\textbf{Write down the value of $\mfalogplain 1000$.}
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
There are two answers, and which one you wrote says where you were educated.

$\mfalogplain 1000 = 3$, if you read the bare mark as base ten.

$\mfalogplain 1000 = \val{f03.ln.thousand}$, if you read it as base $e$.
\end{ansblock}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{The notation is defective, and you are not.} Both readings are correct
applications of a convention, and the two conventions are not shared.

Polish schooling teaches that a bare logarithm means base ten; that is what
tables, examination papers and every textbook on the shelf mean by it.
Machine-learning writing means base $e$, and its own libraries agree \dash{}
\code{math.log(1000)} returns $\val{f03.ln.thousand}$, while it takes
\code{math.log10(1000)} to get $3$.

So a reader who has been taught one convention and is reading a paper written
under the other is not confused about logarithms. They have been handed three
characters that mean two things, in a document that does not say which.

The reasoning that makes it dangerous is that neither party ever notices. A
convention you learned young does not feel like a convention; it feels like
what the symbol says.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{notationbox}
\textbf{This book never writes a bare logarithm.} The base is written every
single time: $\ln$ for base $e$, $\logb{2}$ for base two, $\logb{10}$ for base
ten. The build refuses a bare mark rather than trusting anybody to remember,
and the refusal is lifted in exactly the places where the ambiguity is itself
the subject: this section, the Appendix~\ref{app:A} answer to this program's
entry Quiz, and \S\ref{sec:appB-decisions}, which states the rule for the book
as a whole.

It costs two characters and it removes a class of error entirely. What that
class costs is not hypothetical: entropy is quoted in bits, cross-entropy in
nats, and Programs~\ref{prog:P29} and~\ref{prog:P30} sit next to each other.
\end{notationbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Moving between the three is one derivation, and it is worth doing rather than
memorising, because the memorised form is the one people write upside down.

Start from what a logarithm says. If $y = \logb{b} x$ then $b^{y} = x$. Take
logarithms to some other base $c$ of both sides, and use the third law from
\S\ref{sec:F03-laws} to bring the index down:
\[ \logb{c} x = \logb{c} \left(b^{y}\right) = y \, \logb{c} b \]
Divide, and $y$ is on its own:
\[ \logb{b} x = \frac{\logb{c} x}{\logb{c} b} \]
The base you want is on the bottom. That is the half people invert, and the
derivation is two lines, so derive it.

Use it: write $\logb{2} 10$ in terms of natural logarithms, and say what it is.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\logb{2} 10 = \dfrac{\ln 10}{\ln 2} = \val{f03.log2.ten}$}
Look at what the general form does when $x$ varies and the two bases do not.
The denominator $\logb{c} b$ is a constant, so
\[ \logb{b} x = \text{constant} \times \logb{c} x \]
\textbf{Changing the base multiplies every value by one fixed number.} It does
not bend the curve, move its zero or change which of two quantities is the
larger. Two consequences follow immediately, and the second is the one that
bites.

The shape of a plotted logarithm can never tell you its base \dash{} every
logarithm curve is every other one scaled vertically.

And the one constant worth carrying is $\ln 2 = \val{f03.ln.two}$, with its
reciprocal $1 / \ln 2 = \val{f03.inv.ln.two}$. Those two convert between nats
and bits, which are the units of base $e$ and of base two.

A model reports a loss of $\val{f02.loss.nats}$ nats. What is that in bits?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{f03.nats.to.bits}$ bits}
Multiply by $1/\ln 2$. Nothing about the model changed: it is one measurement
in two units, exactly as a distance is one distance in metres or in feet.

Which way round the constant goes is the part worth drilling, so fill in both
directions for a quantity you have not seen yet:
\[ \logb{2} 7 = \blank \times \ln 7 \qquad\qquad
   \ln 7 = \blank \times \logb{2} 7 \]
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$\val{f03.inv.ln.two}$ and $\val{f03.ln.two}$ respectively.

The way to keep them the right way round is not to remember the constants but
to remember which unit is smaller. A bit is a smaller unit than a nat, so the
same quantity carries a \emph{larger} number of bits than of nats \dash{} and
the constant that produces the bigger number, $\val{f03.inv.ln.two}$, is the
one greater than $1$.
\end{ansblock}

\begin{aibox}
In code the base is either in the function name or an explicit argument, and
never a convention. \code{math.log} and \code{np.log} default to base $e$;
\code{np.log2} and \code{np.log10} say which base they are; and
\code{math.log(x, base)} takes one. That last form is the reason the named
functions exist: \code{math.log(1000, 10)} returns
$\num{2.9999999999999996}$, where \code{math.log10(1000)} returns $3$.

That is a better state of affairs than the papers are in, and it has a
practical consequence when you are reimplementing one. A line that reads
$\mfalogplain p$ in a paper is ambiguous; the line \code{np.log(p)} in the
repository is not. When the two disagree about a reported number by a factor
near $\val{f03.inv.ln.two}$, the repository is the one telling the truth about
what was computed.
\end{aibox}

\yourturn
Three conversions.

Given $\logb{10} 1000 = 3$ and $\logb{10} 2 = \val{f03.log10.two}$, what is
$\logb{2} 1000$?

A loss is reported as $\val{f03.nats.to.bits}$ bits. What is it in nats?

For one and the same quantity, which is the larger number \dash{} the count in
bits or the count in nats?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$\logb{2} 1000 = \frac{3}{\val{f03.log10.two}} = \val{f03.log2.thousand}$. The
base you want goes on the bottom.

$\val{f03.nats.to.bits}$ bits is $\val{f02.loss.nats}$ nats: multiply by
$\ln 2$, which is the conversion of the previous frame run backwards.

The count in bits, always, and by the factor $\val{f03.inv.ln.two}$.
\end{ansblock}

That last answer is worth more than it looks, and \S\ref{sec:F03-perplexity}
comes back to it with two model cards and a number attached. A quantity
reported without its base is not a measurement; it is a measurement and a
coin toss.
\end{fr}

\section{The three laws, which are the index laws read backwards}
\label{sec:F03-laws}
\index{logarithm!laws of}
\index{troubleshooting!logarithm of a sum}

\begin{fr}
Every rule in this section is a rule you already own.
Program~\ref{prog:F01} multiplied two powers of one base by adding their
exponents:
\[ 2^{3} \times 2^{4} = 2^{7} \]
Now throw away everything except the exponents. What is left is $3 + 4 = 7$.

Those three exponents have names. $3$ is $\logb{2} 8$, $4$ is $\logb{2} 16$,
and $7$ is $\logb{2} 128$. So the line above, read as a statement about
exponents, says
\[ \logb{2}(8 \times 16) = \logb{2} 8 + \logb{2} 16 \]
\textbf{The index law and the logarithm law are one statement.} One is about
the numbers and one is about their exponents, and nobody proved anything twice.
Which means there is nothing in this section to learn that you did not learn in
Program~\ref{prog:F01}, and there is a great deal to practise.

Fill these in, and check the arithmetic afterwards:
\[ \logb{2}(4 \times 32) = \blank + \blank \]
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$\logb{2} 4 + \logb{2} 32 = 2 + 5 = 7$, and $4 \times 32 = 128$, whose
logarithm to base two is $7$. Both sides agree, as they must.
\end{ansblock}

In general, and for any base. Write $x = e^{a}$ and $y = e^{b}$, which is
always possible for positive $x$ and $y$, so that $a = \ln x$ and $b = \ln y$.
Then $xy = e^{a}e^{b} = e^{a+b}$, and reading that as exponents gives the first
law:
\[ \ln(xy) = \ln x + \ln y \]
\textbf{A logarithm turns multiplication into addition.} That sentence is the
whole of this program's argument, and everything after
\S\ref{sec:F03-undoing} is a consequence of it.

The second law needs no new idea at all. What is $\ln(x/y)$?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\ln(x/y) = \ln x - \ln y$}
Same derivation, one sign different: $x/y = e^{a}/e^{b} = e^{a-b}$, and
Program~\ref{prog:F01}'s division law subtracts the exponents.

So a quotient becomes a difference. Notice that this is where the domain of
\S\ref{sec:F03-what} starts earning its keep: $x$ and $y$ are both positive, so
the quotient is positive too and the left-hand side exists whenever the right
does.

One law left, and you can guess it from the same source. What is $\ln(x^{n})$?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\ln(x^{n}) = n \ln x$}
$x^{n}$ is $n$ copies of $x$ multiplied together, so its logarithm is $n$
copies of $\ln x$ added together. The index comes down and becomes a
multiplier, and that is not a separate fact from the first law \dash{} it is
the first law applied $n$ times.

The third law is the one that does the most work later, because it is what
turns an exponential into a straight line in \S\ref{sec:F03-scales} and what
makes a maximum-likelihood objective tractable in
Program~\ref{prog:P26}.

All three, with gaps:
\[ \ln(uv) = \blank + \blank \qquad
   \ln\!\left(\frac{u}{v}\right) = \blank - \blank \]
\[ \ln\!\left(u^{5}\right) = \blank \]
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$\ln u + \ln v$, \quad $\ln u - \ln v$, \quad $5 \ln u$.
\end{ansblock}

Here is the whole section on one line each, with its source beside it.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Index law (Program~\ref{prog:F01}) & Logarithm law \\
\midrule
$a^{m} a^{n} = a^{m+n}$ & $\ln(xy) = \ln x + \ln y$ \\
$a^{m} / a^{n} = a^{m-n}$ & $\ln(x/y) = \ln x - \ln y$ \\
$\left(a^{m}\right)^{n} = a^{mn}$ & $\ln\!\left(x^{n}\right) = n \ln x$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{These are not two sets of rules. They are one set, read from the two
ends.} A reader who has to look one of them up should look up the left-hand
column, because the right-hand column can be rebuilt from it in a line.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Cover the rest of the page.

\textbf{Write $\ln(a + b)$ in terms of $\ln a$ and $\ln b$. One line, no
working, as fast as you can do it.}
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
If you wrote $\ln a + \ln b$, you are in the majority, and this is the trap the
section was built to spring.

Check it at $a = 2$ and $b = 3$:
\[ \ln 5 = \val{f03.ln.five} \qquad \text{against} \qquad
   \ln 2 + \ln 3 = \val{f03.ln.six} \]
Now look at what that second number is. $\val{f03.ln.six}$ is $\ln 6$. The
wrong rule did not produce a near miss or a rounding difference: it computed
the logarithm of $a$ times $b$, which is the one thing you were not asked for.
\end{ansblock}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{There is no rule for the logarithm of a sum.} $\ln(a+b)$ does not
decompose, and no amount of rearranging will make it.

The reasoning that produces the wrong answer is a true rule generalised past
its hypothesis, and it is worth naming because it is now the third time this
book has caught the same reasoning. Program~\ref{prog:F01} had
$\sqrt{a+b} \ne \sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b}$. Program~\ref{prog:F02} had
$(a+b)^{2} \ne a^{2} + b^{2}$. Each time, an operation that genuinely
distributes over a \emph{product} was carried across a \emph{sum}, where it
does not.

A logarithm converts one operation into another: multiplication becomes
addition. It has nothing to say about a sum, because a sum was already the
thing it converts \emph{into}.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
That absence matters most in the one place where you will badly want a rule.

Suppose $u$ and $v$ are already logarithms \dash{} log-probabilities, say
\dash{} and what you need is the logarithm of $e^{u} + e^{v}$. You cannot
exponentiate first, because putting them into logarithms was the whole point.

What you can do is take the larger one outside the bracket, which is factorising
from Program~\ref{prog:F02} applied to exponentials. For $v \ge u$:
\[ e^{u} + e^{v} = e^{v}\left(e^{u-v} + 1\right)
   \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
   \ln\!\left(e^{u} + e^{v}\right) = v + \ln\!\left(1 + e^{u-v}\right) \]
Nothing has been approximated. The exponent that remains, $u - v$, is at most
zero, so the exponential is at most $1$ and cannot overflow.

Evaluate $\ln\!\left(e^{1000} + e^{1001}\right)$.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{f03.logsumexp.pair}$}
Take out the larger: the answer is $1001 + \ln\!\left(1 + e^{-1}\right)$, and

\begin{aibox}

\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\section{Undoing an exponential}
\label{sec:F03-undoing}
\index{logarithm!as an inverse}
\index{loss!as a negative log-probability}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$\ln\!\left(\frac{x^{2} y}{z}\right) = 2\ln x + \ln y - \ln z$. The quotient
became a difference and the index came down in front.

$2\ln a + \ln b - 3\ln c = \ln\!\left(\frac{a^{2} b}{c^{3}}\right)$, which is
the first line read backwards.

$\ln(x + y)$ does not simplify, and that is the whole answer. If you reached
for a rule here, reread the trap at the end of \S\ref{sec:F03-laws}, frames~20
and~21.
\end{ansblock}

Ten frames have taken logarithms apart. This section does the other thing.

A logarithm and an exponential undo one another, which is what
\S\ref{sec:F03-what}'s two-way definition already said:
\[ e^{\ln x} = x \qquad \text{and} \qquad \ln\!\left(e^{x}\right) = x \]
One of those needs $x > 0$ and the other holds for every real $x$. Which is
which, and why?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$e^{\ln x} = x$ needs $x > 0$; $\ln\!\left(e^{x}\right) = x$ does not.}
The first writes $\ln x$ down, and \S\ref{sec:F03-what} settled that there is
no such thing unless $x$ is positive. The second raises $e$ to a power, which
is legal for every real $x$ and always lands somewhere positive, so the
logarithm outside it always has something to work on.

That pair is the tool for getting an unknown out of an exponent, and it takes
one move rather than a search. To solve $b^{x} = c$, take logarithms of both
sides:
\[ b^{x} = c \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x \ln b = \ln c
   \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = \frac{\ln c}{\ln b} = \logb{b} c \]
Worked: $2^{x} = 1000$ gives $x = \logb{2} 1000 = \val{f03.log2.thousand}$,
which says $1000$ sits a little under ten doublings above $1$.

Now one in the other direction, and you can do it in your head. A loss starts
at $\num{4.0}$ and is halved repeatedly. How many halvings take it to
$\num{0.5}$?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Three}
$4 \to 2 \to 1 \to \tfrac{1}{2}$, and the general form is
$x = \logb{2} 8 = 3$, because the loss fell by a factor of $8$.

Now the debt. Program~\ref{prog:F02} rearranged a language model's loss on one
example,
\[ L = -\ln p \quad\longrightarrow\quad -L = \ln p
   \quad\longrightarrow\quad p = e^{-L} \]
and said in as many words that the last step was this program's and was being
taken on trust. It is the first identity of this frame: apply $e$ to both sides
of $\ln p = -L$, and $e^{\ln p}$ collapses to $p$. The trust was not misplaced,
and it is now paid.

Granting it, a loss of $\val{f02.loss.nats}$ nats says the model gave
probability $\val{f02.prob.from.loss}$ to what actually happened. That is
Program~\ref{prog:F02}'s number and not a new one \dash{} the same computation,
quoted from the same place \dash{} and it is about to do a great deal of work.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The direction that matters more is the other one. $L = -\ln p$ says that
\textbf{a probability and a loss are one quantity written on two scales}, and
the scale you read it on changes which differences look large.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rr}
\toprule
$p$ & $L = -\ln p$ \\
\midrule
$\num{0.9}$ & $\val{f03.loss.p90}$ \\
$\num{0.5}$ & $\val{f03.ln.two}$ \\
$\num{0.1}$ & $\val{f03.ln.ten}$ \\
$\num{0.01}$ & $\val{f03.loss.p01}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A confident model has a loss near zero; a model that is guessing between two
options has a loss of about $\val{f03.ln.two}$; a model that is badly wrong has
no upper bound at all.

Which is the bigger change: $p$ going from $\num{0.9}$ to $\num{0.5}$, or $p$
going from $\num{0.1}$ to $\num{0.01}$?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Read as probabilities, the first: $\num{0.4}$ against $\num{0.09}$, so the
first change is more than four times the second.

Read as losses, the second: $\val{f03.loss.gap.high}$ against
$\val{f03.ln.ten}$, so the second change is nearly four times the first.

Both readings are arithmetically correct and they disagree about which change
is bigger. That is not a paradox; it is the two scales measuring two different
things.
\end{ansblock}

The probability scale measures \emph{difference}. The loss scale measures
\emph{ratio}: $\num{0.1}$ to $\num{0.01}$ is a factor of ten, and the gap it
opens in the loss column is $\ln 10 = \val{f03.ln.ten}$ exactly \dash{} the
same number, to the digit, that the table gives as the loss at
$p = \num{0.1}$, because that probability is itself one factor of ten below
$1$.

\textbf{A logarithm converts ratios into differences.} Every remaining use in
this program is that sentence: a sequence probability in
\S\ref{sec:F03-logspace}, a perplexity in \S\ref{sec:F03-perplexity}, and a
plotted axis in \S\ref{sec:F03-scales}.
\end{fr}

\section{Why a language model works in log space}
\label{sec:F03-logspace}
\index{logarithm!log-probabilities}
\index{troubleshooting!underflow to zero}

\begin{fr}
A language model does not hand you the probability of a sentence. It hands you
the probability of one token at a time, each conditioned on everything before
it, and the probability of the whole sequence is the product of them:
\[ p(\text{sequence})
   = \prod_{i} p(\text{token } i \mid \text{everything before it}) \]
That is the chain rule of probability. It is exact and it is not an
independence assumption \dash{} Program~\ref{prog:P23} derives it, and it is
used here as given.

Take a sequence of $\val{f03.seq.tokens}$ tokens, which is a few pages of
prose, and give the model $\val{f02.prob.from.loss}$ at every step \dash{} the
probability Program~\ref{prog:F02} obtained from a loss of
$\val{f02.loss.nats}$ nats, which is an ordinary figure for a working model.

Roughly how big is that product?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{About $\val{f03.seq.prod}$}
You did not get that by multiplying, and neither did this book. By the first
law the logarithm of the product is $\val{f03.seq.tokens}$ copies of the
logarithm of one token added together, and one token's logarithm is the loss
with its sign flipped: $\ln p = -\val{f02.loss.nats}$ nats. Divide by $\ln 10$
to put it on the decimal scale:
\[ \logb{10} p(\text{sequence})
   = \val{f03.seq.tokens} \times \frac{-\val{f02.loss.nats}}{\ln 10}
   = \val{f03.seq.prod.log10} \]
so the product is $10^{\val{f03.seq.prod.log10}}$. Written out in full it would
open with more than two thousand zeros after the decimal point, which is why
the logarithm did the work.

Now the part that turns an oddity into a defect. A float has a smallest
positive value it can represent, and below that there is nothing but zero:

\begin{itemize}
\item float64 reaches $\val{f03.f64.tiny}$ as an ordinary number and
  $\val{f03.f64.sub}$ as a \emph{subnormal}, which is a number bought at the
  cost of precision;
\item float32 reaches only $\val{f03.f32.tiny}$ and $\val{f03.f32.sub}$.
\end{itemize}

What a subnormal is, and why a format has two thresholds rather than one, is
Program~\ref{prog:P01}. What matters here is that both are enormously larger
than the product above.

At $\val{f02.prob.from.loss}$ per token, after how many tokens is the running
product exactly zero \dash{} in float32, and in float64?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
The arithmetic predicts it before the machine is asked. After $n$ tokens the
product is $p^{n}$, whose logarithm is $n \ln p$, so it falls below the
smallest subnormal when $n$ passes $\ln(\text{subnormal}) / \ln p$. That is
$\val{f03.f32.predict}$ for float32 and $\val{f03.f64.predict}$ for float64.

Run it one token at a time, rounding to the format at every step, and the first
exact zero arrives at token $\val{f03.f32.zero.at}$ and token
$\val{f03.f64.zero.at}$:
\end{ansblock}

\transcript{f03-underflow}

\noindent
Prediction and measurement agree, each landing on the first integer past the
predicted crossing.

\begin{warning}
\textbf{A float32 gives up $\val{f03.f32.zero.at}$ tokens into a
$\val{f03.seq.tokens}$-token sequence.} Nothing is raised. The product becomes
$\num{0.0}$, its logarithm becomes $-\infty$, the loss for that sequence
becomes $\infty$, and the first thing anyone sees is a training run that has
stopped producing numbers, several layers away from the multiplication that
did it.

The failure is also silent about its own size. There is no partial answer here
to inspect and no gradual loss of accuracy to notice: the value is exact, and
it is exactly wrong.
\end{warning}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The fix is one line, and it is this program's opening claim with a measurement
underneath it. Take the logarithm of the product and the first law turns it
into a sum:
\[ \ln p(\text{sequence})
   = \sum_{i} \ln p(\text{token } i \mid \text{everything before it}) \]
Each term is about $-\val{f02.loss.nats}$, and there are
$\val{f03.seq.tokens}$ of them, so the total is about $-4800$ \dash{} a number
so far inside every float format in use that no format's limits are anywhere
near it.

\textbf{A product no format could hold became a sum every format holds, and
that is the only reason anybody uses a logarithm.} Nothing was approximated on
the way: the first law is an identity, so the sum is the logarithm of the
product exactly, including of the product the machine could not compute.

What does $-4800$ nats tell you about the sequence?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
On its own, almost nothing, because it scales with length. The same model on a
sequence twice as long and of exactly the same quality scores $-9600$, and
comparing the two numbers would rank the shorter sequence higher for being
shorter.

Divide by the number of tokens. That gives $-\val{f02.loss.nats}$ per token,
which does compare across sequences of different lengths \dash{} and that
per-token mean, with its sign flipped, is the loss.
\end{ansblock}

\begin{aibox}
The summed log-probability is not an illustration; it is the line in the
training loop. A model's forward pass produces one log-probability per position,
the loop sums them, divides by the token count and negates, and the number that
comes out is what the optimiser is handed.

Program~\ref{prog:P26} shows that maximising that sum is maximum likelihood,
which is where the loss comes from rather than being chosen for convenience.
Program~\ref{prog:P02} shows how the per-token log-probability is computed
without ever forming a probability, which is the same avoidance of
exponentiation as \S\ref{sec:F03-laws}'s frame on $\logsumexp$.
\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{f03-underflow}{%
  The same sequence probability computed two ways. Multiplied, it falls off the
  bottom of both float formats and the machine reports a clean zero; summed as
  logarithms, it is a small negative number nothing has trouble holding.}{%
  The sequence probability multiplied, which underflows, and summed.}

\begin{fr}
\yourturn
Take a model that is choosing between two options and doing no better than a
coin, so $p = \num{0.5}$ at every token.

A coin is the worst a model can honestly be, so what comes out is a ceiling
rather than a complaint about one bad model: any model worth serving assigns
more than $\num{0.5}$ to the token it actually sees, and a larger factor per
token survives longer. The number you are about to compute is therefore the
shortest sequence this arithmetic can fail on, not the longest.

At what length does the running product become exactly zero in float32, and at
what length in float64? Use the two smallest subnormals from the previous
frames, and note before you start that $\val{f03.f32.sub}$ is exactly
$2^{-149}$ and $\val{f03.f64.sub}$ is exactly $2^{-1074}$.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
float32 at $\val{f03.half.f32.zero}$ tokens; float64 at
$\val{f03.half.f64.zero}$ tokens, both measured the same way as before.

The predictions this time carry no fraction at all. Each halving consumes
exactly one exponent, so the product reaches $2^{-149}$ after exactly $149$
tokens and $2^{-1074}$ after exactly $1074$, and the token after each of those
is the first exact zero.
\end{ansblock}

\textbf{The token budget is the exponent budget.} A format's exponent range is
not an abstraction about very large numbers; it is a hard ceiling on how long a
sequence you can score by multiplying, and the ceiling for a coin-flipping
model in float32 is shorter than a page.

That is Program~\ref{prog:P01}'s subject arriving a whole part early, and it is
the reason this book puts floating point before the linear algebra rather than
after it.
\end{fr}

\section{Perplexity: the exponential of a mean logarithm}
\label{sec:F03-perplexity}
\index{perplexity!definition}
\index{measurement!a number without its unit}

\begin{fr}
A mean log-probability is a fine number for an optimiser and a poor one for a
person. $-\val{f02.loss.nats}$ per token does not say, in any form you can
picture, how uncertain the model was.

Undo the logarithm and it does. $e^{-\val{f02.loss.nats}}$ is
$\val{f02.prob.from.loss}$, and that is a particular probability: the
\emph{geometric mean} of the per-token probabilities, which is what averaging
logarithms and then exponentiating produces. Its reciprocal has a name and is
what everybody quotes:
\[ \text{perplexity} = e^{\,\text{mean negative log-likelihood}} \]
At a loss of $\val{f02.loss.nats}$ nats that is $\val{f03.ppl}$.

A model card reports a perplexity of $7$. What is that model's geometric-mean
token probability, and what is its loss in nats?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
The geometric-mean token probability is $\val{f03.ppl.seven.p}$, the reciprocal
of $7$.

The loss is $\ln 7 = \val{f03.ppl.seven.nats}$ nats, or
$\val{f03.ppl.seven.bits}$ bits.
\end{ansblock}

\textbf{What a perplexity of $7$ says} is that at each step the model is as
uncertain as a fair seven-sided die \dash{} not that it has narrowed the
vocabulary to seven candidates, but that its spread of belief is worth the same
as an even choice among seven.

\textbf{What it does not say} is that the model is right one time in seven.
Perplexity is an average over a distribution, not a hit rate, and a model can
have a good perplexity while being wrong about every token you care about.

For scale, take a vocabulary of about $\val{f03.vocab}$ tokens and a model that
picks uniformly at random from it. Its perplexity is $\val{f03.vocab}$ and its
loss is $\val{f03.vocab.nats}$ nats, or $\val{f03.vocab.bits}$ bits. The
distance between that and $7$ is the whole of what a language model has
learned, and the logarithm is what makes the distance a readable number rather
than a ratio in the thousands.

Perplexity as the exponential of a \emph{cross-entropy}, and as an effective
number of equally likely choices, is Program~\ref{prog:P29}'s subject. This
frame owes it the definition and stops there.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Two model cards, from two teams, on the same benchmark.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Model A & loss $\val{f02.loss.nats}$ \\
Model B & loss $\val{f03.nats.to.bits}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Cover the rest of the page. \textbf{Which model is better, and by how much?}
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
They are the same model.

$\val{f02.loss.nats}$ nats is $\val{f03.nats.to.bits}$ bits. Team A quoted
their loss in nats and team B quoted theirs in bits, and neither card said so,
because to each team the base was too obvious to write down.
\end{ansblock}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{A number without its base is not a measurement.} The bits figure is
larger than the nats figure by $\val{f03.bits.over.nats.pct}$\%, always, for
every model \dash{} because the conversion is the fixed constant
$1 / \ln 2 = \val{f03.inv.ln.two}$ of \S\ref{sec:F03-base}.

The reasoning that produces the error is frame~11's own fact used backwards.
Changing the base multiplies every value by a constant, so the two scales agree
about \emph{ordering} and disagree about \emph{magnitude}: within one card the
numbers are consistent, the curves have the same shape, and nothing on the page
looks wrong. There is no internal evidence of the units at all.

And the direction is not neutral. A team quoting nats against a benchmark
quoted in bits reports a number $\val{f03.bits.over.nats.pct}$\% better than
the comparison deserves, which is the direction nobody checks.
\end{trapbox}

Program~\ref{prog:P29} meets the same collision for a reported entropy, where
it is worse: an entropy in bits and an entropy in nats are both ordinary
numbers of ordinary size, and there is no threshold that flags either as
implausible.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Here is the same shape of error with no units in sight at all.

A decoder is choosing between two candidate continuations. The model likes both
exactly as much, giving every token in each of them a probability of
$\num{0.9}$. One candidate is three tokens long and the other is twenty.

Compute the summed log-probability of each, and then the mean log-probability
of each.

Then, separately, take the four probabilities $\num{0.5}$, $\num{0.2}$,
$\num{0.9}$ and $\num{0.05}$, and compute both their arithmetic mean and their
geometric mean.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Summed: $\val{f03.beam.short.sum}$ for the three-token candidate and
$\val{f03.beam.long.sum}$ for the twenty-token one.

Mean: $\val{f03.beam.mean}$ for both, identical to five decimal places, which
is what you would expect of two candidates the model likes equally.

Arithmetic mean of the four probabilities: $\val{f03.am}$. Geometric mean:
$\val{f03.gm}$.
\end{ansblock}

\textbf{Between candidates the model likes about equally, ranking by summed
log-probability always prefers the shorter.} Each additional token adds another
negative number, so length is penalised \emph{in addition} to quality rather
than instead of it \dash{} twenty confident tokens still outscore three
unconfident ones. The mean is unaffected by length, which is why
implementations rank by it or by something derived from it.

The two means make the second half of the point. $\val{f03.gm}$ is well below
$\val{f03.am}$, and they are not two estimates of one quantity: the arithmetic
mean averages the probabilities and the geometric mean averages their
logarithms. So an average of per-document perplexities is not the perplexity of
the corpus. On documents of equal length the averaged figure always comes out
\emph{above} the true one, and the inequality that guarantees that direction,
and its name, are Program~\ref{prog:P19}'s. On documents of unequal length the
corpus loss is token-weighted, which adds a second error whose sign depends on
the data \dash{} so the averaged number is not the perplexity of anything, in
either direction.

\begin{aibox}
Both halves are live defects rather than curiosities. A decoder that ranks
candidate continuations by their summed score truncates its own output, and the
usual repair is to divide by the length or by a power of it. A leaderboard that
averages per-document perplexity across documents of unequal length is
reporting a number that is not the perplexity of anything.
\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\section{Logarithmic scales, and reading a plot}
\label{sec:F03-scales}
\index{logarithm!logarithmic axis}
\index{troubleshooting!midpoint of a log axis}

\begin{fr}
You have already used the idea behind a logarithmic axis without it being
named. Program~\ref{prog:F01}'s ladder of magnitudes labels every rung with a
power of ten and measures the climb in exponents rather than in the quantities
themselves. An axis that carries the exponent in place of the quantity is a
logarithmic axis.

Take such an axis now. Its ticks read $1$, $10$, $100$, $1000$, equally
spaced. Before you read on, and quickly:

\textbf{what value sits halfway between the $10$ tick and the $100$ tick?}
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$\val{f03.axis.mid}$, not $55$.

Halfway between the ticks is halfway in the \emph{exponent}: the ticks are at
$10^{1}$ and $10^{2}$, so the midpoint is at $10^{\num{1.5}}$, which is
$\sqrt{10 \times 100}$.
\end{ansblock}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Halfway along a logarithmic axis is the geometric mean.} Not the
arithmetic mean, which is what $55$ is.

The reasoning that produces $55$ is that you read the axis as a ruler. On a
ruler, halfway between two marks is their average, and the arithmetic is so
automatic that it does not present itself as a step you took. But this axis is
not a ruler: what is uniform along it is the exponent, so equal distances are
equal \emph{ratios}. Ten to $\val{f03.axis.mid}$ and $\val{f03.axis.mid}$ to a
hundred are the same ratio, roughly $\sqrt{10}$ each, which is what makes them
the same distance.

Note where the geometric mean has turned up. It is exactly the quantity
\S\ref{sec:F03-perplexity} was averaging when it exponentiated a mean
logarithm, and it is here for the same reason: both are averaging in the
exponent.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The correction generalises into the sentence this section exists for.

\textbf{A gap on a logarithmic axis is a ratio.} That is
\S\ref{sec:F03-undoing}'s \emph{a logarithm converts ratios into differences},
read from the paper's side: the axis holds the logarithm, so a difference along
it is a ratio between the quantities it stands for.

One consequence has a number attached. A doubling is the same distance
wherever it happens on the axis, because a doubling is a fixed ratio \dash{}
and you have already met the number that distance is, twice in this program,
doing something else with it both times.

Fill in. On a base-ten logarithmic axis, a doubling covers \blank\ of a
decade, and a factor of ten covers \blank\ of a decade.

Then one to think about. A loss curve falls from $\num{4.0}$ to $\num{2.0}$
early in training, and from $\num{0.5}$ to $\num{0.25}$ late. Which fall is
bigger?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
A doubling covers $\val{f03.log10.two}$ of a decade; a factor of ten covers a
whole one, by definition of a decade. That first number is $\logb{10} 2$, the
constant that counted the digits of $2^{80}$ in frame~7, arriving for the
third time.

As for the two falls: on a linear axis the first is bigger, by a factor of
eight, because it removes $\num{2.0}$ of loss and the second removes
$\num{0.25}$. On a logarithmic axis they are the same gap, because both are a
halving.
\end{ansblock}

Neither answer is the right one in general, and choosing between them is
choosing a question. A linear axis answers \emph{how much loss did I remove};
a logarithmic axis answers \emph{how much of what was left did I remove}.

Late in training only the second question has a visible answer. Everything is
happening in the last fraction of the range, a linear axis flattens all of it
against the floor, and the plot stops carrying information at exactly the point
where you start needing it. That is why loss curves are published on a
logarithmic axis, and it is a decision about which question the reader is being
allowed to ask.

Program~\ref{prog:P33} reads a loss curve as evidence \dash{} what a plateau, a
spike and a suspiciously smooth descent each imply, and which of them are
distinguishable from noise.
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{f03-log-axis}{%
  The same question asked of two axes. On a ruler the midpoint between two
  marks is their average; on an axis carrying the exponent it is their
  geometric mean, and equal distances are equal ratios rather than equal
  differences.}{%
  A linear axis and a logarithmic one, and where each has its midpoint.}

\begin{fr}
One more thing a logarithmic axis does, and it is the reason so many plots in
this field have logarithmic axes on \emph{both} sides.

Take a relationship of the form $y = a x^{b}$ \dash{} a \emph{power law}. Take
logarithms of both sides, and the third law of \S\ref{sec:F03-laws} flattens
it:
\[ \ln y = \ln a + b \ln x \]
That is the equation of a straight line in $\ln x$ and $\ln y$, with intercept
$\ln a$ and gradient $b$.

\textbf{A power law is a straight line on log-log axes, and the gradient of the
line is the exponent.} It is also the cheapest test available: plot the data
that way, and if it is straight the relationship is a power law and you can
read the exponent off with a ruler.

\begin{aibox}
This is why every scaling-law plot you have seen has logarithmic axes on both
sides. Model loss against parameter count, against data, against compute: the
claim being made is that the relationship is a power law, and the plot is the
evidence for it as well as the way the exponent is read.

Program~\ref{prog:P33} treats such a fit as an empirical fit with a stated
extrapolation error rather than as a law of nature, which is the distinction
that matters when somebody projects one three decades to the right of the last
measured point.
\end{aibox}

You have four training runs' worth of budget and want to sweep a learning rate
from $\num{1e-5}$ to $\num{1e-2}$. Where do you put the four?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
On a logarithmic grid, one per decade: $\num{1e-5}$, $\num{1e-4}$,
$\num{1e-3}$, $\num{1e-2}$. The exponents give it to you without a library
\dash{} $-5$, $-4$, $-3$, $-2$, equally spaced \dash{} and
\code{np.logspace(-5, -2, 4)} prints the same four.

Space them linearly instead, with \code{np.linspace(1e-5, 1e-2, 4)}, and the
four points come back as $\num{1e-5}$, then $\val{f03.sweep.lin.second}$, then
two more, ending at $\num{1e-2}$.
\end{ansblock}

\begin{note}
\textbf{Prints, not returns.} That grid is exact in the exponents and not
necessarily exact in the numbers. Ask \code{np.logspace} for the first point and
you may get $\num{1e-5}$, or you may get the value one unit in the last place
below it \dash{} which of the two depends on the build, and the printed form
reads \code{1.e-05} in both cases. The repr is the problem: it will not tell
you, so if the answer matters, ask for \code{float(x).hex()} instead.

It changes nothing about a learning-rate sweep. It is one reason
Program~\ref{prog:P01} comes before the linear algebra rather than after it.
\end{note}

That puts $\val{f03.sweep.lin.top.decade}$ of the four points inside the top
decade alone, and nothing whatever between $\num{1e-5}$ and
$\val{f03.sweep.lin.second}$ \dash{} which is two of the three decades you
meant to search, and where a working learning rate usually turns out to be.
Three of the four runs sat within a factor of three of one another.

The reason is the previous frame's. A learning rate matters by its ratio to the
one next to it, and a linear grid spaces by difference.

\begin{summarybox}
\sumitem{1--3}{\result{A logarithm is an \emph{exponent}: $\logb{b} x$ is the power
  that turns $b$ into $x$, and $b^{y} = x$ says the same thing from the other
  end. $\logb{b} 1 = 0$ for every base, forced by $b^{0} = 1$, and
  $\logb{b} b = 1$}.}
\sumitem{4--5}{\result{The argument must be strictly positive, because no power of a
  positive base is zero or negative. Your libraries disagree about what to do
  instead: \code{math.log} raises, \code{np.log} returns \code{-inf} or
  \code{nan} with a warning}.}
\sumitem{6--7}{\result{A logarithm compresses a range you cannot hold into one you can.
  The digit count of $2^{80}$ takes one multiplication,
  $80 \times \val{f03.log10.two} = \val{f03.two.eighty.exp}$, where
  Program~\ref{prog:F01} needed all $\val{f03.two.eighty.digits}$ digits}.}
\sumitem{8--9}{\result{A bare logarithm means base ten to one readership and base $e$
  to the other, and both are applying a convention correctly. Write the base
  every time; this book's build refuses anything else}.}
\sumitem{10--11}{\result{Change of base is
  $\logb{b} x = \logb{c} x / \logb{c} b$, with the base you want on the bottom.
  It multiplies every value by one constant, so the shape of a plotted
  logarithm never reveals its base}.}
\sumitem{12--14}{\result{$\ln 2 = \val{f03.ln.two}$ and
  $1 / \ln 2 = \val{f03.inv.ln.two}$ convert between nats and bits. A bit is
  the smaller unit, so a count in bits is the larger number}.}
\sumitem{15--19}{\result{The three laws are Program~\ref{prog:F01}'s index laws read
  backwards: $\ln(xy) = \ln x + \ln y$, $\ln(x/y) = \ln x - \ln y$,
  $\ln(x^{n}) = n\ln x$}.}
\sumitem{20--21}{\result{\textbf{There is no rule for the logarithm of a sum.}
  $\ln a + \ln b$ is the logarithm of the \emph{product} \dash{} the third time
  in three programs that a rule true of a product has been carried across a
  sum}.}
\sumitem{22--23}{\result{Where you must have $\ln(e^{u} + e^{v})$, take the larger
  outside: $v + \ln(1 + e^{u-v})$. \code{np.exp(1000.)} is \code{inf};
  \code{np.logaddexp} is $\val{f03.logsumexp.pair}$}.}
\sumitem{25--27}{\result{$e^{\ln x} = x$ for $x > 0$ and $\ln(e^{x}) = x$ always, so
  $b^{x} = c$ is solved in one move and $L = -\ln p$ gives $p = e^{-L}$}.}
\sumitem{28--29}{\result{A probability and a loss are one quantity on two scales, and
  \textbf{a logarithm converts ratios into differences}: the factor of ten from
  $p = \num{0.1}$ to $p = \num{0.01}$ is a gap of $\val{f03.ln.ten}$ in the
  loss}.}
\sumitem{30--32}{\result{A $\val{f03.seq.tokens}$-token sequence at
  $\val{f02.prob.from.loss}$ per token has probability about
  $\val{f03.seq.prod}$. float32 makes the running product exactly zero after
  $\val{f03.f32.zero.at}$ tokens and float64 after
  $\val{f03.f64.zero.at}$, raising nothing either time}.}
\sumitem{33--34}{\result{Sum the logarithms instead and the total is small and
  negative. Divide by the token count and that mean, negated, is the loss
  \dash{} which is the line in the training loop}.}
\sumitem{35--36}{\result{\textbf{The token budget is the exponent budget.} At
  $p = \num{0.5}$ the product reaches zero at $\val{f03.half.f32.zero}$ tokens
  in float32 and $\val{f03.half.f64.zero}$ in float64}.}
\sumitem{37--38}{\result{Perplexity is the exponential of the mean negative
  log-likelihood, and the reciprocal of the geometric-mean token probability.
  A perplexity of $7$ is as uncertain as a fair seven-sided die; it is not a
  hit rate}.}
\sumitem{39--40}{\result{A loss reported without its base is not a measurement:
  $\val{f02.loss.nats}$ nats and $\val{f03.nats.to.bits}$ bits are the same
  model, $\val{f03.bits.over.nats.pct}$\% apart, in the direction that
  flatters}.}
\sumitem{41--42}{\result{Between candidates the model likes about equally,
  ranking by \emph{summed} log-probability prefers the shorter; length is
  penalised on top of quality, not instead of it, and the mean is unaffected.
  And the geometric
  mean $\val{f03.gm}$ is not the arithmetic mean $\val{f03.am}$, so an average
  of perplexities is not a perplexity}.}
\sumitem{43--46}{\result{Halfway between two ticks of a logarithmic axis is the
  \emph{geometric} mean \dash{} $\val{f03.axis.mid}$ between $10$ and $100$,
  not $55$. A gap is a ratio, and a doubling is $\val{f03.log10.two}$ of a
  decade wherever it falls}.}
\sumitem{46--48}{\result{A linear axis answers \emph{how much did I remove}, a
  logarithmic one \emph{how much of what was left}. $y = ax^{b}$ is a straight
  line on log-log axes with gradient $b$, and a learning rate is swept on a
  logarithmic grid for the same reason}.}
\end{summarybox}

\begin{testexercises}
\item Evaluate $\logb{3} 81$ and $\logb{10} \num{0.001}$.
  \answerto{$4$ and $-3$. The second is an exponent too: $10^{-3}$ is
  $\num{0.001}$.}
\item What is $\logb{7} 7$, and what is $\logb{7} 1$?
  \answerto{$1$ and $0$, from $7^{1} = 7$ and $7^{0} = 1$.}
\item Why is $\logb{10}(-100)$ undefined, when $-100$ is a perfectly ordinary
  number?
  \answerto{Because no real power of $10$ is negative. The number is fine; the
  question the logarithm asks has no answer for it.}
\item Write $\ln\!\left(\frac{a^{3}}{bc}\right)$ as separate logarithms.
  \answerto{$3\ln a - \ln b - \ln c$. Both factors of the denominator are
  subtracted.}
\item Collapse $\ln x + 2\ln y - \ln z$ into a single logarithm.
  \answerto{$\ln\!\left(\frac{xy^{2}}{z}\right)$.}
\item Is $\ln(3 + 4)$ equal to $\ln 3 + \ln 4$? Answer with a reason, not a
  calculator.
  \answerto{No. $\ln 3 + \ln 4$ is $\ln 12$ by the first law, and $12$ is not
  $7$. There is no rule for the logarithm of a sum.}
\item Solve $3^{x} = 50$, leaving the answer as a ratio of logarithms.
  \answerto{$x = \frac{\ln 50}{\ln 3} = \logb{3} 50$.}
\item A loss is $\val{f03.ppl.seven.nats}$ nats. What is the model's
  geometric-mean token probability, and what is its perplexity?
  \answerto{$\val{f03.ppl.seven.p}$ and $7$: the probability is
  $e^{-\val{f03.ppl.seven.nats}}$ and the perplexity is its reciprocal.}
\item Convert a loss of $\val{f03.ppl.seven.nats}$ nats into bits.
  \answerto{$\val{f03.ppl.seven.bits}$ bits \dash{} multiply by
  $\val{f03.inv.ln.two}$.}
\item Why is the probability of a long sequence computed as a sum of
  logarithms rather than as a product?
  \answerto{Because the product underflows to exactly zero, with no error
  raised: at $\val{f02.prob.from.loss}$ per token that happens after
  $\val{f03.f32.zero.at}$ tokens in float32. The sum stays small and negative.}
\item On a logarithmic axis whose ticks read $1$, $10$, $100$, what sits
  halfway between the $1$ tick and the $100$ tick?
  \answerto{$10$, the geometric mean of $1$ and $100$ \dash{} which is also the
  tick between them, as it must be.}
\item Data plots as a straight line on log-log axes with gradient $-\num{0.5}$.
  What is the relationship, and what happens to $y$ when $x$ is multiplied by
  four?
  \answerto{$y = ax^{-1/2}$. Multiplying $x$ by four halves $y$, because
  $4^{-1/2} = \tfrac{1}{2}$.}
\end{testexercises}

\begin{furtherproblems}
\item Evaluate $\logb{2} 256$, $\logb{5} 125$ and $\logb{10} \num{0.01}$.
  \answerto{$8$, $3$ and $-2$.}
\item Evaluate $\logb{9} 3$.
  \answerto{$\tfrac{1}{2}$, because $9^{1/2} = 3$. A logarithm need not be a
  whole number and this one is a root wearing an exponent.}
\item Write $\ln\!\left(x^{2}\sqrt{y}\right)$ as separate logarithms.
  \answerto{$2\ln x + \tfrac{1}{2}\ln y$, since a square root is the index
  $\tfrac{1}{2}$ from Program~\ref{prog:F01}.}
\item Collapse $\tfrac{1}{2}\ln a - \tfrac{1}{2}\ln b$ into one logarithm.
  \answerto{$\ln\sqrt{a/b}$.}
\item Show that $\logb{b} \frac{1}{x} = -\logb{b} x$.
  \answerto{$\ln(1/x) = \ln 1 - \ln x = -\ln x$, since $\ln 1 = 0$. The same
  argument runs in any base.}
\item Solve $2^{x} = \num{0.001}$.
  \answerto{$x = \logb{2} \num{0.001} = -\logb{2} 1000
  = -\val{f03.log2.thousand}$, by the previous problem.}
\item If $\ln p = -3$, what is $p$?
  \answerto{$p = e^{-3}$. Leave it in that form unless a decimal is wanted;
  the exponential is the answer, not a step towards one.}
\item A loss doubles from $L$ to $2L$. What happens to the probability?
  \answerto{It is squared: $e^{-2L} = \left(e^{-L}\right)^{2}$. This is
  one of Program~\ref{prog:F02}'s further problems, and now you can prove it.}
\item Two models score $\val{f02.loss.nats}$ and $\val{f03.ppl.seven.nats}$
  nats. What is the ratio of the probabilities they assign to the same token
  sequence, per token?
  \answerto{$e^{\val{f02.loss.nats} - \val{f03.ppl.seven.nats}}$ in favour of
  the second, because a difference of logarithms is a ratio of the
  quantities.}
\item Why does a language model's loss have no upper bound, when a probability
  has both bounds?
  \answerto{$L = -\ln p$, and $\ln p$ falls without bound as $p$ approaches
  zero. The bounded interval $\intcc{0}{1}$ maps onto the whole of the
  non-negative reals.}
\item How many decimal digits has $2^{1000}$?
  \answerto{$\lfloor 1000 \times \val{f03.log10.two} \rfloor + 1 = 302$. The
  same one multiplication as frame~7, with a different exponent.}
\item A quantity grows by $10$\% per step. How many steps double it?
  \answerto{$\frac{\ln 2}{\ln \num{1.1}}$, which is between seven and eight
  steps, so eight are needed for the doubling to have happened.}
\item On a logarithmic axis, is the distance from $2$ to $6$ larger, smaller or
  equal to the distance from $50$ to $150$?
  \answerto{Equal. Both are a factor of three, and equal ratios are equal
  distances.}
\item A plot shows the loss falling from $\num{10.0}$ to $\num{1.0}$ over the
  first tenth of training and from $\num{1.0}$ to $\num{0.9}$ over the rest.
  On which axis does the second half look like progress?
  \answerto{On neither, and that is the point: the second half is a factor of
  $\num{1.11}$ against the first half's factor of ten. A logarithmic axis makes
  the disparity visible instead of flattering it.}
\item Both \code{np.log(0.0)} and \code{math.log(0)} concern the same
  undefined quantity. Why is the first the more dangerous in a training loop?
  \answerto{It returns \code{-inf} and continues, so the defect travels. The
  exception stops at the line that caused it.}
\item You have six trials to sweep a hyperparameter from $\num{1e-6}$ to
  $\num{1e-1}$. Where do they go, and what does a linear grid cost you?
  \answerto{One per decade, at $\num{1e-6}$ up to $\num{1e-1}$. A linear grid
  puts all but one of them inside the top decade.}
\item Show that $\logb{b} x$ and $\logb{c} x$ have the same sign for every
  admissible $x$, when $b$ and $c$ are both greater than $1$.
  \answerto{They differ by the factor $1 / \logb{c} b$, which is positive when
  both bases exceed $1$. A positive multiple cannot change a sign \dash{} which
  is why a change of base cannot reorder anything.}
\item A team reports an entropy of $\num{2.3}$. What is the first question to
  ask, and how much does the answer move the number?
  \answerto{Bits or nats. The two readings differ by the factor
  $\val{f03.inv.ln.two}$, so the bits figure is
  $\val{f03.bits.over.nats.pct}$\% larger for the same measurement.}
\end{furtherproblems}
