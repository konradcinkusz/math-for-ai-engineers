\program[Sums of random variables]{Sums of random variables: the central limit
theorem, concentration and initialisation}
\label{prog:P25}
\index{central limit theorem}
\index{concentration}

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{1--8}{add two variances, and say what independence is buying when
  you do}
\outcome{9--14}{derive the rate at which an average concentrates, and say what
  four times the data buys}
\outcome{15--22}{say what the central limit theorem promises and what it does
  not}
\outcome{23--29}{size an evaluation run from the difference you need to see}
\outcome{30--41}{derive the $1/\sqrt{d_k}$ in attention, and say what it
  corrects}
\outcome{42--54}{say why a run can diverge before it has taken a step}
\end{outcomes}

\begin{quiz}
\item Two independent quantities are added. What happens to their spreads?
  \teachesat{1--2}
  \answerto{Nothing simple \dash{} it is the \emph{variances} that add, not
  the spreads, so the spread of the sum is the root of the sum of the squares.
  Adding two quantities of spread $1$ gives a spread of $\sqrt{2}$, not $2$.}
\item An average is taken over four times as many draws. What happens to its
  spread? \teachesat{13--14}
  \answerto{It halves. The variance falls like $1/n$ and the spread like
  $1/\sqrt{n}$, so buying a factor of two costs a factor of four.}
\item A sum of many independent things is close to Gaussian. Does that let you
  read its tail probabilities off a Gaussian? \teachesat{17--19}
  \answerto{No. The theorem is about the middle, and the tail is the one
  question it does not answer \dash{} which is exactly where a tail
  probability gets used.}
\item An attention score is a dot product over $d_k$ terms, each with unit
  variance. What is the spread of one such score, and what follows for the
  $\softmax$? \teachesat{30--33}
  \answerto{From the variance of a dot product. With unit-variance entries
  each of the $d_k$ terms contributes $1$ and the variances add, so the score
  has variance $d_k$ and spread $\sqrt{d_k}$. Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ returns
  it to $1$ at any head size.}
\item A deep network produces \code{nan} on its first training step. What is
  the first thing you would check? \teachesat{48--52}
  \answerto{The weight scale. Each layer multiplies the signal's variance by
  its fan-in times the weight variance, so a scale that is wrong by a factor
  compounds geometrically with depth \dash{} to nothing, or to overflow.}
\end{quiz}

\section{Two things added}
\label{sec:P25-adding}
\index{variance!of a sum}
\index{independence!and adding variances}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:P24} closed on the thing it had not done. It gave you the
random variable, its expectation and its variance, and then said that what
happens when you \emph{add} random variables is this program's subject.

So start there, with the smallest case anybody can check.

Two fair dice are thrown, independently. Each score on its own has variance
$\frac{\val{p25.die.var.num}}{\val{p25.die.var.den}}$. What is the variance of
their total?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\frac{\val{p25.die.var.num}}{\val{p25.die.var.den}} +
     \frac{\val{p25.die.var.num}}{\val{p25.die.var.den}}$}

The variances add. Not the spreads \dash{} the variances.

\textbf{For independent $X$ and $Y$, $\Var(X + Y) = \Var(X) + \Var(Y)$}, and
that is the whole of this program's machinery. Everything from here to the
last frame is that one line applied somewhere it is not usually recognised.

The script enumerates all thirty-six throws and checks it over fractions, so
nothing here is a limit or an approximation.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Now the question that says what the word \emph{independent} is doing.

Instead of throwing two dice, throw one and double it. That is also a total of
two dice's worth. Is its variance the same
$\val{p25.pair.var}$?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{No: it is $4 \times \frac{\val{p25.die.var.num}}{\val{p25.die.var.den}}$}

Doubling multiplies every distance from the mean by two, and the variance
squares distances, so it multiplies the variance by \emph{four}. Two
independent dice give twice. One die counted twice gives four times.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{Adding more of the same thing averages out the noise.}}

It does when the things are independent and it does not when they are the same
thing wearing two labels. The two cases differ by a factor of two here, and by
a factor of $n$ over $n$ copies \dash{} which is the difference between an
average that concentrates and one that does not move at all.

The failure mode has a name in practice: an evaluation set with duplicated
items, a batch assembled from one document, a bootstrap over rows that share a
user. In each the count says $n$ and the arithmetic gets $1$.
\end{trapbox}

One expression covers both cases. Given that it must reduce to
$\Var(X) + \Var(Y)$ when the two are independent and to $4\Var(X)$ when
$Y$ is $X$, what single term has to be added?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$2\Cov(X, Y)$}

\[ \Var(X + Y) \;=\; \Var(X) + \Var(Y) + 2\Cov(X, Y) \]

Program~\ref{prog:P24} defines that last term and says what it measures. Read
the two special cases off it: independent quantities have zero covariance, so
the cross term vanishes; and $\Var(X + X)$ has a cross term of $2\Var(X)$,
which turns two into four.

On the worked dependent pair from Program~\ref{prog:P24} \dash{} a die and its
own square \dash{} the cross term is $\val{p25.dep.cross}$, and the variance
of the sum is $\val{p25.dep.var}$ against $\val{p25.dep.varsum}$ for the two
separately. The script checks the identity rather than quoting it.

Which raises a question worth being exact about. Reading that line, does the
argument actually require the two quantities to be \emph{independent}?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{No \dash{} a zero covariance is enough}

The cross term is the only thing independence removes, so anything that makes
it vanish will do. That is a weaker hypothesis and it is worth having, because
it is sometimes checkable when independence is not.

It is also a dangerous licence, and Program~\ref{prog:P24} supplied the
warning: it showed a pair with covariance exactly zero and total dependence.
Zero covariance buys you this identity and nothing else.

And \enquote{independent} here is Program~\ref{prog:P23}'s word \dash{} a
condition on the quantities, not a description of how they were collected. Two
measurements taken in different rooms by different people can fail it, if both
depend on something neither of them recorded.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{rigourbox}
\textbf{Not proved here: that the covariance of independent quantities is
zero.}

It follows from $\Ex[XY] = \Ex[X]\Ex[Y]$ for independent $X$ and $Y$, which
follows from the product rule for independent events that
Program~\ref{prog:P23} does state. Writing the expectation as a sum over pairs
of outcomes and factorising it is a first probability course's exercise, and
this program takes the result.

The converse is false, and the book has already met the counterexample: a
quantity \emph{symmetric about zero} and its own square, in
Program~\ref{prog:P24}'s section on what a correlation does not see. The
symmetry is the whole of it \dash{} the die two frames above is not symmetric
about zero, and its cross term with its own square is nowhere near zero.
\end{rigourbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
That is the section. Variances of independent quantities add; the cross term
is what independence removes; and a repeated quantity is not two quantities.

Everything that follows is that identity applied to something larger. The next
section applies it to $n$ copies of one thing, which is what an average is,
and the rate that falls out of it is the one the rest of the program keeps
finding in new clothes.
\end{fr}

\section{The average of many}
\label{sec:P25-average}
\index{concentration!of an average}
\index{measurement!the 1/sqrt(n) rate}

\begin{fr}
An average of $n$ independent draws is a sum divided by $n$, so the last
section already decides what its variance is. Work it out before reading on.

Each draw has variance $\sigma^{2}$. What is the variance of the average of
$n$ of them?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\sigma^{2}/n$}

Two steps and both are in section 1. The sum of $n$ independent draws has
variance $n\sigma^{2}$, because variances add. Dividing a quantity by $n$
divides its variance by $n^{2}$, because the variance squares distances. So
\[ \Var\Bigl(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i\Bigr)
   \;=\; \frac{n\sigma^{2}}{n^{2}} \;=\; \frac{\sigma^{2}}{n} \]
which the script checks as an identity at every $n$ from $1$ to $64$ rather
than at one convenient size.

The spread is the root of a variance. So what does the spread of the average
do as $n$ grows?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{It falls like $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$}

\textbf{That is the rate, and it is the whole of this program's contribution
to practice.} An average concentrates on the thing it is estimating, and the
speed at which it does is fixed: not by the quantity, not by the field, not by
how carefully the data was collected, but by a square root of a count.

Program~\ref{prog:P21} met it in the previous part and \emph{measured} it
\dash{}
twenty thousand values, four thousand trials at each batch size \dash{} while
saying in a rigour box that the reason was here. This is the reason, and it is
four lines of section 1.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The gate is worth naming, because it is what stops the two programs drifting.

Program~\ref{prog:P21}'s population has a spread of $\val{p25.p21.sd}$. The
derivation above says that batches of four times the size have half the
spread, so a batch four times larger than another must land at
$\val{p25.p21.sd.b4}$ against the population figure \dash{} and the script
reads P21's committed number out of its own value file and fails the build if
the two ever disagree.

Neither program can now be corrected without the other noticing. That matters
more than it sounds: P21 measured a law it could not derive, and this program
derives a law it does not measure.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Turn the rate into the sentence you will actually use.

You have an estimate and you want to halve its error bar. How much more data
does that cost?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p25.halve.factor}$ times as much}

Because the count sits under a root. To divide the spread by two you must
multiply $n$ by four; to divide it by ten, by
$\val{p25.tenth.factor}$.

The script checks that the factor is $\val{p25.halve.factor}$ from four
different starting points, which is the part that makes it a rule rather than
an arithmetic coincidence: it does not matter how much data you already have.

\textbf{Every remaining section of this program is that same rate, met
somewhere it is not usually recognised.}
\end{fr}

\section{The shape, and where the theorem is silent}
\label{sec:P25-clt}
\index{central limit theorem!what it promises}
\index{Gaussian!and sums}

\begin{fr}
The width of an average is settled. Its \emph{shape} is a different claim, and
it is the one with a famous name.

\textbf{The central limit theorem}: a sum of many independent quantities, none
of them dominating, has a distribution that approaches a Gaussian \dash{}
whatever the quantities themselves looked like.

That is why a Gaussian turns up so often. Not because nature prefers it, but
because so much of what gets measured is a sum of things nobody enumerated.

A sum of $n$ values drawn uniformly from $\intcc{0}{1}$ has a distribution
with an exact formula, so this is showable rather than assertable. Before the
table: how many uniforms would you guess it takes to be within a per cent of a
Gaussian everywhere?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Three, and only barely; four is comfortably inside.
\end{ansblock}

Measured as the largest gap between the exact distribution and the Gaussian of
the same mean and spread:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rrrrr}
\toprule
$n = 1$ & $n = 2$ & $n = 3$ & $n = 4$ & $n = \val{p25.clt.n.hi}$ \\
\midrule
$\val{p25.clt.gap.1}$ & $\val{p25.clt.gap.2}$ & $\val{p25.clt.gap.3}$ &
$\val{p25.clt.gap.4}$ & $\val{p25.clt.gap.12}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Three uniforms are already closer to a bell than most people would guess, and
twelve are within a quarter of a per cent everywhere. What the script asserts
is the \emph{ordering} rather than the figures, because the ordering is the
theorem and the figures move with the grid.

The convergence is fastest where it is cheapest: most of the distance is gone
by three, and the eight further uniforms between there and twelve buy the last
$\val{p25.clt.gap.3}$ of it.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Now the question this section exists for.

A quantity is a sum of twelve independent pieces, so by the table above it is
close to Gaussian. You want to know how often it exceeds five spreads. Can you
read that off the Gaussian?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
No, and the size of the error is the point.
\end{ansblock}

The same exact distribution, against the Gaussian, in the tail:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
& one spread & three spreads & five spreads \\
\midrule
true & $\val{p25.tail.true1}$ & $\val{p25.tail.true3}$ &
      $\val{p25.tail.true5}$ \\
Gaussian & $\val{p25.tail.gauss1}$ & $\val{p25.tail.gauss3}$ &
      $\val{p25.tail.gauss5}$ \\
ratio & $\val{p25.tail.ratio1}$ & $\val{p25.tail.ratio3}$ &
      $\val{p25.tail.ratio5}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

One spread out the Gaussian is right to within about one and a half per cent.
Five spreads out it is wrong by a factor of $\val{p25.tail.ratio5}$.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{It is a sum of many things, so it is Gaussian.}}

It is Gaussian \emph{in the middle}, which is what the theorem says and all it
says. The error does not sit at some constant small level: it is under a per
cent at one spread and a factor of a hundred and more at five, and it grows in
exactly the direction people take it.

That direction is not an accident of this example. A tail probability is what
you ask for when you want to know whether something was a fluke \dash{} an
outlier, an alert, a result that beat the baseline. So the question the
theorem answers worst is the question it is most often used for.
\end{trapbox}

The extreme case makes it exact rather than merely large. Twelve uniforms have
a mean of six and a spread of exactly $1$, and their sum cannot exceed twelve.
So what is the true probability of exceeding six spreads?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Zero}

Not small \dash{} zero, because six spreads above the mean is the largest
value the quantity can take at all. The Gaussian says
$\val{p25.tail.gauss6}$, and no amount of $n$ will fix it, because the
Gaussian has tails running to infinity and this quantity has a floor and a
ceiling. Most measured quantities do.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{rigourbox}
\textbf{Not proved here: the central limit theorem.}

The statement is above and the demonstration is measured; the proof needs
characteristic functions, which is a second course in probability rather than
a longer section here.

What is worth taking instead is the shape of the hypothesis, because that is
what gets violated in practice rather than the conclusion. The theorem needs
the pieces to be independent, to have a finite variance, and for none of them
to dominate the sum. Heavy-tailed quantities fail the second; a sum over a
population where one member is most of the total fails the third. In both
cases the conclusion does not apply at all, and nothing in the arithmetic will
warn you.
\end{rigourbox}

Program~\ref{prog:P24} left the theorem here with one sentence attached: a
Gaussian used because a sum was involved is being used correctly, and one used
because the data looked bell-shaped is an assumption nobody checked. So what
replaces looking at the histogram?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Three questions about how the number was produced.
\end{ansblock}

What is it a sum of; are those pieces independent; and does any one of them
dominate the total? All three are answerable from the process rather than from
the picture, and all three are the hypothesis rather than the conclusion.

\textbf{That is the difference between the two uses.} The histogram cannot
tell them apart, because both produce a bell.
\end{fr}

\section{What an evaluation run costs}
\label{sec:P25-eval-cost}
\index{measurement!sizing an evaluation}
\index{evaluation!how many items}

\begin{fr}
The same rate, in the place this book's reader meets it most often and prices
least often.

An evaluation set is $n$ items and a model is right on some of them. The
accuracy you report is an average of $n$ independent right-or-wrongs, so
section 2 already knows how far it can be from the truth.

A model that is right $\val{p25.eval.p}$ of the time is measured on
$\val{p25.eval.n.lo}$ items. Within about how many percentage points is the
answer?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\pm\val{p25.eval.hw.100}$ points}

Which is a great deal wider than the two decimal places such a number is
usually quoted to.

A right-or-wrong has variance $p(1-p)$, so an average of $n$ of them has
spread $\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$, and the conventional interval is
$\val{p25.eval.z}$ of those either side \dash{} which is where the customary
$95$ per cent comes from, and why the figure is not quite two spreads.
Nothing here is sampled: it is one square root, and a reader can redo it on
the back of the run's own log line.

Notice what $p(1-p)$ does, because it is the reason two benchmark numbers of
the same size are not equally trustworthy. It is largest at a half and falls
away towards both ends, so a model scoring near chance carries the widest
interval it can have and one scoring near the ceiling carries a narrow one.
The same item count buys more precision at the top of a benchmark than in the
middle of it.

You want that interval halved. Section 2 already answered this; say it in
items rather than in draws.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Four times the items}

Every time, at every starting size:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rr}
\toprule
items & interval \\
\midrule
$\val{p25.eval.n.lo}$ & $\pm\val{p25.eval.hw.100}$ \\
$400$ & $\pm\val{p25.eval.hw.400}$ \\
$1600$ & $\pm\val{p25.eval.hw.1600}$ \\
$\val{p25.eval.n.hi}$ & $\pm\val{p25.eval.hw.6400}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The script asserts the halving between consecutive rows rather than the four
figures, because the halving is the rate and the figures depend on the
accuracy chosen.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Now the question that decides a week of somebody's work.

Two models scoring around $\val{p25.eval.p}$ are compared and one is a
percentage point above the other. How many items does each need to be
evaluated on before that gap means anything?

Give an order of magnitude before reading on \dash{} the point of the question
is the size, not the digits.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p25.eval.n.needed}$ per model}

Twelve thousand, to see one point.

And there is a factor of two inside that number which is the most commonly
dropped step in the whole calculation. \textbf{The quantity you are asking
about is a difference}, and the difference of two independent estimates has
the sum of their variances \dash{} section 1, in the last place anybody looks
for it. So the interval that matters is $\sqrt{2}$ times the one on either
model alone, and the item count is twice what a single interval suggests:
$\val{p25.eval.n.naive}$ against $\val{p25.eval.n.needed}$. Both are rounded
up from counts that are not whole, which is why the second is one short of
twice the first.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{aibox}
\textbf{Where this shows up.} Two lines of the same evaluation report behave
completely differently under it.

A benchmark number quoted as $\num{80.00}$ per cent on
$\val{p25.eval.n.lo}$ items carries an interval of
$\pm\val{p25.eval.hw.100}$ points, so both of those decimal places are noise
\dash{} and the leaderboard sorts on them anyway. Meanwhile a difference
of a point between two rows of that same table is, on that many items, not a
difference at all.

The arithmetic is one square root and it is never printed beside the number.
It costs nothing to compute and it decides whether a run was worth doing.
\end{aibox}

One number before the section closes. Halving an error bar costs four times
the data. What does getting it down to a \emph{tenth} cost?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p25.tenth.factor}$ times}

\textbf{Precision costs quadratically}, and that is worth carrying into every
design review. That is why nobody measures anything
to arbitrary precision, and why \enquote{run it for longer} is a proposal with
a price attached rather than a plan.

It is also the reason the next section matters. When one number in an
architecture grows like $\sqrt{d}$ and another does not, the ratio between
them is not a detail of tuning.
\end{fr}

\section{Why attention divides by a square root}
\label{sec:P25-attention}
\index{attention!the 1/sqrt(d k) scaling}
\index{softmax!saturation}

\begin{fr}
This is the section the book has been pointing at since Part II.

Attention scores a query against a key by their dot product. Both are vectors
of length $d_k$, the head dimension, and at the start of training their
entries are whatever the initialisation made them \dash{} independent, mean
zero, and scaled so that each entry has variance $1$.

So the score is a sum of $d_k$ products. Using section 1 and nothing else:
what is its variance?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$d_k$}

Each term $q_i k_i$ is a product of two independent quantities of mean zero
and variance one, so it has mean zero and variance one itself. The terms are
independent. Variances add. There are $d_k$ of them.

\textbf{So the score has variance $d_k$ and spread $\sqrt{d_k}$}, and there is
nothing else in the derivation. No limit theorem, no approximation: the script
enumerates every sign vector at $d = 1$ to $5$ and gets the answer exactly.

Before spending it, run the same identity on a different scaling. A
\emph{unit} vector's entries have variance $1/d$ rather than $1$. What spread
does the dot product of two of those have?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$1/\sqrt{d}$}

Because $d \times \frac{1}{d} \times \frac{1}{d} = \frac{1}{d}$, and the
spread is its root.

\textbf{Which this book has already measured}, under another name and back in
Part~III. Program~\ref{prog:P05} asked how the cosine between two random
directions behaves as the dimension grows, and committed its spread at four
different dimensions.

\textbf{The two are one theorem at two scalings}, and the derivation
reproduces all four of P05's committed figures to within
$\val{p25.p05.worst.pct}$ per cent \dash{} which the script checks, so the
Part III measurement and this derivation cannot come apart.

Back to the raw score, which has spread $\sqrt{d_k}$ at every head size. You
want a spread of $1$ whatever the head size is. What do you divide by, and why
can the divisor not be a constant?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{By $\sqrt{d_k}$, and a constant cannot do it}

Dividing a raw score by $\sqrt{d_k}$ is not a convention and not a constant
somebody tuned. The quantity being corrected \emph{grows with the head size},
so any fixed divisor is right at one $d_k$ and wrong at every other; the
divisor has to carry the $d_k$ because the thing it cancels does.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Now what that costs when it is left out, which is the half that makes it a
failure rather than an untidiness.

A row of $\val{p25.e9.keys}$ scores goes into a softmax. At head dimension
$\val{p25.e9.d.hi}$ the scores have a spread of about
$\val{p25.e9.raw.sd.512}$ \dash{} they are tens apart rather than ones apart.

What does a softmax do to a row whose entries are tens apart?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{The $1/\sqrt{d_k}$ is a normalisation constant somebody

It is the only number that works, and it is forced by the arithmetic in the
first frames of this section. A constant would not do: the thing being
corrected \emph{grows with the head dimension}, so any fixed divisor is right
at one head size and wrong at every other. A different exponent would not do
either \dash{} $d_k$ is a variance and the spread is its root.

The reason the belief survives is that the number appears in an architecture
diagram, beside genuine choices like the number of heads and the width of the
feed-forward block. It sits among decisions and is not one.

Its own name says so. It is a \textbf{variance correction}, and this program
is where the book can say that without hand-waving: the quantity being
corrected was computed in frame~31 and the correction is its square root.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Two limits on what has just been shown, both worth stating because the section
is otherwise unusually clean.

The derivation assumes the entries are independent with equal variance. That
is true at initialisation, which is when the failure it prevents would happen,
and it becomes less true as training proceeds \dash{} queries and keys learn
structure, and structure is dependence. The scaling stays because nothing that
happens later makes $\sqrt{d_k}$ the wrong order of magnitude, not because the
hypothesis keeps holding.

And E9 is measured here on random vectors rather than on a trained model.
Program~\ref{prog:P32} assembles the architecture and measures it there. This
program's job was to make the number derivable; that one's is to check it
survives contact.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
So the section closes on a sentence the whole book has been assembling.

\textbf{The $1/\sqrt{d_k}$ in attention is the $1/\sqrt{n}$ of section 2, met
in an architecture instead of an average.} Both come from the same line: the
variances of independent quantities add, so a sum of $d$ of them has a spread
that grows like $\sqrt{d}$, and dividing by that root is how you get back to a
scale that does not depend on $d$.

One identity, three names. It has one more to come.
\end{fr}

\section{Why a run can diverge before it starts}
\label{sec:P25-initialisation}
\index{initialisation!signal propagation}
\index{initialisation!He and the factor of two}

\begin{fr}
The fourth name, and it is the one that costs people whole afternoons.

A linear layer takes $n_{\text{in}}$ inputs, multiplies each by a weight and
adds them up. That is a dot product again, so section 5's identity applies
unchanged: the inputs are independent, the weights are independent and drawn
about zero, and the output is their sum.

Notice how the three quantities divide. The fan-in is a count and belongs to
the architecture; the weight variance belongs to the initialiser and the
inputs' size belongs to whatever fed the layer. The identity holds them apart,
which is why the design question below has one answer rather than a family of
them.

Write down the variance of one output, in terms of the fan-in, the weight
variance, and the average of the input squared.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$n_{\text{in}} \times \Var(w) \times \Ex[x^{2}]$}

The same three steps for the third time. The script enumerates it exactly at
four fan-ins and three weight scales, so this is an identity rather than an
approximation, exactly as the attention score was.

\begin{note}
\textbf{The second moment, and not the variance.} They are the same number
when the input is centred, which the first layer's is, so the distinction
costs nothing here and everything two sections down: a $\relu$ output is
non-negative and therefore has a positive mean, so from the second layer on
$\Ex[x^{2}]$ and $\Var(x)$ are different numbers. The script checks the
identity over a centred input alphabet and a non-centred one, and on the
second the variance reading is out by a factor of two.
\end{note}

And it immediately answers the design question. If you want a layer to pass a
signal through without growing or shrinking it, you want that product to be
$1$, which fixes the weight variance at
$1/n_{\text{in}}$ and nothing else.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\mermaidfig{p25-one-rate}{One identity, and where the root comes from.}%
{sum, average, root}

That is the whole of the program's spine, and the rest of this section is what
happens when the fourth instance is got wrong.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Except that a network is not a stack of linear layers, and the thing between
them changes the arithmetic.

A $\relu$ passes its input through unchanged when it is positive and returns
zero when it is negative. Its input at initialisation is symmetric about zero,
because it is a sum of symmetric things.

What fraction of the input's squared size does a $\relu$ pass on?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Exactly half}

Half the inputs are negative and contribute nothing at all; the positive half
contributes exactly what it would have. So the second moment halves, and it
halves exactly, by symmetry rather than by approximation.

The script integrates it against the standard normal rather than sampling it,
and gets $\val{p25.init.relu.keeps}$ \dash{} within
$\val{p25.init.relu.bound}$ of a half, which is the integration and not the
mathematics.

So the design rule has to change. What weight variance holds the signal steady
through a linear layer \emph{and} its $\relu$?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\Var(w) = 2/n_{\text{in}}$}

The weight variance has to make up what the $\relu$ removes:
\[ n_{\text{in}} \times \Var(w) \times \tfrac{1}{2} \;=\; 1
   \qquad\text{so}\qquad \Var(w) \;=\; \frac{2}{n_{\text{in}}} \]

\textbf{That is where the two in He initialisation comes from.} Not from
experiment, not from a hyperparameter sweep: from the observation that a
$\relu$ discards half of a symmetric input's second moment.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Now put depth on it, which is where an untidiness becomes a failure.

Each layer multiplies the signal's variance by the same factor, so over $L$
layers the variance is multiplied by that factor to the power $L$ \dash{}
Program~\ref{prog:F04}'s geometric sequence, applied to a variance instead of
to money.

A network $\val{p25.init.depth}$ layers deep is built with
$\Var(w) = 1/n_{\text{in}}$, the rule for a linear layer, and $\relu$ between
them. What fraction of the signal's \emph{variance} reaches the top?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p25.init.deep.xavier}$}

A half per layer, fifty times. The signal does not shrink gently; it is gone,
and it is gone by the time the forward pass finishes, before any gradient has
been computed or any learning rate has been consulted.

With $\Var(w) = 2/n_{\text{in}}$ the per-layer factor is exactly $1$ and the
figure is $\val{p25.init.deep.he}$. The same depth, the same activation, one
number changed by a factor of two.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\mermaidfig{p25-signal-depth}{What a per-layer factor does over fifty
layers.}{a variance, compounded}

The per-layer factor is one number and the depth is its exponent, so the
tolerance on that number tightens as a network gets deeper. A scheme that was
adequate for a handful of layers stops being adequate the moment depth is the
thing being scaled, and it stops without anybody changing it.

The other direction is worse, because it is loud rather than silent. Set the
weight \emph{scale} to twice what it should be, and say what reaches the top
of the same $\val{p25.init.depth}$ layers.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p25.init.deep.hot}$}

Twice the scale is four times the \emph{variance}, so each layer multiplies
the signal by $\val{p25.init.hot.factor}$ rather than by one.

That is a variance, so the activation itself is around its root, and
Program~\ref{prog:P01} says what either of them is in \code{fp16}: both are
far above the format's ceiling of $\val{p01.fp16.max.exact}$, so both become
\code{inf}. \code{inf} is an ordinary IEEE value with well-defined arithmetic;
\code{nan} arrives one step later, the first time an \code{inf} meets a
subtraction or a multiplication by zero \dash{} a $\softmax$'s max-subtraction,
a normalisation, a loss. So the log says \code{nan} and the first thing
anybody does is halve the learning rate, which cannot help, because no
gradient was ever involved.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{The run diverged, so the learning rate was too high.}}

Sometimes. But a learning rate acts on the \emph{update}, and this failure
happens in the forward pass, before an update exists. The tell is the step
number: a divergence at step one, or a loss that is \code{nan} before the
first evaluation, is almost never the optimiser.

The two are easy to separate and almost nobody does it. Run one forward pass
and print the variance of the activations layer by layer. If it is
multiplying by a constant factor per layer, the initialisation is wrong and
the learning rate is innocent. If it is flat, look at the optimiser \dash{}
which is Program~\ref{prog:P20}'s subject, and where
Program~\ref{prog:P17}'s bound on the step size lives.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{rigourbox}
\textbf{Not covered here: what modern networks actually do about this.}

Residual connections and normalisation layers both change the arithmetic
above, and between them they are why very deep networks train at all despite
the compounding. A residual path adds the input back, so the variance
accumulates rather than multiplies; a normalisation layer resets the scale
outright, which makes the per-layer factor one by construction rather than by
choosing a weight variance.

Neither is proved here, and this book does not measure either. What the
section earns is narrower and prior to both: \textbf{the reason those
mechanisms exist is the geometric sequence above}, and an initialisation
scheme is the cheapest of the three answers to it.
\end{rigourbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
That is the program.

Variances of independent quantities add, and everything else followed. An
average of $n$ draws has a spread that falls like $1/\sqrt{n}$, which prices
every evaluation this book will ever recommend. A sum of many independent
things is Gaussian in the middle and is not Gaussian where you were about to
use it. An attention score is a sum of $d_k$ products, so it has spread
$\sqrt{d_k}$, and dividing by that root is a variance correction rather than a
choice. A layer's output is a sum of $n_{\text{in}}$ products, so its variance
is $n_{\text{in}}\Var(w)$, and getting that number wrong compounds
geometrically with depth.

\mermaidfig{p25-tail-is-silent}{Where the theorem is right, and where it is
asked anyway.}{middle, and outside it}

What is not here is what to do when the pieces are \emph{not} independent, or
when their variance is infinite. Both happen, both break everything above, and
neither is this book's.
\end{fr}

\begin{testexercises}
\item Two independent measurements each have a spread of $3$. Give the spread
  of their sum, and of their difference.
  \answerto{Both are $\sqrt{9 + 9} = \sqrt{18} \approx \num{4.24}$. The
  variances add in \emph{both} cases: subtracting negates one quantity, which
  squares to the same thing, so a difference is no more precise than a sum.
  That is the step section 4 needs and the one people leave out.}
\item An accuracy measured on $1000$ items comes out at $\num{0.90}$. Roughly
  how wide is its $95$ per cent interval, and how many items would halve it?
  \answerto{The spread is $\sqrt{\num{0.9} \times \num{0.1} / 1000} \approx \num{0.0095}$,
  so the interval is about $\pm\num{1.9}$ points. Halving it needs
  $\val{p25.halve.factor}$ times the items, so $4000$.}
\item A quantity is the sum of $30$ independent pieces. A colleague reads its
  one-in-a-million tail off a Gaussian. State the objection in one sentence,
  and say what would make it safe.
  \answerto{The theorem describes the middle and the agreement decays as you
  move out, so a one-in-a-million quantile is the region where it is least
  reliable \dash{} and if the pieces are bounded, the true tail may be exactly
  zero where the Gaussian is merely small. It is safe when the answer is only
  needed near the middle, or when the tail has been checked another way.}
\item A head has dimension $d_k = 64$ and the raw scores come out with a
  spread of about $8$. The head dimension is doubled to $128$. Predict the new
  raw spread, and the new spread after the standard scaling.
  \answerto{The raw spread is $\sqrt{d_k}$, so it becomes
  $\sqrt{128} \approx \num{11.3}$ \dash{} a factor of $\sqrt{2}$, not $2$.
  After dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ it is $1$, as it was at $64$ and as it is at
  every head size. That invariance is the entire purpose of the division.}
\item A $\num{40}$-layer network with $\relu$ activations produces
  \code{nan} on its first training step. Give the first diagnostic to run and
  say what its two outcomes mean.
  \answerto{Run one forward pass and print the variance of the activations
  layer by layer. A constant factor per layer means the weight scale is wrong
  and the optimiser is innocent; a flat profile means the forward pass is
  healthy and the problem is in the update, which is
  Program~\ref{prog:P20}'s.}
\end{testexercises}

\begin{furtherproblems}
\item Show that $\Var(X - Y) = \Var(X) + \Var(Y)$ for independent $X$ and $Y$,
  directly from the general statement in frame~5.
  \answerto{$\Cov(X, -Y) = -\Cov(X, Y) = 0$ by independence, and
  $\Var(-Y) = \Var(Y)$ because the definition squares distances. So the cross
  term vanishes and the two variances add, exactly as for a sum.}
\item An average over $n$ draws has spread $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$. Somebody
  proposes to halve the spread by averaging over the same $n$ draws twice and
  averaging the two results. Say what happens and why.
  \answerto{Nothing happens: the two results are identical, so their average
  is the same number and its spread is unchanged. It is frame~4's trap
  \dash{} $\Var(X+X) = 4\Var(X)$ rather than $2\Var(X)$ \dash{} in the form
  people actually meet it, and the count says $2$ while the arithmetic gets
  $1$.}
\item Attention is sometimes written with the scaling folded into the
  initialisation of the query and key projections instead of applied to the
  scores. Say whether that is the same thing.
  \answerto{For the variance at initialisation it is: scaling both projections
  by $d_k^{-1/4}$ gives entries of variance $d_k^{-1/2}$ each and a score
  variance of $1$. It is not the same afterwards, because training moves the
  weights and only the explicit division is guaranteed to still be there.
  The explicit form also survives a change of head size without a change of
  initialiser.}
\item A network uses an activation that keeps a fraction $c$ of a symmetric
  input's second moment, rather than a $\relu$'s half. Give the weight
  variance that holds the signal steady, and check it against the two cases in
  the text.
  \answerto{$\Var(w) = 1/(c\,n_{\text{in}})$. At $c = 1$ \dash{} a linear
  layer \dash{} that is $1/n_{\text{in}}$, and at $c = \frac{1}{2}$ it is
  $2/n_{\text{in}}$, which are the two the section derives.}
\item An evaluation harness reports accuracy on $200$ items, of which $50$ are
  near-duplicates of others in the set. Say what that does to the interval the
  harness prints, and in which direction.
  \answerto{It makes the printed interval too narrow. The formula assumes
  $200$ independent right-or-wrongs; duplicates carry a positive covariance,
  so the true variance of the average is larger than $\sigma^{2}/n$ by the
  cross terms in frame~5. The reported confidence is an overstatement, and
  the effective item count is somewhere below $200$.}
\end{furtherproblems}
