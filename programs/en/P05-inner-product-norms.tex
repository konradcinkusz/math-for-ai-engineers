\program{Inner product, norms and projection}
\label{prog:P05}
\index{inner product}
\index{vector!norm}

\begin{outcomes}
\outcome{1--9}{say what an inner product adds to a vector space, and what
  cannot be said about a space without one}
\outcome{10--16}{compute three different norms of the same vector, and say what
  each one is measuring}
\outcome{17--23}{project one vector onto another, and say why the answer is
  the closest point rather than merely a point}
\outcome{24--30}{choose between the dot product and the cosine for a given
  index, and say what normalising the vectors decides for you}
\outcome{31--46}{say what a typical pair of high-dimensional vectors looks
  like, and use the tolerance to tell a real claim from a slogan}
\end{outcomes}

\begin{quiz}
\item A vector space has addition and scaling. Name a question about two of
  its vectors that those two operations cannot answer. \teachesat{1--2}
  \answerto{Anything involving size or direction \dash{} how long is this one,
  what angle is between them, are they perpendicular. Combinations alone
  cannot express any of it.}
\item What is the dot product of $(\val{p05.proj.ax}, \val{p05.proj.ay})$ with
  itself, and what is that number to the vector? \teachesat{3--4}
  \answerto{$\val{p05.proj.ax}^{2} + \val{p05.proj.ay}^{2} = 25$, which is the
  square of its length. A vector's length is the square root of its inner
  product with itself, and that is the definition rather than a result.}
\item Two vectors have a dot product of zero. What does that say about them?
  \teachesat{6--7}
  \answerto{They are perpendicular. It is the definition of perpendicular in
  any space with an inner product, and it is the one geometric word that
  survives into dimensions you cannot draw.}
\item A vector is $(3, -4)$. Give its L1 and L2 norms. \teachesat{10--11}
  \answerto{L1 is $3 + 4 = 7$, the sum of the absolute values; L2 is
  $\sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$. They are different numbers because they measure
  different things, not because one is an approximation of the other.}
\item Project $(\val{p05.proj.ax}, \val{p05.proj.ay})$ onto the horizontal
  axis. What is left over, and what is special about it? \teachesat{20--21}
  \answerto{The projection is $(\val{p05.proj.t}, 0)$ and what is left over is
  $(0, \val{p05.proj.dist})$, which is perpendicular to the axis. That
  perpendicularity is exactly why no other point of the axis is nearer.}
\item Your retrieval index ranks by dot product. Somebody normalises the
  embeddings. What have they changed? \teachesat{28--29}
  \answerto{They have made the dot product and the cosine the same query, and
  thrown the lengths away. Whether that is a fix or a loss depends on whether
  the lengths were carrying anything.}
\item Two vectors are drawn at random in a space of $\val{f09.dim}$
  dimensions. Roughly what angle is between them? \teachesat{35--36}
  \answerto{About a right angle, and much more reliably than most people
  expect: $\val{p05.deg.within.768}\%$ of such pairs are within five degrees
  of one. In the plane it is $\val{p05.deg.within.2}\%$.}
\item Exactly $d$ vectors can be mutually perpendicular in $d$ dimensions.
  Roughly how many can be mutually \emph{nearly} perpendicular?
  \teachesat{41--44}
  \answerto{It depends entirely on what \enquote{nearly} means, and that is
  the whole answer. At a tolerance of $\val{p05.near.tol}$ it is fewer than
  $d$ below about $\val{p05.near.cross}$ dimensions and astronomically more
  above it.}
\end{quiz}

\section{What one more operation buys}
\label{sec:P05-inner}
\index{inner product}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:P04} ended by naming what it had withheld. A vector space has
two operations, addition and scaling, and everything that program proved was
proved from those two alone \dash{} which is why the counting bound survives
every later choice about how to measure the space.

The price of that generality is that there is a great deal you cannot say. You
have a space of $\val{f09.dim}$ dimensions and no way to ask how long
anything in it is.

Before reading on: name two questions about a pair of vectors that addition and
scaling cannot answer.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
How long is this one, and what angle is between them.
\end{ansblock}

Perpendicular, nearest, most similar, unit length, orthogonal basis \dash{} none
of it is expressible. Program~\ref{prog:F09} used several of those words freely
and Program~\ref{prog:P04} used none of them, and the difference was not style.
F09 was working in $\R^{n}$ with the arithmetic everybody assumes; P04 was
proving things about spaces in general, and had to give the vocabulary up to do
it.

What follows adds \textbf{one} operation and gets every one of those words back.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
An \emph{inner product} takes two vectors and returns a single number. In
$\R^{n}$ it is the dot product Program~\ref{prog:F09} defined:

\[ a \cdot b = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i b_i \]

Three properties are required of it, and they are the ones you would write down
without being asked: it is symmetric, it is linear in each argument, and
$a \cdot a$ is positive for every $a$ except the zero vector.

That last one is doing more work than it looks. Take
$a = (\val{p05.proj.ax}, \val{p05.proj.ay})$ and compute $a \cdot a$. What is
that number, to the vector?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p05.proj.ax}^{2} + \val{p05.proj.ay}^{2} = 25$, which is the square
of its length.}
So the length is defined \emph{from} the inner product rather than measured
independently of it:

\[ \lVert a \rVert = \sqrt{a \cdot a} \]

and $\lVert a \rVert = \val{p05.proj.alen}$ here. This is the direction of
definition that gets remembered backwards. A length is not a thing the space
came with that the dot product happens to reproduce. \textbf{The inner product
is what a length is made of}, and a space with a different inner product has
different lengths in it.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{note}
The requirement that $a \cdot a > 0$ for every non-zero $a$ is what makes
$\sqrt{a \cdot a}$ a length rather than a formula. Drop it and you can have
non-zero vectors of length zero, and a \enquote{nearest neighbour} that is at
distance nothing from half the space.

That is not a curiosity. It is the reason relativity uses the word
\emph{interval} rather than \emph{distance}: its inner product is not positive
definite, and light travels along paths of zero interval.
\end{note}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Now the word this program exists to make available.

Two vectors are \emph{orthogonal} when their inner product is zero. In two
dimensions that is exactly perpendicular \dash{} check it on $(1, 0)$ and
$(0, 1)$, or on $(\val{p05.proj.ax}, \val{p05.proj.ay})$ and
$(-\val{p05.proj.ay}, \val{p05.proj.ax})$.

The definition never mentions a picture, an angle or a number of dimensions.
So what happens to it at $\val{f09.dim}$ components?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Nothing at all. It is a sum being zero, and it does not know how long the sum
is.
\end{ansblock}

\textbf{Orthogonality is the one piece of geometry that transfers to high
dimension without qualification}, because it was never geometry in the first
place \dash{} it is an arithmetic condition that happens to mean
\enquote{perpendicular} in the two cases you can draw.

Program~\ref{prog:F09} put a warning box around the claims that do not
transfer. This is the other column, and section 5 is about what a space full of
almost-orthogonal directions does with it.
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{p05-what-it-buys}{One operation, and what perpendicular turns
into once you have it.}{what an inner product buys}

\begin{fr}
The last of F08's debts, and you can settle it yourself in one line.

Program~\ref{prog:F08} measured that $a \cdot b = \lVert a \rVert \lVert b
\rVert \cos\theta$ over 3481 pairs, and used it without being able to say what
$\lVert \cdot \rVert$ was. Now it can. Make $\cos\theta$ the subject, and say
what changes about the status of that equation when you do.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\cos\theta = \frac{a \cdot b}{\lVert a \rVert \lVert b \rVert}$, and
it stops reporting the angle and starts defining it.}
In two dimensions this agrees with the protractor. In
$\val{f09.dim}$ it is the \emph{definition} of the angle, there is no
protractor to disagree with it, and every property of $\cos$ you know still
holds because nothing about the derivation used a picture.
\end{fr}

\section{Norms, and what each one measures}
\label{sec:P05-norms}
\index{vector!norm}

\begin{fr}
The length built from the inner product is one way of measuring a vector, and
it is not the only one. A \emph{norm} is any function that assigns a
non-negative size to a vector, is zero only for the zero vector, scales the way
you would want, and obeys the triangle inequality Program~\ref{prog:F09} gave.

Notice what is not on that list. Nothing requires a norm to come from an inner
product, and most do not \dash{} which is why most of them have no notion of
angle attached, and why the previous section's vocabulary does not automatically
travel to every way of measuring a vector.

Three of them come up constantly. Take $v = (3, -4)$ and compute all three:
the sum of the absolute values, the square root of the sum of squares, and the
largest absolute value.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
\[ \lVert v \rVert_1 = 7 \qquad \lVert v \rVert_2 = 5 \qquad
   \lVert v \rVert_\infty = 4 \]
\end{ansblock}

They are called L1, L2 and L-infinity, and they are three different numbers for
one vector. Not three approximations of a true size \dash{} three answers to
three different questions. L1 asks how far you would walk along the axes, L2
how far in a straight line, L-infinity how bad the worst single component is.

Only L2 comes from an inner product. That is why it is the one with angles
attached, and why the other two have no notion of perpendicular at all.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The way to hold the difference is to draw what each calls a unit vector \dash{}
the set of all $v$ with $\lVert v \rVert = 1$.

For L2 it is a circle. For L-infinity it is a square, because the constraint is
on the largest component and the others are free.

What is it for L1?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
A square standing on one corner \dash{} a diamond, with vertices on the axes.
\end{ansblock}

That diamond is the whole of the sparsity argument, and it is worth being
precise about why, because the argument is usually quoted as a slogan.

Minimising a loss subject to a norm being small means finding where the loss's
contours first touch the ball. The L2 ball is round, so it can be touched at
any angle at all and the touching point has no reason to sit on an axis. The L1
ball is not round: it has corners, and its corners are exactly the points where
all but one coordinate is zero.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{The sparsity of L1 is a fact about the shape of the ball, not about the
size of the penalty.}

A contour arriving from a generic direction meets a diamond at a corner far
more often than chance would suggest, and a corner is a sparse vector by
construction. Turn the penalty up on an L2 objective and every coefficient
shrinks together, arriving at zero only in the limit; turn it up on an L1
objective and coefficients reach exactly zero one after another.

So \enquote{L1 gives sparsity} is not a rule of thumb somebody found by
experiment. It is a statement about corners, and it would still be true if the
optimiser were replaced.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
One consequence worth carrying, because it explains a class of confusing
benchmark.

The three norms do not order vectors the same way. Take $a = (3, -4)$ and
$b = (5, 0)$: which is bigger?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Under L2 they are equal, at $5$ each. Under L1, $a$ is bigger, at $7$ against
$5$. Under L-infinity, $b$ is bigger, at $5$ against $4$.
\end{ansblock}

Three norms, three different answers, one pair of vectors, and none of them is
wrong. \textbf{\enquote{Bigger} is not a property of a pair of vectors until a
norm has been named.}

That is the same shape as Program~\ref{prog:F09}'s finding about nearest
against most similar, one level up: there the two measures disagreed about
which of two vectors was closer, here three measures disagree about which is
larger, and in both cases the disagreement is the measures doing their jobs.
\end{fr}

\section{Projection, and what closest means}
\label{sec:P05-projection}
\index{projection}

\begin{fr}
Here is a question that could not be asked two sections ago.

You have a vector $a$ and a line \dash{} all the multiples of some $b$. Which
point of that line is nearest to $a$?

It is worth noticing that this is a minimisation over infinitely many
candidates, and that the answer is going to be a formula with no search in it.
That only happens when something forces it, and the something is
orthogonality.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Write the candidate as $t\,b$ and the leftover as $a - t\,b$. You want the $t$
that makes the leftover shortest.

The trick is to ask a different question, and the different question has a
one-line answer: for which $t$ is the leftover \emph{orthogonal} to $b$?

Set $(a - t\,b) \cdot b = 0$ and solve for $t$.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
\[ t = \frac{a \cdot b}{b \cdot b} \]
\end{ansblock}

Expand $(a - t\,b) \cdot b = a \cdot b - t\,(b \cdot b)$, set it to zero, and
divide \dash{} and state the condition, because $b \cdot b = 0$ only for the
zero vector, which is not a line. That is Program~\ref{prog:F06}'s rule about
dividing by a letter, and the inner product's positivity is what discharges it.

The \emph{projection} of $a$ onto $b$ is then $t\,b$, and it is the answer to
the minimisation even though nothing has been minimised yet.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Which is the step worth being suspicious about. Solving for orthogonality is
not the same as solving for shortest, and the frame has quietly swapped one
for the other.

Take $a = (\val{p05.proj.ax}, \val{p05.proj.ay})$ and $b = (1, 0)$. The formula
gives $t = \val{p05.proj.t}$, so the projection is
$(\val{p05.proj.t}, 0)$ and the leftover is $(0, \val{p05.proj.dist})$.

Why is no other point of that line nearer?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Because Pythagoras says so, and the right angle is what lets it be applied.
\end{ansblock}

Any other point of the line is $t\,b$ for some other $t$, and the distance from
$a$ to it is the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose legs are the leftover and
the distance along the line. A hypotenuse is longer than either leg unless the
other leg is zero.

So the perpendicular is not a construction that happens to work. \textbf{It is
the shortest distance, and orthogonality is the reason rather than a
coincidence of the picture.}
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{p05-closest-point}{Why a perpendicular is the shortest way to a
line.}{why the perpendicular is nearest}

\begin{fr}
\begin{note}
The script does not take that argument on trust. It sweeps
$\val{p05.proj.sweep}$ points along the line and confirms that none is nearer
than the projection, and separately that the leftover's inner product with $b$
is zero to within $10^{-12}$.

The point of doing both is that a formula agreeing with itself is not
evidence. The claim on the page is \enquote{closest}, so what is checked is
closest.
\end{note}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
And the reason this is worth a section of its own rather than a formula.

The derivation used the inner product's three properties and nothing else. No
picture, no two dimensions, no right-angled triangle drawn on paper. So the
same formula projects onto a line in $\val{f09.dim}$ dimensions, and onto a
subspace rather than a line, and \dash{} once Program~\ref{prog:P08} arrives
\dash{} onto the column space of a matrix, which is what least squares is.

Every one of those is the same three lines with a bigger object in them.
\end{fr}

\section{Which measure, and what normalising decides}
\label{sec:P05-choosing}
\index{cosine similarity}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:F09} left a question open and named it.

It worked a query along $(1, 0)$ against two candidates, one short and pointing
almost exactly the right way, one about the query's own length and pointing
less well. The cosine preferred the first and the distance preferred the
second, and neither was wrong. What F09 could not do was tell you which to
choose, because choosing needs the vocabulary this program has now built.

Here are the three numbers for that same pair.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
& length & dot product & cosine \\
\midrule
$A$ & $\val{p05.norm.len.a}$ & $\val{p05.norm.dot.a}$ & $\val{p05.norm.cos.a}$ \\
$B$ & $\val{p05.norm.len.b}$ & $\val{p05.norm.dot.b}$ & $\val{p05.norm.cos.b}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The dot product prefers $B$ and the cosine prefers $A$, exactly as F09 found.
Look at the first column and say what the dot product is doing that the cosine
is not.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Multiplying by the length.
\end{ansblock}

$a \cdot b = \lVert a \rVert \lVert b \rVert \cos\theta$, so the dot product is
the cosine with both lengths in it. $B$ wins on the dot product because it is
$\val{p05.norm.len.b}$ long against $A$'s $\val{p05.norm.len.a}$, and that
factor of about three swamps the cosine's preference.

\textbf{So the two measures are not rivals. One of them is the other one
multiplied by something, and the question is only ever whether you want that
something in the answer.}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Which turns the choice into a question about your data rather than a question
about geometry.

An embedding's length is not decoration. Depending on the model it can track
how frequent the token is, how confident the encoder was, or how much text went
in. Sometimes that is signal you want in the ranking. Sometimes it means your
retrieval quietly prefers long documents.

\textbf{Ask what the length means in your embeddings, and rank by the dot
product if it means something you want and by the cosine if it does not.} That
is the whole decision, and it cannot be made from the formulas.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Now the operation that gets reached for instead of the decision.

\emph{Normalising} replaces every vector by $v / \lVert v \rVert$, which has
length one and the same direction. Do it to both arguments and compute the dot
product. What do you get?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
The cosine, exactly \dash{} the denominator is already one.
\end{ansblock}

Checked over $\val{p05.norm.identity.trials}$ random pairs in two to sixty-four
dimensions, the largest disagreement is $\val{p05.norm.identity.err}$, which is
rounding and not a difference.

So normalising does not make the dot product \emph{behave like} the cosine. It
makes the two queries \textbf{the same query}, and after it there is nothing
left to choose between.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Normalising is not a fix. It is the decision, taken silently, in favour
of throwing the lengths away.}

Read the sentence that way and it stops being a preprocessing step and starts
being a modelling choice: you have declared that nothing your embeddings encode
in their magnitude is worth ranking on. That may well be right. It is not the
kind of thing that should happen in a utility function nobody reviewed.

Program~\ref{prog:F09}'s rule is the operational half of this \dash{} *the
question to ask of a similarity index is not which measure it uses but whether
the vectors going into it were normalised*, because the first question only has
consequences when the answer to the second is no.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\section{What high dimension actually does}
\label{sec:P05-high-dimension}
\index{vector!in high dimension}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:F09} closed with a table in two columns. The left column was
everything that transfers to high dimension unchanged; the right column was
three claims it said do not, and deferred here by name:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X@{}}
\toprule
how many directions are far apart \\
how much of the space is near the middle \\
what a \enquote{typical} pair of vectors looks like \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

All three are questions about angles and lengths, which is why they had to
wait. Take the third one first: two vectors are drawn at random in the plane.
What angle would you expect between them?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Any angle at all. In the plane the angle between two random directions is
uniform, so there is no expected value worth the name.
\end{ansblock}

That is the honest answer in two dimensions, and it is why \enquote{typical}
feels like a word with no content. Every angle is as likely as every other, so
a typical pair is nothing in particular.

Now the same question in $\val{f09.dim}$ dimensions, and the answer is
completely different.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Here is the measurement. $\val{p05.pairs}$ random pairs of unit vectors at each
of six dimensions, with the spread of their cosines:

\transcript{p05-spread}

Before reading the next frame: the four numbers are $\val{p05.cos.sd.2}$,
$\val{p05.cos.sd.10}$, $\val{p05.cos.sd.100}$ and $\val{p05.cos.sd.768}$. Say
what rule they follow.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Each is $1/\sqrt{d}$.}
$1/\sqrt{2} = \num{0.707}$, $1/\sqrt{10} = \num{0.316}$, $1/\sqrt{100} = \num{0.100}$,
$1/\sqrt{768} = \num{0.036}$. Measured against predicted, the worst disagreement over
the whole range is $\val{p05.sd.err.pct}\%$.

That is what the script asserts, rather than any one of the numbers: a
particular measured cosine is a random variable and would move with the seed,
where the rule will not.

\textbf{The cosine between two random directions concentrates at zero, and the
width of that concentration is $1/\sqrt{d}$.}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Put it in degrees, because that is where it stops being a statistic and starts
being surprising.

Out of a hundred random pairs of directions at $\val{f09.dim}$ dimensions, how
many would you guess are within five degrees of a right angle?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p05.deg.within.768}$ of them.}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
$d$ & $2$ & $10$ & $100$ & $\val{f09.dim}$ \\
\midrule
within $5^{\circ}$ of $90^{\circ}$ & $\val{p05.deg.within.2}\%$ &
  $\val{p05.deg.within.10}\%$ & $\val{p05.deg.within.100}\%$ &
  $\val{p05.deg.within.768}\%$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{At $\val{f09.dim}$ dimensions, $\val{p05.deg.within.768}\%$ of random
pairs are within five degrees of perpendicular.} In the plane it is
$\val{p05.deg.within.2}\%$.
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{p05-spread-shrinks}{Why a typical pair becomes a specific thing as
the dimension grows.}{the spread narrows with d}

\begin{fr}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{So a cosine similarity of $\num{0.3}$ between two embeddings is not
\enquote{weakly similar}. It is enormous.}

At $\val{f09.dim}$ dimensions the spread is $\val{p05.cos.sd.768}$, so a cosine
of $\num{0.3}$ sits about eight of those out from what chance produces. Read on the
two-dimensional intuition \dash{} where $\num{0.3}$ is a fairly open angle and means
very little \dash{} it looks like a weak signal, and it is nothing of the kind.

The correction is not to memorise a threshold. It is to divide by
$1/\sqrt{d}$ and ask how many spreads out you are, which is a question with an
answer at any dimension.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The second of F09's three, and it is the one that sounds least likely.

A point is drawn uniformly from a ball of radius $1$ in $d$ dimensions. The
fraction of the ball's volume within radius $r$ is $r^{d}$, which is worth
convincing yourself of before going on: doubling a length scales an area by
four and a volume by eight, and $d$ carries on.

So what fraction of the volume lies in the outer tenth of the radius \dash{}
between $\num{0.9}$ and $1$ \dash{} when $d = 1$, and when $d = 10$?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$\val{p05.shell.outer.1}\%$ and $\val{p05.shell.outer.10}\%$.
\end{ansblock}

$1 - \num{0.9}^{1}$ and $1 - \num{0.9}^{10}$. On a line the outer tenth is a tenth, which
is the answer everybody's intuition gives, and by ten dimensions it is already
two thirds.

At $\val{f09.dim}$ the complement is the number worth printing, because the
fraction itself rounds to a hundred per cent and would be a lie in that form:
the inner nine tenths of the radius hold $\val{p05.shell.inner.768}$ of the
ball. \textbf{Essentially all of a high-dimensional ball is in its skin.}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{note}
Those figures are computed rather than sampled, and the reason is the same one
this book gives for not asserting things it has not run.

The inner half of the radius holds $\val{p05.shell.half.768}$ of a
$\val{f09.dim}$-dimensional ball. No sample of any size anybody can run will
put a point there, so a sampled estimate would report zero \dash{} and finding
nothing is not the same as measuring nothing, which is the shape of an
assertion that cannot fail.
\end{note}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
And the third, which is the debt Program~\ref{prog:P04} named explicitly.

P04 proved that at most $d$ vectors can be mutually orthogonal in $d$
dimensions, and then stopped, saying that relaxing \emph{exactly} orthogonal to
\emph{nearly} orthogonal needs a way to measure \enquote{almost}, which is what
this program has been building.

So relax it. Call two directions nearly orthogonal when their cosine is under
$\val{p05.near.tol}$ in absolute value. How many can be mutually nearly
orthogonal in $64$ dimensions \dash{} more than $64$, or fewer?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Fewer. About $\val{p05.near.n.64}$.}
That is not the answer almost anybody expects, and the draft of this program
asserted the opposite before the script refuted it.

The reason is the previous section. At $d = 64$ the spread is
$1/\sqrt{64} = \num{0.125}$, so a tolerance of $\val{p05.near.tol}$ is only about one
and a half spreads out, and a random pair violates it
$\val{p05.near.tail.64}$ of the time. Ask for a whole set of directions to be
pairwise inside it and you run out almost immediately.

\textbf{At $64$ dimensions, \enquote{nearly orthogonal} at this tolerance is a
harder requirement than \emph{exactly} orthogonal, not an easier one.}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Which is the frame the slogan skips.

\enquote{High dimension gives you exponentially many nearly-orthogonal
directions} is a real result and it is quoted without its condition. The
condition is that the tolerance has to sit several spreads out, and the spread
is $1/\sqrt{d}$, so whether a given tolerance is generous or demanding depends
on the dimension.

There is therefore a crossover: below it the relaxation buys nothing, above it
the capacity runs away. At a tolerance of $\val{p05.near.tol}$, roughly what
dimension is it at?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$d = \val{p05.near.cross}$, where the tolerance sits
$\val{p05.near.cross.sigmas}$ spreads out.}
Above it the numbers stop being comparable to $d$ at all:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
$d$ & $64$ & $\val{f09.dim}$ & $4096$ \\
\midrule
$P(\lvert\cos\rvert > \val{p05.near.tol})$ & $\val{p05.near.tail.64}$ &
  $\val{p05.near.tail.768}$ & $\val{p05.near.tail.4096}$ \\
capacity & $\val{p05.near.n.64}$ & $\val{p05.near.n.768}$ &
  $\val{p05.near.n.4096}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

At $\val{f09.dim}$ dimensions it is about $\val{p05.near.mult.768}$ times $d$.
At $4096$, where exactly $4096$ directions are mutually orthogonal, roughly
$\val{p05.near.n.4096}$ are within $\val{p05.near.tol}$ of it.

\textbf{That gap is what P04 could not measure, and it is why its counting
bound constrains far less than it appears to.}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{rigourbox}
\textbf{The capacity figures are a bound obtained one particular way, and the
way matters.}

They come from the union bound: draw $n$ directions at random and the expected
number of pairs closer than the tolerance is $\binom{n}{2}$ times the
probability for one pair, so the largest $n$ for which that stays under one is
a size you can expect to achieve by drawing at random. A deliberate
construction can do better, and the true maximum for a given tolerance is a
known hard problem in coding theory that this book does not enter.

What it is not is a search result. The first version of this measurement drew
directions and kept the ones that fitted, and reported \emph{fewer} than $d$ at
$d = 64$ for a reason that had nothing to do with the geometry: acceptance
falls off as the kept set grows, so a greedy search stalls long before the
space does. It was measuring the search. The tail probability above is exact.
\end{rigourbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The last frame, and it is about what the reader can now do that F09 could not.

F09 said its right-hand column was a different class of question and named
three of them. All three now have numbers: a typical pair is within
$\val{p05.deg.within.768}\%$-of-five-degrees of perpendicular at
$\val{f09.dim}$ dimensions, essentially all of a ball is in its skin, and the
number of nearly-orthogonal directions depends on a tolerance measured against
$1/\sqrt{d}$ rather than on the dimension alone.

Every one of them came from a single operation that returns a number, added to
a space that had nothing but addition and scaling. Program~\ref{prog:P06} adds
the next object, and the pattern repeats: one definition, and a great deal
follows.
\end{fr}

% ============================================================
\begin{summarybox}
\sumitem{1--2}{\textbf{A vector space with two operations cannot express size or
  direction.} Perpendicular, nearest, unit length, orthogonal basis \dash{}
  none of it. That is why Program~\ref{prog:P04} never used those words and
  Program~\ref{prog:F09} used them freely: F09 was in $\R^{n}$ with the
  arithmetic assumed, P04 was proving things about spaces in general.}
\sumitem{3--5}{An \textbf{inner product} returns one number from two vectors,
  is symmetric, is linear in each argument, and has $a \cdot a > 0$ for every
  non-zero $a$. \textbf{The length is defined from it}, $\lVert a \rVert =
  \sqrt{a \cdot a}$, and not the other way round \dash{} a different inner
  product gives a space different lengths.}
\sumitem{6--9}{Two vectors are \textbf{orthogonal} when their inner product is
  zero, and that definition never mentions a picture, so it is the one piece
  of geometry that transfers to any dimension unchanged. Making $\cos\theta$
  the subject of F08's identity turns it from a report into a
  \textbf{definition} of the angle.}
\sumitem{10--11}{\textbf{Three norms, three questions.} L1 is how far along the
  axes, L2 how far in a straight line, L-infinity how bad the worst component
  is; for $(3, -4)$ they are $7$, $5$ and $4$. Only L2 comes from an inner
  product, which is why only L2 has angles.}
\sumitem{12--14}{\textbf{The L1 ball is a diamond and its corners are the whole
  of the sparsity argument}: a corner is a point where all but one coordinate
  is zero, and a contour arriving from a generic direction meets a diamond at
  one. It is a statement about shape, not about the size of the penalty.}
\sumitem{15--16}{The three norms \textbf{do not order vectors the same way}:
  $(3,-4)$ and $(5,0)$ are equal under L2, and each is larger than the other
  under one of the remaining two. \enquote{Bigger} is not a property of a pair
  of vectors until a norm has been named.}
\sumitem{17--21}{The \textbf{projection} of $a$ onto $b$ is $t\,b$ with $t = (a
  \cdot b)/(b \cdot b)$, obtained by demanding the leftover be orthogonal to
  $b$ \dash{} and it is the \emph{closest} point because Pythagoras makes the
  hypotenuse longer than the leg. The derivation uses only the inner product's
  three properties, so it works at any dimension and on any subspace.}
\sumitem{24--26}{\textbf{The dot product is the cosine with both lengths in
  it.} They disagree only when the lengths differ, and choosing between them is
  a question about what your embeddings encode in their magnitude, which the
  formulas cannot answer.}
\sumitem{27--30}{\textbf{Normalising makes the two queries the same query}, to
  $\val{p05.norm.identity.err}$ over $\val{p05.norm.identity.trials}$ pairs.
  So it is not a preprocessing step \dash{} it is the decision to throw the
  lengths away, taken silently.}
\sumitem{31--37}{\textbf{The cosine between two random directions concentrates
  at zero with spread $1/\sqrt{d}$}, measured to within
  $\val{p05.sd.err.pct}\%$ over three decades. At $\val{f09.dim}$ dimensions
  $\val{p05.deg.within.768}\%$ of pairs are within five degrees of a right
  angle, against $\val{p05.deg.within.2}\%$ in the plane \dash{} so a cosine of
  $\num{0.3}$ is about eight spreads out, and enormous.}
\sumitem{38--40}{\textbf{Essentially all of a high-dimensional ball is in its
  skin}: the fraction inside radius $r$ is $r^{d}$, so the inner nine tenths of
  the radius hold $\val{p05.shell.inner.768}$ of a $\val{f09.dim}$-dimensional
  ball. Computed rather than sampled, because no sample would ever land there.}
\sumitem{41--45}{\textbf{\enquote{Nearly orthogonal} is not automatically
  cheaper than orthogonal.} At $d = 64$ and tolerance $\val{p05.near.tol}$ the
  capacity is $\val{p05.near.n.64}$, below the $64$ that are exact, because
  the tolerance is only about one and a half spreads out. The crossover is at
  $d = \val{p05.near.cross}$; above it the capacity runs away, to about
  $\val{p05.near.n.4096}$ at $4096$ dimensions. \textbf{The slogan is true
  above a threshold and is always quoted without it.}}
\end{summarybox}

\begin{testexercises}
\item State the three properties an inner product must have, and say which one
  makes $\sqrt{a \cdot a}$ a length.
  \answerto{Symmetry, linearity in each argument, and $a \cdot a > 0$ for every
  non-zero $a$. The third: without it a non-zero vector could have length zero,
  and \enquote{nearest} would stop meaning anything.}
\item Compute the L1, L2 and L-infinity norms of $(2, -3, 6)$.
  \answerto{$11$, $7$ and $6$. The L2 norm is $\sqrt{4 + 9 + 36} = \sqrt{49}$,
  which is exact here by construction.}
\item Say why only the L2 norm has a notion of angle attached to it.
  \answerto{Because only L2 comes from an inner product, and the angle is
  defined from the inner product. L1 and L-infinity measure size without ever
  producing a number from a \emph{pair} of vectors.}
\item Project $(2, 5)$ onto the line through $(1, 1)$, and give the leftover.
  \answerto{$t = (2 + 5)/(1 + 1) = \num{3.5}$, so the projection is $(\num{3.5}, \num{3.5})$ and
  the leftover is $(-\num{1.5}, \num{1.5})$. Its inner product with $(1, 1)$ is zero, which
  is the check.}
\item Say why the projection is the nearest point of the line rather than
  merely a point on it.
  \answerto{The leftover is perpendicular to the line, so the distance to any
  other point of the line is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with the
  leftover as one leg. A hypotenuse is longer unless the other leg is zero.}
\item Two candidate vectors have the same cosine with a query and different
  lengths. Say which the dot product prefers and why.
  \answerto{The longer one, because $a \cdot b = \lVert a \rVert \lVert b
  \rVert \cos\theta$ has both lengths in it. The cosine cannot separate them at
  all.}
\item Somebody normalises the embeddings feeding a dot-product index. Say what
  has changed and what decision has been taken.
  \answerto{The dot product and the cosine have become the same query. The
  decision taken is that nothing the embeddings encode in their magnitude is
  worth ranking on \dash{} which may be right, and is a modelling choice rather
  than a preprocessing step.}
\item Give the spread of the cosine between two random unit vectors in $d$
  dimensions, and use it to say how unusual a cosine of $\num{0.3}$ is at
  $\val{f09.dim}$ dimensions.
  \answerto{The spread is $1/\sqrt{d}$, which is $\val{p05.cos.sd.768}$ here,
  so $\num{0.3}$ is about eight spreads out. Far from weak: it is a very large
  similarity by the standards of that space.}
\item Say what fraction of a $d$-dimensional ball lies within radius $r$, and
  what that gives for the inner nine tenths at $\val{f09.dim}$ dimensions.
  \answerto{$r^{d}$, so the inner nine tenths hold $\num{0.9}^{768} =
  \val{p05.shell.inner.768}$ of the volume. Essentially all of the ball is in
  its outermost skin.}
\item At $d = 64$ and a tolerance of $\val{p05.near.tol}$, more or fewer than
  $64$ directions can be mutually nearly orthogonal? Give the reason.
  \answerto{Fewer \dash{} about $\val{p05.near.n.64}$. The spread at $d = 64$
  is $\num{0.125}$, so the tolerance is only about one and a half spreads out and a
  random pair violates it often. The relaxation only becomes cheap above
  $d = \val{p05.near.cross}$.}
\end{testexercises}

\begin{furtherproblems}
\item Show that $\lVert a + b \rVert^{2} = \lVert a \rVert^{2} + 2(a \cdot b) +
  \lVert b \rVert^{2}$ directly from the definition of the norm.
  \answerto{Write the left side as $(a+b) \cdot (a+b)$ and expand using
  linearity in each argument and symmetry. Setting $a \cdot b = 0$ recovers
  Pythagoras, which is why orthogonality and the right angle are the same
  statement.}
\item Show that normalising both vectors makes the dot product equal to the
  cosine.
  \answerto{The cosine is $(a \cdot b)/(\lVert a \rVert \lVert b \rVert)$, and
  after normalising both lengths are $1$, so the denominator is $1$. There is
  nothing left of the difference.}
\item A vector has L1 norm $1$ and $d$ components. Give the largest and
  smallest its L2 norm can be.
  \answerto{Largest $1$, when one component carries everything; smallest
  $1/\sqrt{d}$, when all components are equal. The gap between them widens with
  $d$, which is the sparsity argument stated as a ratio.}
\item Explain why the derivation of the projection never needed a picture, and
  name one thing that lets it project onto.
  \answerto{It used only symmetry, linearity and positivity, none of which
  mentions dimension. So it projects onto a line in any number of dimensions,
  and onto a subspace, and \dash{} once Program~\ref{prog:P08} arrives \dash{}
  onto the column space of a matrix, which is least squares.}
\item Two random unit vectors are drawn in $d$ dimensions. Say how the expected
  angle between them behaves as $d$ grows, and what does not change.
  \answerto{It concentrates on a right angle, with spread $1/\sqrt{d}$. What
  does not change is the expected cosine itself, which is zero at every
  dimension \dash{} it is the \emph{spread} that shrinks, and that is the whole
  effect.}
\item A colleague says high dimension gives you exponentially many
  nearly-orthogonal directions. State the condition their sentence is missing.
  \answerto{That the tolerance must sit several spreads out, and the spread is
  $1/\sqrt{d}$. Below the crossover \dash{} $d = \val{p05.near.cross}$ at a
  tolerance of $\val{p05.near.tol}$ \dash{} the relaxation gives you fewer
  directions than exact orthogonality does.}
\item Say why the capacity figures in this program are a lower bound rather
  than a maximum.
  \answerto{They come from the union bound on \emph{random} draws: the largest
  $n$ for which the expected number of too-close pairs stays under one. A
  deliberate construction can pack more, and the true maximum is a hard problem
  in coding theory this book does not enter.}
\item The first attempt at that measurement drew directions greedily and kept
  the ones that fitted. Say why it reported fewer than $d$ at $d = 64$ and what
  it was really measuring.
  \answerto{Acceptance decays as the kept set grows, because a new direction
  must clear every kept one at once, so the search stalls long before the
  geometry does. It was measuring the search rather than the space \dash{}
  which is why the reported figure comes from the exact tail probability
  instead.}
\end{furtherproblems}
