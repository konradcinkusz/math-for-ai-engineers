\section{Which numbers there are}
\label{sec:F01-sets}
\index{numbers!natural}
\index{numbers!rational}

\begin{fr}
Counting is the oldest thing arithmetic does, and the numbers you count with
are the \emph{natural numbers}: $0, 1, 2, 3, \dots$ and on without end. They
are written $\N$.

Whether $0$ belongs in $\N$ is a convention, and it is not settled: some
countries teach that it does and some that it does not. This book takes
$0 \in \N$ throughout, and says so here rather than letting you find out from
an example.

Now a question. You are asked to record a temperature of three degrees below
zero. Can you do it with a natural number?
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{No. You need a sign as well as a magnitude.}
Extend $\N$ with the negatives and you have the \emph{integers}, $\Z$:
\[ \dots,\; -3,\; -2,\; -1,\; 0,\; 1,\; 2,\; 3,\; \dots \]
Every natural number is an integer. The reverse is not true, which is what
makes this an extension rather than a renaming.

Next: you have three biscuits to divide between two people. Which set do you
need?
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Neither. You need a number between $1$ and $2$.}
A \emph{rational} number is one that can be written as a ratio of two integers,
$p/q$ with $q \ne 0$. The set is written $\Q$. It contains $\tfrac{3}{2}$, and
it contains $-17$ (as $-17/1$), and it contains $\num{0.1}$ (as $1/10$).

Write down the decimal expansion of $\tfrac{1}{3}$.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\num{0.333}\dots$, repeating without end.}
A rational number's decimal expansion either stops or repeats for ever. It
never does anything more interesting than that. $\tfrac{1}{3}$ repeats;
$\tfrac{1}{4} = \num{0.25}$ stops; $\tfrac{1}{7} = \num{0.142857142857}\dots$ repeats
with a period of six.

That $\tfrac{1}{3}$ cannot be written down exactly in decimal is not a fact
about $\tfrac{1}{3}$. It is a fact about \emph{base ten}. In base three,
$\tfrac{1}{3}$ is exactly $\num{0.1}$.

Hold that thought. Now: is $\num{0.1}$ a simple number, or an awkward one?
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Simple. It is $\tfrac{1}{10}$, one digit, exact.}
That is the right answer in base ten and it is the wrong answer about your
computer, and the gap between those two sentences is the subject of
Program~\ref{prog:P01}.

\begin{trapbox}
Your machine does not work in base ten. It works in base two, and in base two
$\tfrac{1}{10}$ behaves exactly the way $\tfrac{1}{3}$ behaves in base ten: it
repeats for ever, and any finite machine has to stop writing it down somewhere.

So the number your program holds when you type \code{0.1} is not $\num{0.1}$.
It is the nearest number the machine can represent, and it is not the same. It
is not a small inaccuracy in a big calculation; it is there in a single
assignment, before any arithmetic has happened.

Nothing in this program depends on that. Everything in
Program~\ref{prog:P01} does.
\end{trapbox}

There are also numbers that are not rational at all. $\sqrt{2}$ is one, and
$\pi$ is another: their decimal expansions neither stop nor repeat. Together
with the rationals they make the \emph{real} numbers, $\R$.
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{f01-number-sets}{%
  The four sets, nested, and the one that is not nested with them. What a
  machine stores is a finite subset of the rationals \dash{} so it holds
  neither all of $\Q$ nor any irrational number exactly.}{%
  Nested number sets, with the machine's floating-point set shown as a finite
  subset of the rationals.}

\begin{fr}
Classify each of these. Write $\N$, $\Z$, $\Q$ or $\R$ for the \emph{smallest}
set each one belongs to:
\[ 7, \qquad -17, \qquad \tfrac{22}{7}, \qquad \sqrt{2}, \qquad \sqrt{9} \]
\dotline
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$7 \in \N$. \quad $-17 \in \Z$. \quad $\tfrac{22}{7} \in \Q$. \quad
$\sqrt{2} \in \R$ and nothing smaller. \quad $\sqrt{9} \in \N$, because
$\sqrt{9} = 3$.

The last one is the one worth pausing on. $\sqrt{9}$ \emph{looks} irrational
and is not. The way a number is written is not the same thing as the set it
lives in, and a good many mistakes in this book's later programs come from
reading a form instead of a value.
\end{ansblock}

That is the whole of the vocabulary you need. On to the tool you will use
every day.
\end{fr}

\section{Powers}
\label{sec:F01-powers}
\index{indices!laws of}
\index{powers}

\begin{fr}
$a^{n}$, read \enquote{$a$ to the power $n$}, means $a$ multiplied by itself
$n$ times. $a$ is the \emph{base}; $n$ is the \emph{index}, or the exponent.
\[ 2^{5} = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \]
Work it out.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$2^{5} = 32$}
Now put two powers of the same base side by side:
\[ 2^{3} \times 2^{4} = (2\times2\times2) \times (2\times2\times2\times2) \]
Count the twos on the right. There are seven of them, so the product is
$2^{7}$. Nothing was memorised; the exponents were counted.

That is the first law of indices:
\[ a^{m} \times a^{n} = a^{m+n} \]
Use it: $10^{3} \times 10^{6} = \blank$
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$10^{9}$}
Division works the same way in reverse. In $2^{5} / 2^{3}$ there are five twos
on top and three underneath; three cancel, two are left:
\[ \frac{a^{m}}{a^{n}} = a^{m-n} \]
And a power of a power multiplies:
\[ (a^{m})^{n} = a^{mn} \]
because $(2^{3})^{4}$ is four copies of three twos.

Now a question that is not what it looks like. Is $2^{3} \times 3^{2}$ equal to
$6^{5}$?
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{No. $2^{3} \times 3^{2} = 8 \times 9 = 72$, while $6^{5} = 7776$.}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Every law of indices requires a common base.} $a^{m}\times a^{n} =
a^{m+n}$ says nothing whatever about $a^{m} \times b^{n}$, and there is no rule
that combines them. The temptation to add the exponents anyway is strong,
because the pattern looks the same on the page, and it is the single most
common error in this material.

When the bases differ, you have two separate numbers. Compute them and
multiply.
\end{trapbox}
Now the two cases that look like exceptions and are not. What is $5^{0}$?
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$1$.}
That is not a definition adopted for convenience. It follows:
\[ 1 = \frac{5^{3}}{5^{3}} = 5^{3-3} = 5^{0} \]
The division law forces it. Anything non-zero over itself is $1$, and the law
says that quotient is $a^{0}$; so $a^{0}$ must be $1$ for every $a \ne 0$.

Run the same argument one step further:
\[ \frac{5^{3}}{5^{5}} = 5^{3-5} = 5^{-2} \quad\text{and also}\quad
   \frac{5^{3}}{5^{5}} = \frac{1}{5^{2}} \]
So what does a negative index mean?
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{A reciprocal: $a^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{a^{n}}$.}
A negative index is not a negative number. $2^{-3} = \tfrac{1}{8}$, which is
small and positive. The sign of the index says which way the division goes; it
says nothing about the sign of the result.

Simplify each of these, leaving no negative indices:
\[ 3^{2} \times 3^{-5}, \qquad \frac{7^{-2}}{7^{-4}}, \qquad (2^{-1})^{-3} \]
\dotline
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$3^{2} \times 3^{-5} = 3^{-3} = \tfrac{1}{27}$.

$\dfrac{7^{-2}}{7^{-4}} = 7^{-2-(-4)} = 7^{2} = 49$. Subtracting a negative
adds; this is where sign slips happen, so do it in two steps rather than one.

$(2^{-1})^{-3} = 2^{3} = 8$.
\end{ansblock}

\yourturn
Simplify $\dfrac{(x^{2})^{3} \times x^{-4}}{x^{-1}}$, giving one power of $x$.
\dotline
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$(x^{2})^{3} = x^{6}$; then $x^{6} \times x^{-4} = x^{2}$; then
$x^{2}/x^{-1} = x^{2+1} = x^{3}$.
\end{ansblock}

\begin{aibox}
This is not throat-clearing. A transformer's attention step costs on the order
of $n^{2}d$ operations for a sequence of length $n$ and width $d$, and its
memory for a cached key--value pair grows like $n d$. Every argument in this
book about what long context costs is an argument about the exponent on $n$,
and you cannot follow it without the fluency this section is building.
\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\section{Roots}
\label{sec:F01-roots}
\index{roots}
\index{indices!fractional}

\begin{fr}
A square root undoes a square. $\sqrt{64} = \blank$
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$8$, because $8^{2} = 64$.}
Roots and indices are the same machinery. Suppose $\sqrt{a} = a^{k}$ for some
index $k$. Then
\[ a^{k} \times a^{k} = a^{2k} \quad\text{must equal}\quad a^{1} \]
so $2k = 1$. What is $k$?
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$k = \tfrac{1}{2}$, so $\sqrt{a} = a^{1/2}$.}
The same argument with an $n$th root gives $\sqrt[n]{a} = a^{1/n}$, and
combining it with the power-of-a-power law gives the general form:
\[ a^{m/n} = \left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^{m} = \sqrt[n]{a^{m}} \]
Both orders give the same answer, and one of them is usually much easier
arithmetic than the other.

\textbf{Worked.} $81^{3/4}$. Take the root first: $\sqrt[4]{81} = 3$, then
$3^{3} = 27$. Taking the power first would have meant finding the fourth root
of $531441$, which is the same answer by a worse road.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\textbf{Worked, with gaps.} Evaluate $32^{2/5}$.

The fifth root of $32$ is $\blank$, because $2^{5} = 32$.

Raising that to the power $2$ gives $\blank$.
\dotline
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$\sqrt[5]{32} = 2$, and $2^{2} = 4$. So $32^{2/5} = 4$.
\end{ansblock}

A negative fractional index combines both rules and nothing new:
\[ 16^{-3/4} = \frac{1}{16^{3/4}} = \frac{1}{(\sqrt[4]{16})^{3}}
             = \frac{1}{2^{3}} = \frac{1}{8} \]

\yourturn
Evaluate $\left(\dfrac{27}{8}\right)^{-2/3}$.
\dotline
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
The negative index inverts the fraction: $\left(\tfrac{8}{27}\right)^{2/3}$.
The cube root of $\tfrac{8}{27}$ is $\tfrac{2}{3}$; squared, $\tfrac{4}{9}$.
\end{ansblock}

One more question before we leave roots, and it is a trap. Is
$\sqrt{9 + 16}$ equal to $\sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16}$?
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{No. $\sqrt{25} = 5$, and $3 + 4 = 7$.}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{A root does not distribute over a sum.} Neither does a power:
$(a+b)^{2} \ne a^{2} + b^{2}$, which Program~\ref{prog:F02} takes apart
properly.

Distribution over a \emph{product} is fine \dash{}
$\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\sqrt{b}$ \dash{} and that is exactly what makes the
error easy to make: one of the two looks like the other, and only one of them
is true.

You will meet this again in a form that costs real money. The norm of a sum of
vectors is not the sum of the norms, and the assumption that it is turns up in
plausible-looking arguments about embeddings. Program~\ref{prog:P05} deals with
it there.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\section{Scientific notation}
\label{sec:F01-scientific}
\index{scientific notation}
\index{numbers!orders of magnitude}

\begin{fr}
Here is a number from the exercise you will do in a few frames:
\[ \val{f01.weights.bytes} \]
Count its digits. Now imagine comparing it, by eye, with $1400000000$ and
saying which is bigger and by how much. The difficulty is not arithmetic; it
is that a run of zeros carries no shape.

\emph{Scientific notation} \dash{} also called standard form \dash{} writes
every number as
\[ m \times 10^{e}, \qquad 1 \le m < 10, \quad e \text{ an integer} \]
$m$ is the mantissa and carries the precision; $e$ is the exponent and carries
the size. Write $4\,500$ in that form.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\num{4.5e3}$}
The exponent is the number of places the point moves. Moving it left makes the
exponent go up, because you are compensating for a smaller mantissa.

Small numbers move the point the other way, so the exponent is negative:
\[ \num{0.00042} = \num{4.2e-4} \]
Convert $\num{0.0000031}$.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\num{3.1e-6}$}
Arithmetic in standard form is where it earns its keep. \textbf{Multiplication}
multiplies the mantissas and adds the exponents:
\[ (\num{3e8}) \times (\num{2e-3})
   = (3 \times 2) \times 10^{8 + (-3)} = \num{6e5} \]
\textbf{Division} divides and subtracts:
\[ \frac{\num{8e12}}{\num{4e4}} = 2 \times 10^{12-4} = \num{2e8} \]
Do one: $(\num{6e-2}) \times (\num{5e7}) = \blank$
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\num{3e6}$. The mantissas give $30$, which is renormalised to
$3 \times 10^{1}$, and $10^{1} \times 10^{5} = 10^{6}$.}
That renormalisation step is where the mistakes are: $30 \times 10^{5}$ is
correct arithmetic and is not standard form, and if you leave it there you will
mis-read your own exponent later.

Now addition, which does \emph{not} work the same way. What is
$(\num{3e8}) + (\num{2e7})$?
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\num{3.2e8}$}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{You cannot add exponents when you are adding numbers.} There is no
short cut here at all: the two quantities must be brought to a common exponent
first, and only then do the mantissas add.
\[ \num{3e8} + \num{2e7} = \num{3e8} + \num{0.2e8} = \num{3.2e8} \]
The reason this matters beyond arithmetic: it is the same reason a floating-
point sum of numbers with very different exponents loses the small one
entirely. Line up the exponents, and the small number's digits fall off the end
of the mantissa. Program~\ref{prog:P02} measures exactly that.
\end{trapbox}

\yourturn
Evaluate $(\num{7.2e5}) + (\num{4e4})$, then
$\dfrac{\num{1.5e-3}}{\num{5e2}}$.
\dotline
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$\num{7.2e5} + \num{0.4e5} = \num{7.6e5}$.

$\dfrac{\num{1.5}}{5} = \num{0.3}$ and $10^{-3-2} = 10^{-5}$, giving $\num{0.3} \times 10^{-5}$,
which renormalises to $\num{3e-6}$.
\end{ansblock}
\end{fr}

\section{Orders of magnitude}
\label{sec:F01-magnitudes}
\index{measurement!estimation}
\index{orders of magnitude}

\begin{fr}
An \emph{order of magnitude} is the exponent alone. Two quantities are
\enquote{the same order of magnitude} if their ratio is somewhere near one, and
\enquote{three orders of magnitude apart} if the ratio is about a thousand.

This is a coarse instrument on purpose. Most of the questions worth asking
before you build something \dash{} will this fit in memory, will this finish
this week, will this cost more than the project \dash{} are answered by an
exponent, and answering them to three significant figures is work you did not
need to do.

Here is the estimator's basic move. In binary, sizes go up in powers of two,
and $2^{10} = 1024$. What decimal power is that near?
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$10^{3}$. So $2^{10} \approx 10^{3}$, and a factor of $1024$ can be read
as a factor of a thousand.}
$2^{10}$ exceeds $10^{3}$ by \val{f01.kib.over.si.pct}\%, which for a
back-of-envelope answer is nothing.

Say it that way round, and always. \enquote{The approximation is low by
\val{f01.kib.over.si.pct}\%} is a different claim about a different
denominator, and the two answers are not the same number. This is
\S\ref{sec:F01-ratios} arriving eighteen frames early, and it will keep
arriving.

Now push it. $2^{80}$ is eight of those factors stacked up, so it should be
about $10^{24}$. Is the error still nothing?
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{No. $2^{80} = \val{f01.two.eighty}$, which is
\val{f01.two.eighty.err.pct}\% above $10^{24}$.}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{A small relative error compounds into a large one.} Each factor of
$1024$ carries \val{f01.kib.over.si.pct}\% and the errors multiply rather than
add, so eight of them is not eight times \val{f01.kib.over.si.pct}\% \dash{}
it is \val{f01.two.eighty.err.pct}\%.

The rule to carry away: an approximation is safe once, and a repeated
approximation needs checking. It is the same arithmetic that makes compound
interest surprising, and it is the same arithmetic that will make a per-layer
error bound in Program~\ref{prog:P02} much worse than you expect after
ninety-six layers.
\end{trapbox}

Estimating in standard form is now three steps: round each quantity to one
significant figure, add or subtract the exponents, and renormalise.
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{f01-magnitudes}{%
  The quantities this book keeps returning to, on one ladder. Every step is
  an exponent, and every question about cost is a subtraction along it.}{%
  A logarithmic ladder from a single token to the FLOPs of a training run.}

\begin{fr}
\textbf{Worked.} A novel runs to about \val{f01.novel.words} words. A rule of
thumb puts an English word at about three quarters of a token. Roughly how
many tokens is the novel?

\[ \frac{\num{1e5}}{\num{0.75}} \approx \num{1.3e5} \]

So a little over $10^{5}$ tokens, and \val{f01.novel.tokens} on the nose. A
context window of $10^{5}$ tokens holds about one novel, which is a far more
useful thing to remember than the exact figure.

\begin{note}
The three-quarters figure is a rule of thumb, not a measurement, and it varies
with the tokeniser, the language and the subject matter. It is quoted here as
judgement. Polish, with its inflections, tokenises less efficiently than
English in most tokenisers built primarily on English text, and this book has
not measured by how much.
\end{note}

\yourturn
A device performs about $\num{4e14}$ floating-point operations per second. How
many seconds would $\num{8e22}$ operations take, at that rate?
\dotline
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$\dfrac{\num{8e22}}{\num{4e14}} = 2 \times 10^{22-14} = \num{2e8}$ seconds.

And $\num{2e8}$ seconds is worth converting while you are here: a year is about
$\num{3e7}$ seconds, so this is about six or seven years. On one device.
\end{ansblock}

That number is not hypothetical, and the rest of this program is where it comes
from.
\end{fr}

\section{Sizing a model}
\label{sec:F01-sizing}
\index{measurement!model memory}
\index{parameters!counting}

\begin{fr}
Everything so far has been arithmetic. This section is the reason it was worth
doing.

A model's weights are numbers. If you know how many numbers there are, and how
many bytes each one takes, you know how much memory the weights need. That is
the whole calculation:
\[ \text{bytes} = \text{parameters} \times \text{bytes per parameter} \]
A \enquote{7B} model has \val{f01.params} parameters. Stored in a two-byte
format, how many bytes is that?
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{f01.weights.bytes}$ bytes.}
In standard form that is
\[ (\num{7e9}) \times 2 = \num{1.4e10} \text{ bytes} \]
and since $10^{9}$ bytes is a gigabyte, \val{f01.weights.gb}~GB.

You have just derived, from two facts you already had, the number that gets
quoted at you constantly. It is worth noticing how little was involved.

Now: the same model in a four-byte format needs how much?
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{\val{f01.weights.fp32.gb}~GB. Double the bytes per parameter, double the
memory.}
\begin{aibox}
This is why the choice of numeric format is a first-order engineering decision
rather than a detail. Halving the bytes per parameter halves the weight memory
exactly, and it is the cheapest lever available: no retraining, no architecture
change, one flag.

What it costs \dash{} what precision you give up, why \code{bf16} became the
training format while \code{fp16} needs a scaling trick to work at all, and
what \enquote{two bytes} actually buys you \dash{} is
Program~\ref{prog:P01}, and it is the reason that program comes before the
linear algebra rather than after it.
\end{aibox}

\begin{warning}
\textbf{The weights are not the whole memory bill,} and a reader who leaves
this section thinking they are will under-provision every machine they ever
size. Training additionally holds optimiser state and activations; serving
holds a growing cache of keys and values that scales with how much text is in
flight. Both are counted properly in Program~\ref{prog:P03}.

What you have here is a floor, and floors are useful: if the weights alone do
not fit, nothing else matters.
\end{warning}

\yourturn
A model has \val{f01.big.params} parameters and is stored two bytes to the
parameter. How many gigabytes are its weights?
\dotline
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$(\num{7e10}) \times 2 = \num{1.4e11}$ bytes, which is \val{f01.big.gb}~GB.
\end{ansblock}

Worth a moment: \val{f01.big.gb}~GB is more memory than any single accelerator
in common use carries today, which is why a model of that size has to be split
across several devices before anybody can run it at all. That the split is
necessary follows from the arithmetic; how it is done, and what it costs, does
not, and this book does not cover it.
\end{fr}

\section{Gigabytes and gibibytes}
\label{sec:F01-prefixes}
\index{troubleshooting!GB versus GiB}
\index{units!binary prefixes}

\begin{fr}
You computed \val{f01.weights.gb}~GB. You load the model, look at what your
tooling reports, and it says \textbf{\val{f01.weights.gib}~GiB}.

Which of the two is wrong?
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Neither. They are different units, and they are both describing the same
$\val{f01.weights.bytes}$ bytes.}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{A gigabyte is $10^{9}$ bytes. A gibibyte is $2^{30}$ bytes.} They are
not the same size, and they are not approximately the same size once they are
stacked:
\[ 2^{30} = \val{f01.gib.bytes}, \qquad
   \text{which is } \val{f01.gib.over.si.pct}\% \text{ more than } 10^{9} \]
So $\val{f01.weights.bytes}$ bytes is \val{f01.weights.gb}~GB \emph{and}
\val{f01.weights.gib}~GiB. Both statements are exact. Neither tool is lying.

The habit that saves you: whenever a memory figure disagrees with your
arithmetic by something in the region of seven per cent, this is why. It is the
single most common false alarm in capacity planning, and it costs an afternoon
the first time.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{f01-prefixes}{%
  The two prefix ladders and the gap between them. The gap compounds: it is
  \val{f01.kib.over.si.pct}\% at kilo and \val{f01.tib.over.si.pct}\% at tera,
  which is the same compounding as $2^{80}$ against $10^{24}$.}{%
  SI decimal prefixes against IEC binary prefixes, with the widening gap.}

\begin{fr}
The two ladders, side by side:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}llrX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Decimal} & \textbf{Binary} & \textbf{Binary is larger by} &
  \textbf{Where you meet it} \\
\midrule
kB $= 10^{3}$  & KiB $= 2^{10}$ & \val{f01.kib.over.si.pct}\% & File sizes \\
MB $= 10^{6}$  & MiB $= 2^{20}$ & \val{f01.mib.over.si.pct}\% & Model shards \\
GB $= 10^{9}$  & GiB $= 2^{30}$ & \val{f01.gib.over.si.pct}\% & Device memory \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\noindent
As a rough division of the world: hardware vendors and storage sell in decimal,
because it makes the number bigger; operating systems and accelerator tooling
report in binary, because that is how the addresses work. The abbreviations are
frequently written the same way even when the unit is not, so read the tool
rather than the letters.

\yourturn
A device advertises 80~GB of memory. How many gibibytes is that, and will
\val{f01.big.gb}~GB of weights fit on one?
\dotline
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
$80 \times 10^{9}$ bytes divided by $2^{30}$ is about $\num{74.5}$~GiB.

And no: \val{f01.big.gb}~GB of weights does not fit in 80~GB, before anything
else is loaded at all. The answer would have been \enquote{it is close} without
the arithmetic, and it is not close.
\end{ansblock}
\end{fr}

\section{Ratios, percentages and the word \enquote{faster}}
\label{sec:F01-ratios}
\index{measurement!reporting a speed-up}
\index{percentages}

\begin{fr}
A request took \val{f01.base.ms}~ms. Someone reports it is now
\enquote{fifty per cent faster}.

How long does it take?
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{You cannot tell, and that is the point.}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\enquote{Fifty per cent faster} has two readings, and they differ by
\val{f01.speedup.discrepancy.ms}~ms.}

\emph{Half the time}: $\val{f01.base.ms} \times \num{0.5} =
\val{f01.fifty.pct.less.ms}$~ms.

\emph{One and a half times the rate}: $\val{f01.base.ms} / \num{1.5} =
\val{f01.fifty.pct.more.rate.ms}$~ms.

Both are ordinary English and both are in common use. The discrepancy here is
a third of the original, which is larger than most of the improvements anybody
reports.

\textbf{Say the number.} \enquote{Latency fell from \val{f01.base.ms}~ms to
\val{f01.fifty.pct.less.ms}~ms} cannot be misread. A percentage improvement
almost always can be, and a table of them is worse, because the reader has no
way to recover the quantities.
\end{trapbox}
Two more that are worth being exact about. If a rate goes from 4\% to 6\%, has
it risen by two per cent or by fifty per cent?
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Two percentage \emph{points}, which is a fifty per cent relative
increase.}
Both descriptions are correct and they sound wildly different, which is why the
distinction has its own vocabulary. When the quantity is itself a percentage,
say \emph{percentage points} for the difference and \emph{per cent} for the
ratio, and the sentence stops being ambiguous.

Last one. Is \enquote{three times faster} the same as \enquote{three times the
time}?
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{No \dash{} they are reciprocals of each other. Three times faster is a
third of the time.}
A speed-up is a ratio of rates; a duration is the reciprocal of a rate. Writing
\enquote{$3\times$} beside a latency figure leaves the reader to guess which
one you meant, and half of them will guess wrong.

\begin{aibox}
This is the last section of the program and it is the one that will come back
most often. Almost every claim you will read about a model \dash{} faster,
cheaper, better \dash{} is a ratio quoted without its two quantities.
Program~\ref{prog:P33} is about reading those claims properly, and
Program~\ref{prog:P26} is about deciding whether the difference in one is real
at all. The habit starts here: recover the two numbers, or do not repeat the
claim.
\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\begin{summarybox}
\sumitem{1--5}{$\N \subset \Z \subset \Q \subset \R$. This book takes
  $0 \in \N$. A rational's decimal expansion stops or repeats; nothing else
  does. What repeats depends on the \emph{base}, which is why $\num{0.1}$ is
  exact in base ten and is not exact in your machine.}
\sumitem{9--11}{$a^{m}a^{n} = a^{m+n}$, \quad $a^{m}/a^{n} = a^{m-n}$, \quad
  $(a^{m})^{n} = a^{mn}$. All three require a \emph{common base}.}
\sumitem{12--13}{$a^{0} = 1$ and $a^{-n} = 1/a^{n}$, both forced by the
  division law rather than defined for convenience.}
\sumitem{17--18}{$a^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{a}$ and $a^{m/n} =
  (\sqrt[n]{a})^{m}$. Take the root first; the arithmetic is smaller.}
\sumitem{22}{$\sqrt{a+b} \ne \sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b}$. Roots distribute over a
  product, never over a sum.}
\sumitem{23--24}{Scientific notation is $m \times 10^{e}$ with
  $1 \le m < 10$.}
\sumitem{25--26}{Multiplying adds the exponents; dividing subtracts them; then
  renormalise the mantissa.}
\sumitem{27}{Adding requires a \emph{common exponent} first. This is also why a
  floating-point sum loses a small addend.}
\sumitem{30--31}{$2^{10}$ exceeds $10^{3}$ by
  \val{f01.kib.over.si.pct}\% \dash{} and state it that way round, because
  the other denominator is a different number. Repeated, the error compounds:
  $2^{80}$ is \val{f01.two.eighty.err.pct}\% above $10^{24}$.}
\sumitem{34--36}{Weight memory $=$ parameters $\times$ bytes per parameter.
  \val{f01.params} parameters at two bytes is \val{f01.weights.gb}~GB.}
\sumitem{36}{That is a \emph{floor}. Optimiser state, activations and the
  key--value cache are counted in Program~\ref{prog:P03}.}
\sumitem{38--39}{GB $= 10^{9}$; GiB $= 2^{30}$, larger by
  \val{f01.gib.over.si.pct}\%. A seven per cent disagreement between your
  arithmetic and your tooling is almost always this.}
\sumitem{42--43}{\enquote{Fifty per cent faster} is ambiguous by
  \val{f01.speedup.discrepancy.ms}~ms on a
  \val{f01.base.ms}~ms baseline. Quote the two numbers instead.}
\sumitem{44}{Percentage \emph{points} for a difference between percentages;
  \emph{per cent} for the ratio.}
\end{summarybox}
