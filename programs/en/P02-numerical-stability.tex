\begin{quiz}
\item Five readings are $\val{p02.var.offset}$ and the four microseconds after
  it. Their variance is computed in \code{float32} by
  $\Ex[x^{2}] - (\Ex[x])^{2}$. What comes back? \teachesat{2--3}
  \answerto{$\val{p02.var.naive}$. A variance cannot be negative, and the true
  answer is $\val{p02.var.true}$.}
\item Two numbers agree to nine significant figures and are subtracted in
  \code{float32}. How many significant figures does the answer have?
  \teachesat{5--6}
  \answerto{None. \code{float32} carries about seven, so the two are stored
  identically in the digits that agree and the difference is made entirely of
  what was left after rounding.}
\item An attention score reaches $\val{p02.over.fp16.int}$. What does its
  exponential do in \code{fp16}? \teachesat{14--15}
  \answerto{Overflows to \code{inf}. The ceiling is passed at a score of
  $\val{p02.over.fp16}$, and everything downstream becomes \code{nan}.}
\item $\ln\sum_i e^{z_i} = c + \ln\sum_i e^{z_i - c}$ holds for every $c$. So

  at or below zero, so it is the only one guaranteed not to overflow for every
  input rather than for this input.}
\item You need $\ln \sigma(x)$ at $x = \val{p02.logsig.under}$. What does
  computing $\sigma$ first give you? \teachesat{24--25}
  \answerto{\code{-inf}, because $\sigma$ has underflowed to
  exactly zero. Computed as one operation the answer is
  $\val{p02.logsig.stable}$.}
\item $\val{p02.sum.n}$ values of $\val{p02.sum.small}$ are added one at a time
  to a \code{float32} running total that starts at $1$. What is the total?
  \teachesat{29--30}
  \answerto{$\val{p02.sum.naive32}$. Every one of them is below half a gap at
  $1$, so not one of them moves the total.}
\item Name two ways of fixing that sum, and say which is better.
  \teachesat{31--35}
  \answerto{Any two of: add smallest first, compensated (Kahan) summation, a
  pairwise tree, a wider accumulator. Sorting recovers
  $\val{p02.sum.got.sorted}$\% of the contribution and the other three recover
  more than $\val{p02.sum.got.kahan}$\%.}
\item Why is a cross-entropy computed from logits rather than from
  probabilities? \teachesat{22--23}
  \answerto{Because the probability is the intermediate that overflows,
  underflows or rounds to exactly $0$ or $1$; the composed operation never
  forms it and has no such range problem.}
\end{quiz}

\section{Correct in exact arithmetic}
\label{sec:P02-correct}
\index{troubleshooting!an algorithm that is correct and useless}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:P01} established what the arithmetic is: a finite set of
numbers, spaced in proportion to their size, with rounding after every
operation. This program asks the question that follows, and it is an
engineering question rather than a mathematical one. \textbf{Given that, which
algorithms survive it?}

Start with one that does not, and it is one you have written.

Five latency readings, in microseconds: $\val{p02.var.offset}$, and the four
consecutive microseconds after it. What is their variance?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p02.var.true}$}
Four values at distances $-2, -1, 1, 2$ from the mean of
$\val{p02.var.offset} + 2$, one at zero, squares summing to $10$, divided by
five.

Now the computation. Every textbook offers a one-pass formula, and it is
genuinely attractive: it needs a single sweep and two running totals rather
than two sweeps.
\[ \Var(x) = \Ex[x^{2}] - \left(\Ex[x]\right)^{2} \]
The algebra is beyond reproach \dash{} expand $(x - \mu)^2$ and it falls out in
two lines. Predict what it returns for these five readings in \code{float32}.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p02.var.naive}$}
\transcript{p02-variance}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{A variance is a mean of squares. It cannot be negative, and this one
is $\val{p02.var.naive}$.}

Nothing in the algebra went wrong. The two quantities being subtracted are
\[ \Ex[x^{2}] = \val{p02.var.ex2}, \qquad
   \left(\Ex[x]\right)^{2} = \val{p02.var.exsq} \]
and they agree to $\val{p02.var.shared}$ significant figures, where
\code{float32} carries about seven. So in that format the two are stored as
numbers whose leading digits are identical, the subtraction cancels every
digit they share, and what is handed back is made of what happened to be left
underneath \dash{} which is the rounding, not the variance.

The result is exactly one gap at that magnitude \dash{} the \code{float32}
gap near $\val{p02.var.ex2}$ is $\val{p02.var.gap}$ \dash{} which is why it is
$\val{p02.var.naive}$ and not some ragged number. It is the smallest non-zero
answer the subtraction could have given. It is not noise; it is the spacing.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The mechanism has a name and it is worth having, because you will recognise it
by shape long before you can prove it: \textbf{catastrophic cancellation}.

Subtract two numbers that agree in their leading significant digits and the
digits they agree on cancel to nothing. That is not a loss of accuracy in the
subtraction \dash{} the subtraction is exact, and is often the only exact step
in the whole calculation. The loss happened earlier, when each operand was
rounded, and the subtraction merely removed everything else and left it
exposed.

\medskip
\noindent
The rule to carry: \textbf{look for a subtraction of two nearly equal
quantities, and ask where they came from.}
\end{fr}


\begin{fr}
Two numbers agree to $\val{p02.var.shared}$ significant figures and are
subtracted in \code{float32}, which carries about seven. How many significant
figures does the answer have?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{None. Not \enquote{fewer} \dash{} none.}
Every digit \code{float32} was holding is a digit the two operands shared, so
every one of them cancels. Whatever comes back is built from bits that were
already wrong.

That is the \emph{catastrophic} in the name, and it is a genuine cliff rather than
a gradient: agree to six figures in \code{float32} and you keep about one;
agree to eight and you keep none at all. The quantity that matters is
\emph{how many figures the operands share against how many the format holds},
and it is worth computing before trusting any difference.
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{p02-cancellation}{What a subtraction removes, and what it leaves
behind.}{where the rounding shows}

\begin{fr}
The fix is not more precision. Run the same formula in \code{float64} and it
survives these readings comfortably \dash{} and fails on readings a few orders
of magnitude larger, because nothing structural has changed. \textbf{A wider
format moves the cliff; it does not remove it.}

The fix is a different algorithm, and for a variance there is a good one.
Welford's method keeps a running mean and a running sum of squared deviations
\emph{from that mean}, updating both as each value arrives:
\begin{align*}
  \mu_n &= \mu_{n-1} + \frac{x_n - \mu_{n-1}}{n} \\
  M_n &= M_{n-1} + (x_n - \mu_{n-1})(x_n - \mu_n)
\end{align*}
It is still one pass and two running totals. Look at what it never forms, and
say why that is the whole of the difference.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
It never forms $\Ex[x^{2}]$, so there is never a large quantity to subtract a
large quantity from.
\end{ansblock}

Every subtraction in Welford's is $x_n - \mu$, a value minus a mean of values
like it \dash{} which is a difference of the size of the spread rather than of
the size of the data. The catastrophic subtraction has been designed out
rather than made more accurate, and on the readings above it returns
$\val{p02.var.welford}$ against a true $\val{p02.var.true}$.

\begin{warning}
\textbf{This is the shape of every fix in this program.} None of them makes an
operation more accurate. Each one removes the operation that was destroying the
information, and replaces it with arithmetic on quantities of comparable size.
If a proposed remedy does not do that, it is a wider format in disguise and it
will fail later rather than not at all.
\end{warning}
\end{fr}

\section{What a subtraction costs, and what it compounds to}
\label{sec:P02-cost}
\index{floating point!relative error}

\begin{fr}
The cost is worth stating as a quantity rather than as a warning, because you
can then estimate it.

Suppose $a$ and $b$ each carry a relative error of about $\eps$ \dash{} which
under Program~\ref{prog:P01}'s rules they do, $\eps$ being the format's
epsilon. Their absolute errors are then about $\eps|a|$ and $\eps|b|$, and the
difference carries the sum of those. As a \emph{relative} error on the answer:
\[ \frac{\eps\left(|a| + |b|\right)}{|a - b|} \]
Read the denominator. When $a$ and $b$ are far apart it is comparable to the
numerator and nothing much happens. When they are close it goes to zero while
the numerator does not.

One assumption of that derivation is worth naming, because it makes the
expression a floor rather than an estimate. It takes each operand's relative
error to be about $\eps$, and $\eps$ is what Program~\ref{prog:P01} measured:
the cost of rounding one number into the format. Nothing in it covers error a
value arrives with. If $a$ and $b$ are themselves the results of long
computations they arrive carrying more than that, and the same factor
multiplies whatever they arrive with.

So write the amplification factor down, and then put a size on it for the
variance above.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\frac{|a| + |b|}{|a - b|}$, and for the variance above it is of the
order of $10^{\val{p02.var.shared}}$.}

That ratio is worth recognising because it is not confined to subtraction.

Program~\ref{prog:F06} asked, in a further problem, what happens when one
equation of a system is multiplied through by $10^{6}$: the solution is
unchanged, the pair of lines is unchanged, and a solver's behaviour is not.
The reason is the same quantity. A problem in which a small relative change to
the input forces a large relative change to the output is \emph{badly
conditioned}, and conditioning is a property of the problem rather than of your
code \dash{} no amount of careful implementation removes it.

\begin{rigourbox}
\textbf{The general form of that number is the condition number}, and for a
system of equations it is a statement about a matrix, which this book has not
yet defined. Program~\ref{prog:P10} gives it properly. What belongs here is the
scalar version and the habit it buys: before trusting a computed difference,
ask what its inputs' relative errors were and what the operation did to them.
\end{rigourbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:F01} left this program a specific debt, and warned that the
answer would be worse than expected.

A layer of a network does arithmetic, so each layer's output carries a
relative error of at least $\eps$. Ninety-six layers apply that
$\val{p02.layers}$ times. If every layer's error pushed the same way, the
worst case would be $(1 + \eps)^{\val{p02.layers}} - 1$.

Work it out for \code{fp16}, whose epsilon Program~\ref{prog:P01} gives as
about $10^{-3}$, and give the answer as a percentage.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p02.grow.fp16}$\%}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
 & worst case over $\val{p02.layers}$ layers & errors of random sign \\
\midrule
\code{fp64} & $\val{p02.grow.fp64}$\% & $\val{p02.walk.fp64}$\% \\
\code{fp32} & $\val{p02.grow.fp32}$\% & $\val{p02.walk.fp32}$\% \\
\code{fp16} & $\val{p02.grow.fp16}$\% & $\val{p02.walk.fp16}$\% \\
\code{bf16} & $\val{p02.grow.bf16}$\% & $\val{p02.walk.bf16}$\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{warning}
\textbf{A bound is not a prediction, and quoting the first column as though it
were is the mistake this frame exists to prevent.}

The worst case assumes every rounding pushes the same way, which is what
Program~\ref{prog:F01}'s compounding argument describes. Roundings of random
sign do not accumulate like the count; they accumulate like its square root,
which is the second column \dash{} a factor of about $\val{p02.grow.ratio}$
smaller for \code{fp16}.

Neither column is what your network does. The first is an upper bound you
cannot exceed and the second is an estimate that assumes independence you have
not checked. What both establish is the shape: \textbf{depth multiplies your
format's epsilon by something between the square root of the depth and the
depth itself}, and in sixteen bits that is a number you have to care about.
\end{warning}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
Which sets up the rest of this program.

There is no principle that makes floating-point code safe. There is a small
catalogue of situations that destroy information, and for each one a known
alternative that does not. You have met the first: a subtraction of nearly
equal quantities, fixed by not forming them.

The remaining three are the ones you will meet in a training loop:

\begin{itemize}
\item an exponential of something large, which leaves the format entirely;
\item a probability formed as an intermediate value, which is the same problem
  wearing a name you trust;
\item a very long sum, where the small terms are individually below the
  threshold Program~\ref{prog:P01} established.
\end{itemize}
\end{fr}

\section{The exponential, and its two cliffs}
\label{sec:P02-cliffs}
\index{floating point!overflow and underflow}
\index{softmax!why the maximum is subtracted}

\begin{fr}
An exponential is the operation most likely to take a number out of its
format, because it turns an addition of scores into a multiplication of
magnitudes.

Program~\ref{prog:P01} computed \code{fp16}'s ceiling from its exponent budget.
The score at which $e^{z}$ passes that ceiling is therefore not a new fact
\dash{} it is the logarithm of a number you already have.

Give it, to one decimal place, and then give the smallest whole score that
overflows.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p02.over.fp16}$, so a score of $\val{p02.over.fp16.int}$ overflows.}
\transcript{p02-cliff}

\textbf{That is not an exotic number.} It is a score, on the scale scores are
naturally on, and keeping attention scores small enough to be safe is the
entire job of the $1/\sqrt{d_k}$ factor that Program~\ref{prog:P25} derives.
The failure is not a rare event at the edge of the format's range; it is a
value the architecture has a correction built in to avoid, and what it produces
is \code{inf} and then \code{nan} rather than an error anybody would call an
error.

And it fails as a step rather than as a slope, which is the opposite of what
the previous section showed you. A cancellation degrades by degrees: the
amplification factor grows as the operands close on each other, so you lose
digits, then more digits, before you lose all of them. An exponential below the
threshold is an ordinary number carrying the format's own precision, and one
unit above it is
\code{inf}, with nothing in between. A test that sweeps a range and checks the
error therefore passes cleanly right up to the last safe score and says nothing
whatever about the next one.

The next frame tabulates that score for all four formats. Before it does: two
of the four overflow at the same score. Say which two, and say why.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{\code{bf16} and \code{fp32}, because they share an exponent budget and
the range of the exponential is set by that budget alone.}

Both cliffs, in all four formats \dash{} the score at which the exponential
leaves at the top, and the score at which it leaves at the bottom:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
 & overflows above & underflows below \\
\midrule
\code{fp64} & $\val{p02.over.fp64}$ & $\val{p02.under.fp64}$ \\
\code{fp32} & $\val{p02.over.fp32}$ & $\val{p02.under.fp32}$ \\
\code{fp16} & $\val{p02.over.fp16}$ & $\val{p02.under.fp16}$ \\
\code{bf16} & $\val{p02.over.bf16}$ & $\val{p02.under.bf16}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

That is Program~\ref{prog:P01}'s point restated, and the table says the rest
of it too: \textbf{the significand does not appear anywhere in it.} Neither
column moves when the significand does, at either end.
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{p02-two-cliffs}{The two ways a number leaves its format, and what
sets the distance between them.}{the two cliffs}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:F07} has already settled what to do about the top cliff, and
it settled it as algebra rather than as advice: subtracting a constant from
every score before exponentiating leaves a softmax unchanged, because the
constant's exponential factors out of the numerator and the denominator alike.
Its warning box puts it plainly \dash{} \textbf{it is an identity, and the
version that does not do it is the one that is wrong.}

Program~\ref{prog:F03} took the same step for a pair of logarithms, factoring
the larger one out of $\ln(e^{u} + e^{v})$.

Both left the same question open, and it is the one worth a section. Written
generally, the identity is
\[ \ln \sum_i e^{z_i} = c + \ln \sum_i e^{z_i - c} \]
and \emph{it holds for every $c$}. Nothing above forces a choice. So why is the
maximum the one every library uses?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{ansblock}
Because it is the only choice that bounds every remaining exponent at or below
zero, so it is the only one guaranteed not to overflow \dash{} for every input,
rather than for this one.
\end{ansblock}

With $c = \max_i z_i$, every $z_i - c$ is at most $0$, so every $e^{z_i - c}$
is at most $1$ and no term can pass any format's ceiling. The sum of at most
$n$ terms each at most $1$ is at most $n$, which no format has trouble with
either.

Take any other $c$ and the largest term formed is $e^{\max_i z_i - c}$, which
is above $1$ and grows exponentially as $c$ falls. \textbf{Whether it fits is
then a property of your data.}
\end{fr}

\mermaidfig{p02-pivot}{Any constant may be taken outside; what changes is the
largest term that has to be formed.}{choosing the pivot}

\begin{fr}
That last sentence is worth a measurement, because it explains why the choice
is so easy to get away with.

Take a row of $\val{p02.pivot.total}$ scores and pivot on each of them in turn,
in \code{fp16}. How many of the $\val{p02.pivot.total}$ choices keep every term
inside the format?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p02.pivot.safe}$ of them.}
\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Most wrong choices work.} \code{fp16} tolerates a shortfall of up to
$\val{p02.over.fp16}$ between the largest score and the pivot, and on an
ordinary row most entries are within that. So a naive implementation that
takes the first score, or the last, or no pivot at all on a row whose scores
happen to be small, runs correctly \dash{} through a test suite, through a
review, and into production.

The worst pivot in this row has to form a term of $\val{p02.pivot.worst}$,
which is not close. But it is only the worst \emph{on this row}: change the
data and the boundary moves, because it was always a fact about the data.

\textbf{The maximum is the only choice that is not.} That is what
\enquote{numerically stable} means as a technical term \dash{} not \emph{more accurate}, but \emph{safe for every input
rather than for the ones you tried}.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The bottom cliff needs one frame, because the fix for the top one handles it
by accident.

After subtracting the maximum, the smallest terms are $e^{z_i - \max}$ for the
scores furthest below the top, and those underflow to zero once a score falls more than
$\val{p02.drop.fp16}$ below the maximum in \code{fp16}. That is not a failure:
such a term was going to contribute less than $\val{p02.drop.fp16.frac}$ of the
largest one anyway, and dropping it changes nothing you can measure.

\textbf{Underflow in a sum is usually harmless and overflow never is}, which is
worth knowing because it tells you which direction to protect. A term that
becomes zero is a term you did not need. A term that becomes \code{inf} takes
the sum, the logarithm, the loss and every gradient with it.

The whole operation \dash{} subtract the maximum, exponentiate, sum, take the
logarithm, add the maximum back \dash{} is $\logsumexp$, and on the row above
it gives $\val{p02.lse.exact}$.
\end{fr}

\section{Never form a probability}
\label{sec:P02-logits}
\index{softmax!computed from logits}
\index{loss!cross-entropy from logits}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:F03} promised that this program would show how a per-token
log-probability is computed \emph{without ever forming a probability}, and the
phrasing was deliberate. It is the same instruction as the last section, one
level up.

A probability is a number between $0$ and $1$. Program~\ref{prog:P01} says what
that range costs: below $\val{p02.under.fp16}$ in the exponent an \code{fp16}
value is zero, and a probability near $1$ has its interesting digits in exactly
the place a float has fewest of them, because the gap at $1$ is the largest gap
below $1$.

So a probability is a bad intermediate at both ends, and a log-probability is a
good one at both. \textbf{The rule is that the probability should not exist.}

For a softmax the two steps compose into one operation, and the algebra that
does it is three lines. Write $\ln \softmax(z)_j$ out and simplify it.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$z_j - \logsumexp(z)$: no exponential of a raw score, no division and no
probability anywhere in it.}

\[ \ln \softmax(z)_j = \ln \frac{e^{z_j}}{\sum_k e^{z_k}}
   = z_j - \ln \sum_k e^{z_k} = z_j - \logsumexp(z) \]
A log-softmax is a
subtraction of the previous section's quantity from the score, and the
subtraction is between numbers of the same size, which is what
\S\ref{sec:P02-correct} said to look for.

That is what a library means by a function taking \emph{logits} rather than
probabilities, and it is why passing it probabilities \dash{} by helpfully
applying a softmax first \dash{} is a defect rather than a redundancy.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The binary case has the same shape and its own name, and
Program~\ref{prog:F05} pointed at it: $\ln \sigma(x)$, the log-sigmoid, which
appears in every binary cross-entropy whose input is a logit.

Program~\ref{prog:F05} said the composition is safe mathematically and unsafe
in floating point. Say at roughly what $x$ the composed form fails, and what it
returns when it does.
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Below about $\val{p02.logsig.under}$ it returns \code{-inf}.}
$\sigma(x)$ behaves like $e^{x}$ for large negative $x$, so it underflows to
exactly zero once $x$ passes $\val{p02.under.fp64}$ \dash{} the bottom cliff
from the last section, arriving under a different name. And $\ln 0$ is
\code{-inf}, which propagates into the loss and then into every
gradient.

Computed as one operation there is no difficulty at all:
\[ \ln \sigma(x) = -\ln\!\left(1 + e^{-x}\right) \]
which for negative $x$ is rearranged to $x - \ln(1 + e^{x})$ so that the
exponential's argument is never positive. At $x = \val{p02.logsig.under}$ it
returns $\val{p02.logsig.stable}$, which is the right answer and is barely
distinguishable from $x$ itself.

That was the bottom end. The top end fails too, and it is the half people
miss: a binary cross-entropy needs $\ln p$ and $\ln(1 - p)$, and
Program~\ref{prog:F07} measured where $\sigma$ rounds to exactly $\num{1.0}$.
Say what the loss does there.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$1 - p$ is exactly zero, so $\ln(1 - p)$ is \code{-inf} \dash{} on a
prediction that was confident and \emph{right}.}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{And that is the half people miss.}

Program~\ref{prog:F07} measured that $\sigma$ rounds to exactly
$\num{1.0}$ from about $\val{f07.sig.saturates}$ upwards; the first whole
number at which it does is $\val{p02.logsig.saturates}$. A binary cross-entropy
needs both $\ln p$ and $\ln(1 - p)$, and at that point $1 - p$ is exactly zero,
so $\ln(1 - p)$ is \code{-inf}.

A confident \emph{correct} prediction therefore produces the same
\code{-inf} as a confident wrong one, and the loss becomes
\code{nan} on an example the model got right. That is the failure that sends
people looking for a bug in their data.

\textbf{Both ends are removed by the same move}: keep the logit, and let the
loss function do the composition. The library ships one function precisely
because there is no safe way to write it as two.
\end{trapbox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{aibox}
The consequence in a training loop, stated as a rule you can check by reading
a diff.

A classifier's forward pass ends at the \emph{logits}, and nothing after that
point forms a probability until something outside the loss needs to display
one. The loss is handed logits and targets; the metric may be handed
probabilities, because a metric that is off by a rounding is a cosmetic fault
where a loss that is \code{nan} is a stopped run.

The tell in code is a softmax or a sigmoid immediately followed by a
logarithm, or by a loss whose name mentions logits. Both are the two-operation
form of something the library ships as one, and both work on the batches you
tried.
\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
One boundary, so that this section is not read as more than it is.

Nothing here says what a probability \emph{is}, why the logarithm of one is the
right thing to sum, or why cross-entropy is the loss rather than a convenient
choice. Program~\ref{prog:P26} derives the loss from maximum likelihood and
Program~\ref{prog:P30} gives cross-entropy its meaning in bits.

What belongs to this program is narrower and is a fact about arithmetic:
\textbf{of the two quantities, the logarithm is the one your machine can hold.}
That is true whatever the loss turns out to mean.
\end{fr}

\section{Adding a great many numbers}
\label{sec:P02-summation}
\index{floating point!summation order}

\begin{fr}
Program~\ref{prog:P01} established the threshold and handed the consequence
here by name. This is that section.

A contribution below half the gap at the running total moves it by exactly
nothing. So take $\val{p02.sum.n}$ values of $\val{p02.sum.small}$ and add them
one at a time to a \code{float32} total that starts at $1$. The exact answer is
$\val{p02.sum.exact}$.

What does the loop return?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p02.sum.naive32}$. Every single one of them was lost.}
Not most of them, and not to a rounding: the gap at $1$ in \code{float32} is
about $10^{-7}$, half of it is about $\val{p01.swamp.fp32}$, and
$\val{p02.sum.small}$ is below that. Each addition is a no-op that returns
successfully, a million times over.

\begin{warning}
\textbf{There is no error, no warning and no \code{nan}.} The loop runs, the
total is a perfectly ordinary number, and the answer is wrong by
$\val{p02.sum.err.naive32}$ \dash{} which is the whole of the quantity you were
summing. A gradient accumulation that does this reports a healthy loss and
learns nothing, and the symptom is a model that will not train rather than a
program that will not run.
\end{warning}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The catalogue has four entries and they are not equivalent. The first is free.

\textbf{Add the small values to each other before adding them to the large
one.} Sorting ascending does it: the million tiny values accumulate among
themselves, where they are of comparable size, and the total only meets the $1$
at the end, once it is large enough to survive the meeting.

That is Program~\ref{prog:P01}'s three-value non-associativity at scale, run
deliberately rather than by accident. What do you expect it to recover?
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{$\val{p02.sum.got.sorted}$\% of the contribution \dash{} nearly all of it,
and not all of it.}
The shortfall is $\val{p02.sum.err.sorted}$, and it is not a rounding: it is a
million roundings. Each addition into a growing total rounds, the errors mostly
share a sign, and over $\val{p02.sum.n}$ of them that drift is visible.

\textbf{Sorting fixes the catastrophe and leaves the drift}, which is the
honest description and is worth having, because sorting is the fix people reach
for and stop at.

The drift is there because each addition rounds and the rounding is thrown
away. What would you have to keep, alongside the total, to stop throwing it
away?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{The part of the last addition that was lost: one extra variable, added
back into the next term before it is used.}

The second entry attacks the drift directly. \emph{Compensated summation}
\dash{} Kahan's \dash{} keeps exactly that variable:
\begin{align*}
  y &= x_i - c \\
  t &= s + y \\
  c &= (t - s) - y \\
  s &= t
\end{align*}
Read the third line. $t - s$ is what the addition \emph{actually} moved the
total by, and $y$ is what it was asked to move it by; the difference is exactly
what was rounded away, and it is recovered by a subtraction of two nearly equal
numbers.

\textbf{Cancellation is the mechanism, used deliberately.} The same operation
that destroyed the variance in \S\ref{sec:P02-correct} is here the only one
that can see the lost bits at all.

The third entry needs no extra variable at all. Add in a balanced tree rather
than in a line: pair the values, add the pairs, pair those, and so on. The
number of additions does not change. Say what does, and by how much, for a
million values.
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{The \emph{depth}. Each value now reaches the total through about twenty
additions rather than through a million.}

\textbf{Add in a balanced tree} rather than a line, and the drift has twenty
chances to accumulate along any path instead of a million.

Pairwise summation is what an array library's reduction does, which is why a
naive Python loop over an array is often \emph{less} accurate than the library
call it replaced, as well as slower. Which library and which version is a fact
about a release rather than about the mathematics, so this book does not name
one.

The fourth needs no cleverness at all: \textbf{accumulate in a wider format
than the values.} Sixteen-bit values summed into a \code{float32} accumulator,
or \code{float32} values into \code{float64}. It costs a little memory and no
thought, and it is what mixed-precision training does.

Four fixes, and the next frame scores all of them on the same million values.
Before it does: which of the four do you expect to come out worst?
\dotline
\nextframe
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\ans{Sorting \dash{} the cheapest of the four and the one most often described
as sufficient.}

All five, on the same million values:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
 & total & of the contribution \\
\midrule
naive \code{float32} & $\val{p02.sum.naive32}$ & $\val{p02.sum.got.naive32}$\% \\
smallest first & $\val{p02.sum.sorted}$ & $\val{p02.sum.got.sorted}$\% \\
compensated & $\val{p02.sum.kahan}$ & $\val{p02.sum.got.kahan}$\% \\
pairwise & $\val{p02.sum.pairwise}$ & $\val{p02.sum.got.pairwise}$\% \\
wider accumulator & $\val{p02.sum.wide}$ & $\val{p02.sum.got.wide}$\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Exact is $\val{p02.sum.exact}$, computed in integers so that the thing every
row is scored against is not itself a floating-point result.

Two things in that table are worth more than the ranking. The first is the gap
between row one and every other row, which is the difference between an answer
and no answer. The second is the size of the shortfall you predicted:
\textbf{sorting is an order of magnitude worse than the other three}, so the
cheapest fix is not merely the weakest of the four, it is not in the same
class as the other three.
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
\begin{aibox}
Program~\ref{prog:F02} left a question here: what a layer normalisation's
$\eps$ is protecting against, and how much of it a given format can absorb.

It is protecting against a division by a standard deviation that has come out
as zero \dash{} which happens when a feature is constant across the batch, and
also when it is nearly constant and the variance was computed by the formula
this program opened with. The $\eps$ makes the denominator positive whatever
the numerator did.

How much a format absorbs is now a number you can produce: $\eps$ has to be
larger than the format's own noise floor at the scale of the variance, so an
$\eps$ of $10^{-8}$ does nothing at all in \code{fp16}, whose epsilon is around
$10^{-3}$. That is why mixed-precision recipes compute normalisation statistics
in \code{float32} even when everything around them is sixteen bits.
\end{aibox}
\end{fr}

\begin{fr}
The last frame, and it is a checklist rather than a conclusion.

Four situations destroy information, and each has a known alternative:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{a subtraction of nearly equal quantities} \dash{} find where they
  came from, and compute the difference directly if you can;
\item \textbf{an exponential of a raw score} \dash{} subtract the maximum,
  always, and take the logarithm rather than the ratio;
\item \textbf{a probability as an intermediate} \dash{} keep the logit and let
  the loss compose;
\item \textbf{a long sum} \dash{} pair it, compensate it, or widen the
  accumulator, and do not stop at sorting.
\end{itemize}

None of the four is about being careful. Each is a specific operation with a
specific replacement, and the skill is recognising the operation rather than
remembering the fix. Program~\ref{prog:P03} asks the next question about the
same arithmetic \dash{} not whether it is right, but what it costs.
\end{fr}

\begin{summarybox}
\sumitem{1--3}{\result{\textbf{An algorithm can be correct in exact arithmetic and
  useless in floating point.} The one-pass variance
  $\Ex[x^{2}] - (\Ex[x])^{2}$ on five readings at $\val{p02.var.offset}$
  returns $\val{p02.var.naive}$ in \code{float32}, against a true
  $\val{p02.var.true}$. The algebra is beyond reproach and the result is not a
  variance}.}
\sumitem{4--6}{\result{\textbf{Catastrophic cancellation} is the mechanism: the two
  quantities agree to $\val{p02.var.shared}$ significant figures where the
  format holds seven, so the subtraction cancels every digit they share and
  hands back the rounding. The subtraction is exact; the loss happened when the
  operands were rounded. Look for a difference of nearly equal quantities and
  ask where they came from}.}
\sumitem{7--8}{\result{\textbf{The fix is never more precision} \dash{} a wider format
  moves the cliff rather than removing it. Welford's method never forms
  $\Ex[x^{2}]$, so every subtraction is of the size of the spread rather than
  of the data, and it returns $\val{p02.var.welford}$. Every fix in this
  program has that shape: remove the destroying operation, do not make it more
  accurate}.}
\sumitem{9--10}{\result{The amplification is $\frac{|a| + |b|}{|a - b|}$, and it is a
  property of the \emph{problem}. Its general form is the condition number,
  which is Program~\ref{prog:P10}'s because it is a statement about a matrix}.}
\sumitem{11--13}{\result{Over $\val{p02.layers}$ layers \code{fp16}'s worst case is
  $\val{p02.grow.fp16}$\% and \code{bf16}'s is $\val{p02.grow.bf16}$\%
  \dash{} but \textbf{a bound is not a prediction}: errors of random sign grow
  like the square root of the depth, about $\val{p02.grow.ratio}$ times
  smaller. Depth multiplies epsilon by something between $\sqrt{n}$ and $n$}.}
\sumitem{14--16}{\result{\textbf{An exponential leaves a format at the logarithm of its
  ceiling}, which is $\val{p02.over.fp16}$ for \code{fp16} \dash{} so a score
  of $\val{p02.over.fp16.int}$ overflows, and that is an ordinary attention
  logit. \code{bf16} and \code{fp32} share the row at
  $\val{p02.over.fp32}$, because the range is the exponent budget alone}.}
\sumitem{17--20}{\result{$\ln\sum_i e^{z_i} = c + \ln\sum_i e^{z_i - c}$ holds for
  \emph{every} $c$, so the identity forces no choice. \textbf{The maximum is
  the only $c$ that bounds every exponent at or below zero}, hence the only one
  safe for every input. Measured: $\val{p02.pivot.safe}$ of
  $\val{p02.pivot.total}$ pivots happen to work on one ordinary row, which is
  why the wrong choice survives review \dash{} and the worst forms
  $\val{p02.pivot.worst}$}.}
\sumitem{21}{\result{\textbf{Underflow in a sum is usually harmless and overflow never
  is.} A term that becomes zero was contributing nothing; a term that becomes
  \code{inf} takes the loss and every gradient with it. The whole operation is
  $\logsumexp$}.}
\sumitem{22--23}{\result{\textbf{A probability is a bad intermediate at both ends} and
  a log-probability is a good one, so the probability should not exist.
  $\ln\softmax(z)_j = z_j - \logsumexp(z)$: no exponential of a raw score, no
  division, and a subtraction between numbers of the same size}.}
\sumitem{24--26}{\result{$\ln\sigma(x)$ computed in two steps returns
  \code{-inf} below about $\val{p02.logsig.under}$, where
  $\sigma$ has underflowed; as one operation it returns
  $\val{p02.logsig.stable}$. \textbf{The upper end fails too}: $\sigma$ is
  exactly $\num{1.0}$ from $\val{p02.logsig.saturates}$, so $\ln(1-p)$ is
  \code{-inf} and a confident \emph{correct} prediction produces
  a \code{nan}}.}
\sumitem{27--28}{\result{The forward pass ends at the logits and the loss composes.
  The tell in a diff is a softmax or sigmoid immediately followed by a
  logarithm. What a probability \emph{is}, and why cross-entropy is the loss,
  belong to Programs~\ref{prog:P26} and~\ref{prog:P30}; what belongs here is
  that of the two quantities the logarithm is the one the machine can hold}.}
\sumitem{29--30}{\result{$\val{p02.sum.n}$ values of $\val{p02.sum.small}$ added to a
  \code{float32} total of $1$ give $\val{p02.sum.naive32}$ \dash{}
  \textbf{every one lost}, with no error and no warning, wrong by the whole of
  the quantity being summed}.}
\sumitem{31--35}{\result{Four fixes, and they are not equivalent: smallest first
  recovers $\val{p02.sum.got.sorted}$\%, compensated summation and a pairwise
  tree $\val{p02.sum.got.kahan}$\%, a wider accumulator
  $\val{p02.sum.got.wide}$\%. \textbf{Sorting is the cheapest and the weakest},
  and it is the one most often called sufficient. Kahan's third line recovers
  the lost bits by a cancellation used deliberately}.}
\sumitem{36--37}{\result{A layer norm's $\eps$ protects a division by a standard
  deviation that came out zero, and it must exceed the format's noise floor at
  the scale of the variance \dash{} which is why normalisation statistics are
  computed in \code{float32} inside a sixteen-bit model. \textbf{The skill is
  recognising the operation, not remembering the fix.}}}
\end{summarybox}

\begin{testexercises}
\item Say what catastrophic cancellation is, and where in the calculation the
  information was actually lost.
  \answerto{Subtracting two numbers that agree in their leading significant
  digits, so those digits cancel and what remains is built from the low-order
  bits. The subtraction itself is exact; the loss happened when each operand
  was rounded.}
\item The one-pass variance returned $\val{p02.var.naive}$ where the true
  answer is $\val{p02.var.true}$. Say why running it in \code{float64} is not
  a fix.
  \answerto{It moves the offset at which the formula fails and changes nothing
  structural. The subtraction of two nearly equal large quantities is still
  there, so the algorithm still destroys the information for large enough
  data.}
\item State the amplification factor when $a - b$ is computed from operands
  each carrying relative error $\eps$, and say what it depends on.
  \answerto{$\frac{|a| + |b|}{|a - b|}$, times $\eps$. It depends on how close
  the operands are, which is a property of the problem rather than of the
  code.}
\item Give the score at which an exponential overflows \code{fp16} and the
  score at which it overflows \code{bf16}, and say why they differ so much.
  \answerto{$\val{p02.over.fp16}$ and $\val{p02.over.bf16}$. The range of an
  exponential is the logarithm of the format's ceiling, which is set by the
  exponent budget alone \dash{} five bits against eight.}
\item $\ln \sum_i e^{z_i} = c + \ln \sum_i e^{z_i - c}$. Say what constrains
  $c$ mathematically and what constrains it in practice.
  \answerto{Mathematically nothing \dash{} the identity holds for every $c$.
  In practice only $c = \max_i z_i$ bounds every remaining exponent at or below
  zero, so only it is safe for every input.}
\item A colleague reports that their softmax pivots on the first score rather
  than the maximum and has never failed. Say what to conclude.
  \answerto{That their rows have never been spread by more than about
  $\val{p02.over.fp16}$ in \code{fp16}. It is a fact about the data they have
  seen, not about the code, and the boundary moves the moment the data does.}
\item Say why passing probabilities to a loss function that expects logits is a
  defect rather than an equivalent route.
  \answerto{The probability is the intermediate with the range problem: it
  overflows nothing but underflows to zero and rounds to exactly one. The
  logit-taking form never constructs it and composes the two steps into
  arithmetic on numbers of comparable size.}
\item A binary classifier produces a \code{nan} loss on an example it is
  confident about and correct on. Give the mechanism.
  \answerto{$\sigma$ has rounded to exactly $\num{1.0}$, which happens from
  $\val{p02.logsig.saturates}$ upwards, so $1 - p$ is exactly zero and
  $\ln(1-p)$ is \code{-inf}. Computing the loss from the logit
  removes it.}
\item $\val{p02.sum.n}$ values of $\val{p02.sum.small}$ are added to a
  \code{float32} total of $1$. Give the result and say what warning you get.
  \answerto{$\val{p02.sum.naive32}$, and none. Each value is below half the gap
  at $1$, so each addition succeeds and moves nothing.}
\item Rank the four summation fixes by what they recover, and say which one is
  most often over-sold.
  \answerto{Wider accumulator, then compensated and pairwise together, then
  smallest-first at $\val{p02.sum.got.sorted}$\%. Sorting is the one described
  as sufficient and it is an order of magnitude worse than the other three.}
\end{testexercises}

\begin{furtherproblems}
\item Derive $\Var(x) = \Ex[x^{2}] - (\Ex[x])^{2}$ from the definition, and say
  at which line the numerical trouble is introduced.
  \answerto{Expand $\Ex[(x - \mu)^{2}] = \Ex[x^{2}] - 2\mu\Ex[x] + \mu^{2}$ and
  use $\Ex[x] = \mu$. The trouble arrives with the expansion itself: it
  replaces a sum of small squared deviations with a difference of two large
  quantities, and those are equal in exact arithmetic and not on a machine.}
\item Show that Welford's update keeps $M_n = \sum_{i \le n} (x_i - \mu_n)^2$,
  and say why the two different means in its second line are not a typo.
  \answerto{The identity to check is
  $M_n - M_{n-1} = (x_n - \mu_{n-1})(x_n - \mu_n)$, which follows from
  $\mu_n - \mu_{n-1} = (x_n - \mu_{n-1})/n$. The old mean and the new one
  appear because the correction has to account for every earlier deviation
  shifting when the mean moves, and their product does exactly that.}
\item A row of scores is spread over a range of $200$. Say what happens under a
  softmax that subtracts the maximum, in \code{fp16}.
  \answerto{The top terms are fine and everything more than
  $\val{p02.drop.fp16}$ below the maximum underflows to zero, so those tokens
  receive probability exactly zero. They were receiving less than
  $\val{p02.drop.fp16.frac}$ of the largest term, so nothing measurable is lost
  \dash{} but their gradients are exactly zero rather than very small.}
\item Explain why $\logsumexp$ of a row of $n$ equal scores is the score plus
  $\ln n$, and use it to check an implementation.
  \answerto{Subtracting the maximum leaves $n$ terms each equal to $1$, whose
  sum is $n$; adding the maximum back gives $z + \ln n$. It is the cheapest
  possible test and it catches a pivot error immediately, because a wrong pivot
  still gives the right answer here.}
\item A cross-entropy is computed as \code{-log(softmax(z)[target])}. Rewrite
  it in the stable form and say how many exponentials each version computes.
  \answerto{$\logsumexp(z) - z_{\text{target}}$. The naive version exponentiates
  every score and then takes a logarithm of a ratio; the stable version
  exponentiates every shifted score once inside $\logsumexp$ and then does one
  subtraction. The count of exponentials is the same and the range problem is
  not.}
\item Kahan's compensation relies on $(t - s) - y$ recovering the rounding.
  Say what property of the arithmetic makes that work, and when it fails.
  \answerto{For floating-point addition the rounding error of $s + y$ is itself
  exactly representable when $|s| \ge |y|$, so the subtraction recovers it with
  no error of its own. It fails when the compiler is allowed to re-associate
  the expression, which is why the algorithm is often written with barriers.}
\item A million \code{fp16} gradients are summed into an \code{fp16}
  accumulator. Estimate what fraction survives, and say what one change fixes
  most of it.
  \answerto{Anything below about $\val{p01.swamp.fp16}$ of the running total
  contributes nothing, so once the total has grown the later terms are lost
  wholesale. Accumulating in \code{float32} fixes almost all of it for the cost
  of the accumulator, which is why it is the standard recipe.}
\item An $\eps$ of $10^{-8}$ is added inside a square root in a layer
  normalisation running in \code{fp16}. Say what it achieves.
  \answerto{Nothing. \code{fp16}'s epsilon is around $10^{-3}$, so at any
  variance the format can represent, adding $10^{-8}$ changes no bit. The
  $\eps$ has to be scaled to the format, or the statistics computed in a wider
  one.}
\end{furtherproblems}
